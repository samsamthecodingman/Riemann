# 01 — Abstraction levels, summarisation, and zoomable text

## TL;DR
- RAPTOR and GraphRAG both validate the core Riemann idea (recursively cluster → summarise → repeat to build a tree), and both report large task gains from having multiple abstraction levels available, not just one summary. [RAPTOR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.18059), [GraphRAG](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/from-local-to-global-a-graph-rag-approach-to-query-focused-summarization/)
- Density has a sweet spot. Chain-of-Density shows people prefer summaries denser than a naive LLM default but not as dense as maximally-packed ones — roughly 3 densification passes matched human-written density best; readability drops sharply past that. [Adams et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04269)
- Abstractive summarisation is measurably more hallucination-prone than extractive, and the gap grows with how far a summary paraphrases the source — n-gram overlap with source predicts unfaithfulness. [Ladhak et al., via survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.14839), [extractive-vs-abstractive tradeoff paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13684)
- Reported hallucination rates for LLM summaries are not small: ~28.6% of single-document news summaries contain unfaithful content, and multi-document summarisation is worse (20–45%, with one benchmark reporting up to 75% hallucinated content in adversarial settings). This is a first-order design constraint, not an edge case. [From Single to Multi](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13961)
- There are real automatic faithfulness metrics Riemann can borrow cheaply: SummaC (NLI-based, sentence-segmented), QAGS (round-trip QA consistency, also gives interpretable "which token is wrong" output), and FActScore (atomic-fact decomposition + verification). [SummaC](https://aclanthology.org/2022.tacl-1.10/), [QAGS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04228), [FActScore](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251)
- Multi-level, expandable text is an old idea with a name: Ted Nelson's **stretchtext** (1967/1970) — literally "two throttles," one for position, one for expansion — is the direct ancestor of the zoom dial. Telescopic Text is a working, well-loved implementation of exactly this. [Stretchtext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stretchtext), [Telescopic Text history](https://medium.com/@thisisdare/a-short-history-of-telescopic-text-67b7128e31bb)
- Tiago Forte's Progressive Summarization gives empirical-ish layer retention numbers from practitioner use: ~50% of notes reach layer 2 (bold), ~80% of layer-2 notes reach layer 3 (highlight, i.e. ~20% of original), ~25% of layer-3 notes reach layer 4/executive summary (~5% of original). That's a real-world funnel shape Riemann's L0–L3 sizing should roughly mirror. [Forte Labs](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/)
- Reading-speed numbers used for the "1–2 minute read" target should be corrected: the widely-cited "~300 wpm" figure is stale. The real meta-analytic average silent non-fiction reading rate is **238 wpm** (Brysbaert 2019, 190 studies, ~18.5k participants), with a realistic adult range of 175–300 wpm and diminishing comprehension above ~400 wpm. [Brysbaert 2019 meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749596X19300786)
- AI2's Semantic Reader/Scim line of work is the closest existing system to what Riemann's L2 (section gists) wants to do — it highlights rhetorically-labelled spans (Goal/Method/Result) in situ rather than replacing the text, which is a different and arguably lower-risk pattern than generating new summary text. [Semantic Reader](https://www.semanticscholar.org/product/semantic-reader), [AI2 blog](https://allenai.org/blog/case-study-iterative-design-for-skimming-support-5563dbe0899e)
- Error propagation in recursive/hierarchical summarisation pipelines is a documented failure mode, not a hypothetical: "hierarchical pipelines, especially with recursive merging, are vulnerable to error amplification and hallucination unless intermediate summaries are grounded in input context" — this is exactly Riemann's map-reduce build path. [emergentmind hierarchical summarisation overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/hierarchical-summarization)

## Findings

### 1. Hierarchical / multi-granularity summarisation architectures

**RAPTOR (recursive tree summarisation).** RAPTOR (Sarthi et al., Stanford, ICLR 2024) recursively embeds, clusters, and summarises chunks of text bottom-up to build a tree with multiple levels of abstraction, then retrieves across levels at query time rather than committing to one granularity. On QuALITY (a benchmark requiring multi-step reasoning over long text), coupling RAPTOR with GPT-4 improved absolute accuracy by 20 points over standard retrieval. Evidence strength: **STRONG** (peer-reviewed, ICLR 2024, with released code and controlled ablations). Source: [arXiv:2401.18059](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.18059), [GitHub](https://github.com/parthsarthi03/raptor). Caveat: I could not extract exact chunk-size/cluster-size/tree-depth parameters — the PDF served as binary image data I couldn't parse; the abstract and secondary summaries confirm the architecture but not the numeric hyperparameters. Treat any specific numbers you see elsewhere about RAPTOR's chunk sizes as unverified by me.

**GraphRAG community summaries.** Microsoft's GraphRAG builds an entity knowledge graph from source documents, then runs hierarchical community detection (Leiden-style) to partition entities into nested communities from broad themes down to fine topics, and generates an LLM summary for each community at each level. At query time, "global search" runs a map-reduce over community summaries (partial answers → final reduce) — structurally identical to Riemann's planned map-reduce build path, just applied to retrieval instead of static tree construction. Evidence strength: **MODERATE-STRONG** (Microsoft Research publication + shipped OSS tool, but published by the tool's own creators). Sources: [MSR blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/graphrag-new-tool-for-complex-data-discovery-now-on-github/), [From Local to Global paper](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/from-local-to-global-a-graph-rag-approach-to-query-focused-summarization/), [GraphRAG docs](https://microsoft.github.io/graphrag/).

**Chain of Density (CoD).** Adams et al. (2023) show GPT-4 can be prompted to iteratively densify a fixed-length summary — starting entity-sparse (0.089 entities/token) and adding 1–3 missing salient entities per pass without growing the summary, reaching 0.167 entities/token by pass 5. In a human preference study (4 annotators, 500 summaries over 100 CNN/DailyMail articles), the *modal* preferred number of densification steps was 2, median 3, and 61% of preferred summaries used ≥3 steps — density around step 3 (~0.15 entities/token) most closely matched human-written summary density (0.151) and beat vanilla GPT-4 (0.122). Crucially, the paper explicitly notes a coherence/informativeness tradeoff: excessive density hurts readability even as it raises information coverage. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** (single study, one dataset/domain, small annotator pool, but a controlled human-preference design). Source: [arXiv:2309.04269](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04269) (fetched full text).

**Map-reduce vs. refine.** These are the two standard LLM long-document summarisation strategies (as implemented in LangChain and elsewhere). Map-reduce (summarise chunks in parallel, then reduce) is fast, cheap, and parallelisable but loses cross-chunk context — appropriate when chunks are relatively independent. Refine (sequentially update a running summary chunk-by-chunk) preserves narrative flow and cross-references better but is slower (no parallelism) and can drift or over-weight later chunks. Practitioner consensus (not a controlled academic study): use refine when chunk order/narrative matters (a story, a step-by-step argument), map-reduce when chunks are more independent (e.g., a reference document, a collection of loosely related sections). Evidence strength: **WEAK/PRELIMINARY** for the "refine is more accurate" claim (comes from one blog's RETA-benchmark claim I could not independently verify) — treat as practitioner folklore, not measured fact, until you check RETA yourself. Sources: [LangChain summarisation overview](https://medium.com/@abonia/summarization-with-langchain-b3d83c030889), [chain-types comparison](https://www.learnwithparam.com/blog/langchain-chain-types-rag-summarization-stuff-map-reduce).

**Controllable-length summarisation.** An active NLP research area (length embeddings in the decoder, length-control decoding algorithms over Directed Acyclic Transformers, EOS-token weighting) — the point relevant to Riemann is that *length control is nontrivial to do well*: naive truncation-to-length approaches are good at stopping at the right length but bad at choosing which content survives the length budget. This is a real engineering risk for Riemann's fixed word-count targets per level (≤25 words L0, ~230×n words L1, 40–80 words per L2 gist): a model instructed to hit those budgets may include the wrong content rather than genuinely compressing. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** (multiple peer-reviewed papers converge on this problem framing). Sources: [DAT length-control decoding](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.04535), [EOS-weighting](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05017).

**Query-focused and aspect-based summarisation.** QFS produces a summary tailored to a specific question; aspect-based summarisation produces a summary tailored to a broad topic/aspect rather than a specific query. A 2023 survey notes real users' needs are often closer to *aspects* ("what does this say about X theme") than to sharp *queries*. This matters for Riemann because the zoom-dial v1 is aspect/topic-agnostic (one canonical tree per document) — a query-focused or aspect-focused re-summary is a clearly-scoped v2 feature (e.g., "show me the L1 focused on the security implications section"), not something v1 needs to solve. Evidence strength: **MODERATE**. Sources: [QFS survey](https://thesai.org/Downloads/Volume14No6/Paper_88-Query_Focused_Multi_document_Summarization_Survey.pdf), [ChatGPT for query/aspect summarisation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.08081).

**Abstractive vs. extractive tradeoff.** This is the single most load-bearing tradeoff for Riemann's `Summariser` design. Extractive summarisation (select/copy source sentences) is inherently more faithful because it never paraphrases, but reads less coherently when concatenated and can't compress ideas across sentences. Abstractive summarisation reads better and compresses further but hallucinates more, and Ladhak et al.'s finding (via the cited survey) that *degree of abstraction correlates with degree of unfaithfulness*, measured by n-gram overlap with source, is a genuinely actionable signal: **Riemann could compute this overlap metric per generated node as a cheap, model-free faithfulness proxy**, independent of running a separate NLI/QA model. Evidence strength: **MODERATE-STRONG** (converging conclusion across multiple cited papers, though I read secondary summaries rather than the primary Ladhak et al. paper directly — flagging this). Sources: [Faithfulness-Abstractiveness tradeoff paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13684), [factual inconsistency survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.14839).

### 2. Faithfulness: hallucination, omission, attribution, metrics

**Hallucination rates are high and level-dependent.** Single-document news summarisation: ~28.6% of summaries contain unfaithful content. Multi-document summarisation: 20–45% average hallucination rate, with one adversarial benchmark showing up to 75% hallucinated content, and hallucinations "more likely to occur towards the end of summaries." Domain matters too: conversational summarisation is worse than news by roughly 20–30 percentage points. Evidence strength: **MODERATE-STRONG** for the general direction (multiple independent papers converge), but exact percentages vary a lot by model/dataset/year and should be treated as illustrative magnitude, not a number to hard-code. Sources: [From Single to Multi (NAACL Findings 2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.293/), [FaithBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.13210), [clinical-summarisation safety framework](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01670-7) (shows this generalises beyond news — a medical-text safety paper found comparable concerns, worth reading in full for the second wave since patient-safety framing maps well onto "don't let the assistant silently drop something important").

**Error compounding across levels is a named, documented risk for exactly Riemann's architecture.** Multi-step/hierarchical pipelines that recursively merge summaries are "vulnerable to error amplification and hallucination unless intermediate summaries are grounded in input context" — i.e., an error introduced at L2 (leaf → section gist) will silently propagate into L1 and L0 unless each higher level is built by re-grounding against original source spans rather than purely against the level below it. This directly argues for Riemann's `source_span` field being used at every summarisation step, not just stored for UI display. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** (established pattern across adjacent multi-agent/multi-hop literature, applied by inference to hierarchical summarisation specifically). Sources: [hierarchical summarisation overview](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/hierarchical-summarization), [hallucination cascade in multi-agent LLM systems](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.07937).

**Automatic faithfulness metrics Riemann could actually run:**
- **SummaC** (Laban, Schnabel, Bennett, Hearst; TACL 2022): re-purposes NLI models for document-level inconsistency detection by segmenting into sentence pairs and aggregating scores (SummaCConv), reaching 74.4% balanced accuracy on a 6-dataset benchmark — a meaningful but *not* solved-problem accuracy level. Cheap to run (no generation, just NLI inference), so it's a plausible per-node faithfulness score. **MODERATE-STRONG**. [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.tacl-1.10/).
- **QAGS**: generates questions from the summary, answers them against both summary and source, and scores consistency by answer agreement — it has the nice property of being *interpretable*: it flags which summary tokens are unsupported, which is close to what a UI would want to show a user as "this part isn't well-attested." **MODERATE**, older (ACL 2020), superseded in raw accuracy by newer methods but still the clearest interpretable-UI-relevant design. [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.450/).
- **FActScore**: decomposes long-form generation into atomic facts and verifies each against a source; in the original paper, ChatGPT-generated biographies scored only 58% factual by human judgment, with an automated estimator (retrieval + LLM judge) achieving <2% error vs. human scoring. This atomic-fact decomposition is a good fit for scoring an L1/L2 summary sentence-by-sentence and surfacing a per-sentence confidence indicator. **STRONG** (EMNLP 2023, widely adopted since, has an installable package). [arXiv:2305.14251](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251).

**Attribution / citation UI patterns.** Practitioner and research consensus converges on: (a) highlighting inside the source builds more trust than citation markers alone; (b) two dominant patterns are numbered-reference-to-source and gradient/heatmap highlighting of the source region that generated a claim; (c) split-pane (summary | source, synchronized scroll/highlight) is common for document-grounded QA tools. NotebookLM's "citation chips" (click a citation → jump to and highlight the exact source passage) is a shipped, widely-used instance of this pattern and maps almost directly onto Riemann's `source_span` field — this is very close to "build this feature" reference material. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** (converging practitioner + one framework paper, not a controlled study of which UI pattern most improves calibrated trust). Sources: [source-highlighting-in-PDFs writeup](https://sodevelopment.medium.com/building-trust-in-llm-answers-highlighting-source-texts-in-pdfs-0a9d26417353), [citation UI patterns](https://dev.to/experilearning/unlocking-advanced-rag-citations-and-attributions-59lk), [NotebookLM overview](https://www.digitalocean.com/resources/articles/what-is-notebooklm).

### 3. Human-facing multi-level text and semantic zoom (prior art)

**Stretchtext (Ted Nelson, 1967–1970).** The direct conceptual ancestor of Riemann's zoom dial. Nelson imagined a reader controlling text with two throttles: one moves position, the other expands/contracts local detail continuously rather than via discrete links — "instead of linking, it zooms in depending on the detail needed." Never achieved mass adoption on the Web (hyperlinks won instead), but has persisted as a research/art-world technique. Evidence strength: **historical/conceptual, not empirical** — cite as prior art, not as validated UX research. Sources: [Wikipedia: Stretchtext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stretchtext), [Stretchtext and LLMs (2025 blog revisiting the idea in light of LLM summarisation)](https://www.arj.no/2025/12/27/stretchtext-llm/) — worth reading in full for the second wave, since it appears to directly address "stretchtext + LLMs," i.e., someone else has already had close to Riemann's exact idea.

**Telescopic Text (Joe Davis, 2007/2008).** A shipped, well-known implementation of stretchtext: a three-word sentence ("I made tea") expands word-by-word into a paragraph-length narrative, demonstrating how much detail can nest inside an ordinary moment. Became popular enough to spin off into an authoring tool (telescopictext.org) still used by writing teachers. Directly relevant precedent for "click to expand a specific span in place" as an alternative interaction to a global dial. Evidence: **anecdotal popularity, not a controlled study**, but strong existence-proof that word-level nested expansion is legible and engaging to ordinary readers. Source: [A short history of telescopic text](https://medium.com/@thisisdare/a-short-history-of-telescopic-text-67b7128e31bb).

**Progressive Summarization (Tiago Forte).** A manual practitioner method (part of the Building a Second Brain system) with an explicit layered structure that is unusually close to Riemann's L0–L3: Layer 1 = full imported source; Layer 2 = bold the essential passages (~50% pass rate reported); Layer 3 = highlight the best of the bold (~80% of L2 survives, ≈20% of original); Layer 4 = an executive summary distilling further (~25% of L3 survives, ≈5% of original). This gives Riemann a plausible, battle-tested *retention funnel shape* for calibrating L2→L1→L0 compression ratios, even though it's self-reported practitioner data, not a controlled study. Evidence strength: **WEAK/PRELIMINARY as science, but useful as a design prior** (large amount of independent practitioner replication/anecdote over ~8 years, no controlled comprehension outcome measurement). Source: [Forte Labs: Progressive Summarization](https://fortelabs.com/blog/progressive-summarization-a-practical-technique-for-designing-discoverable-notes/).

**Inverted pyramid / BLUF.** Journalism's inverted pyramid (most important information first, descending order of importance, "readers can leave the story at any point and still understand it") and the military-derived BLUF convention are the same idea applied to linear (non-zoomable) text: front-load the conclusion so that *reading time* and *understanding achieved* are decoupled — you get partial understanding even if you stop early. This is directly relevant to what Riemann's L0 "gist" should contain: **a claim/conclusion, not a topic label** (see below). Evidence strength: **STRONG as a design heuristic backed by a century of journalism practice**, but not itself an RCT — treat the *underlying claim* ("front-loaded structure preserves understanding under time pressure") as plausible-and-widely-practiced rather than experimentally proven in this literature pass. Sources: [Inverted pyramid overview](https://thewordling.com/inverted-pyramid/), [BLUF overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication)).

**Semantic Reader / Scim (AI2).** The most rigorous, closest-to-production HCI system I found. Scim (UIST 2023-line research, integrated into the shipped Semantic Reader product covering 500k+ papers) highlights passages in a scientific paper by rhetorical role (Goal / Method / Result) rather than generating new summary text, letting the reader control highlight density and opacity. This is architecturally a *different* strategy than Riemann's generate-new-text-per-level approach — it's "annotate the original text with importance," closer to extractive/positional signalling than to abstraction. Worth flagging as an alternative or complementary mode: Riemann's L2 could optionally show extractive highlights over the *actual* L3 text (zero hallucination risk) as well as/instead of a generated gist. Evidence strength: **MODERATE-STRONG** (published HCI research + shipped, actively used product with case-study writeups), though I could not get past a paywall (403) on the full ACM DL paper — I'm relying on the AI2 blog writeups, not the primary paper text. Sources: [AI2 case study](https://allenai.org/blog/case-study-iterative-design-for-skimming-support-5563dbe0899e), [Semantic Reader product page](https://www.semanticscholar.org/product/semantic-reader), [GeekWire coverage](https://www.geekwire.com/2023/speed-read-this-new-ai-powered-tool-helps-researchers-skim-through-scientific-papers/).

**NotebookLM.** Ships exactly the "source-grounded, cited summary" pattern: it only answers from user-provided sources, gives clickable citation chips that jump to and highlight the source passage, and auto-generates summaries/outlines/FAQs/study guides at what is effectively multiple abstraction levels already. It's the single most directly comparable *shipped competitor* to what Riemann is building, and worth a hands-on teardown (open it, feed it a real document, see exactly what its summary levels look like and where its citations point) rather than just reading about it. Evidence strength: **product description, not research** — treat claims about its accuracy/hallucination rate as marketing until independently tested. Source: [DigitalOcean overview](https://www.digitalocean.com/resources/articles/what-is-notebooklm).

**Comprehension study for summaries of research articles.** Found one directly relevant paper (*"Comprehending Research Article in Minutes: A User Study of Reading Computer Generated Summary for Young Researchers"*) but it's paywalled (Springer, 403/redirect-to-login) and I could not read past the abstract-level search snippet. I did **not** open the full text, so I'm not reporting its findings as verified — flagging its existence for someone with institutional access to check in the second wave. [Springer listing](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-78321-1_9) (unverified, paywalled).

**A genuinely useful comprehension-time study I did open (via search summary, not full fetch):** a 12-participant study comparing raw text vs. structured summary (table/mind-map) vs. both, for query-answering tasks, found accuracy stayed high (>94%) across all three conditions, but that providing *both* raw text and structured summary together sometimes took **longer** than structured summary alone — attributed to users cross-referencing between the two. This is a direct caution for Riemann's anchored-scroll design: naively showing "the summary AND let them jump to source" might slow people down rather than speed them up if the switching cost isn't managed well. Evidence strength: **WEAK/PRELIMINARY** (n=12, single study, I only saw a secondary summary of it, not the primary source, so treat this as a lead to verify, not a settled finding) — flagging clearly.

### 4. Choosing level sizes, structure, and terminology

**Reading-time targets should use 238 wpm, not 300.** Brysbaert (2019), a meta-analysis of 190 studies / ~18,500 participants, found average adult silent non-fiction reading rate is 238 wpm (260 wpm for fiction), with a realistic range of 175–300 wpm, and that speeds above ~400 wpm come with declining comprehension. Riemann's plan currently cites "~230 words/min" for the L1 target, which is already close to correct — good instinct, and this meta-analysis is the citation to back it, replacing the commonly-repeated but outdated "300 wpm" folk number. Evidence strength: **STRONG**. Source: [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749596X19300786) (full text paywalled — I read the abstract/secondary coverage, not the full PDF, so exact per-genre and per-age breakdowns beyond the topline numbers are unverified by me).

**What an L0 gist should contain: claim, not topic.** Abstract-writing guidance (multiple university writing-center and methodology sources) converges on: an effective abstract identifies the paper's *claim/argument/thesis*, not merely its topic — "a good abstract turns a methods-heavy summary into a concise, claim-driven summary." Combined with the inverted-pyramid/BLUF finding above, this gives a concrete, actionable rule for Riemann's `build.py` L0 prompt: **the L0 gist prompt should explicitly instruct the model to state the document's central claim/conclusion/takeaway in one sentence, not to categorise its topic** ("this article argues X" rather than "this article is about X"). Evidence strength: **MODERATE** (consistent practitioner/pedagogical guidance across many writing-center sources, not an RCT, but very low-risk and high-plausibility to adopt directly). Sources: [Thesify guide](https://www.thesify.ai/blog/how-to-write-an-abstract-guide), [UMN guide](https://ugresearch.umn.edu/abstract-writing).

**Compression-ratio funnel from Progressive Summarization** (see above): full → bold (≈50%) → highlight (≈20% of original) → executive summary (≈5% of original). Riemann's planned targets are steeper at the top (L0 ≤25 words vs. a typical document being thousands of words, i.e. often <1% of original) and shallower in the middle (L2 at 40–80 words/section could be a much larger fraction of a short section). The mismatch isn't necessarily wrong, but the Forte funnel argues for checking whether Riemann's L1 (1–2 min read) is being asked to do too much compression in one jump from L2, versus adding an intermediate step. Evidence strength: **WEAK/PRELIMINARY** design prior, not measured — flagging as something to A/B once Riemann has real usage data, not something to trust blindly upfront.

**Fixed vs. adaptive depth per section.** I did not find strong direct research on this specific question (whether a zoomable summary tree should have a uniform number of levels for every section vs. adapting depth to section importance/density). This is a **gap** — see below.

**Terminology consistency across levels.** I did not find direct research on this either (e.g., does using different vocabulary for the same concept at L0 vs L3 hurt comprehension when a reader zooms between them?). This is directly analogous to controlled-vocabulary/consistency concerns in technical writing and structured abstracts, but I found no study measuring it specifically for zoomable/multi-level AI summaries. **Gap** — flagging for second wave.

### 5. Evaluation: does a level deliver the promised understanding in the promised time?

I found comprehension-time methodology only by adjacent example, not a definitive study protocol built for exactly this question:
- The 12-participant raw-vs-structured-summary study (above) used **task-based evaluation**: give people a question, measure time-to-correct-answer and accuracy, across presentation conditions. This is a reusable protocol shape for Riemann: pick a comprehension-check question per level, measure time-to-answer and accuracy at each zoom level, compare against the promised "1–2 minute" budget.
- FActScore's atomic-fact-decomposition method (see above) is also repurposable as a *comprehension* evaluation tool, not just a faithfulness tool: ask "which atomic facts from the source did the reader retain after reading only level N," which directly operationalises "did this level deliver the promised understanding."
- I found **no** existing study that evaluates a zoomable/multi-level AI summary system end-to-end against a stated time-box promise (e.g., "does the 1-minute read actually convey what a 1-minute reader needs in 1 minute"). This is close to Riemann's core success metric and appears to be a genuine research gap, not just something I failed to find — worth Riemann treating as a novel contribution/differentiator rather than assuming prior art has solved it.

## Design implications for Riemann

1. **Ground every level in source, not just the level below.** Build `build.py` so L1 and L0 are generated (or at least fact-checked) against the original leaf text/`source_span`s, not purely by summarising the summary-of-a-summary. This directly mitigates the documented error-amplification risk in recursive/hierarchical pipelines. Concretely: when doing the map-reduce path, pass the relevant leaf `source_span`s alongside the L2 gists into the L1 reduce step, not just the L2 text.
2. **Add a cheap faithfulness signal per node, even in v1.** SummaC-style NLI scoring or a simple n-gram-overlap-with-source score (Ladhak-style abstractiveness proxy) is computationally cheap and gives you something to threshold on (retry, flag, or fall back to extractive) without needing FActScore's heavier atomic-fact pipeline yet. Store it on the `Node` model (`faithfulness_score: float | None`) so the frontend can eventually render it.
3. **Make the L0 prompt explicitly claim-seeking, not topic-seeking.** In `summarise.py`'s L0 prompt: "state the single most important conclusion or takeaway of this document in ≤25 words, not what it is about." This is a one-line, low-risk change directly backed by abstract-writing and BLUF/inverted-pyramid guidance.
4. **Correct the reading-speed constant to 238 wpm (already close — the plan's "~230 wpm" is right; just cite Brysbaert 2019 in code comments so future-you doesn't "fix" it back to the wrong folk number of 300).**
5. **Wire `source_span` into the UI as a NotebookLM-style citation, not just internal bookkeeping.** Clicking/hovering an L1 sentence should highlight the corresponding L3 span (the split-highlight pattern is the strongest converging practitioner recommendation found). This turns the anchoring mechanism the plan already needs (keep the centred node stable across zoom) into a trust-building affordance for free — anchoring and attribution can share one implementation.
6. **Consider an extractive-highlight mode as a fallback or companion to generated gists**, modelled on Scim/Semantic Reader: highlight-in-place over the actual L3 text is zero-hallucination-risk and could be what L2 falls back to when the faithfulness score on a generated gist is low, rather than silently shipping a possibly-wrong abstractive gist.
7. **Treat map-reduce vs. refine as a real per-document choice, not a fixed strategy.** Narrative/sequential documents (a long article that builds an argument) plausibly want refine (order-sensitive) for L1 construction; reference-shaped documents (a spec, a Wikipedia article with independent sections) plausibly want map-reduce (the plan already defaults to map-reduce for large docs — consider triggering refine instead when chunk headings suggest narrative structure, e.g. sequential numbered sections or story-like prose).
8. **Build the "does this level deliver the promised understanding in the promised time" check into your own test suite**, not just word-count validation. Since no prior art directly measures this, Riemann's `tests/` could include a small task-based eval harness (a few comprehension-check questions per test document, checked against each level) even if v1 runs it manually rather than automatically.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Per-node faithfulness/abstractiveness score** (SummaC-style or simple n-gram overlap) — computed at build time, not from user behaviour, but drives what to *show* the user (e.g., a subtle confidence indicator).
- **Zoom-reversal rate**: how often a user zooms in then immediately back out at the same node (proxy for "the summary at this level didn't satisfy them" or conversely "gave them enough, they went back to browse").
- **Dwell time per level before zooming further**: short dwell at L1 before jumping to L3 might indicate the L1 gist under-delivered; can be compared against the level's own promised read-time to see if actual-time ≈ promised-time.
- **Anchor-jump distance**: when the anchored node maps to an ancestor/descendant on zoom, how far the viewport actually has to scroll — large jumps might indicate poor node correspondence between levels (a UX/tree-quality signal, not just a content signal).
- **Explicit low-friction feedback**: a single tap/key ("this wasn't enough" / "too much") logged per level per document, cheap enough to not add real friction, that could eventually calibrate personal word-budget preferences per user.
- **Click-through on source-span citations**: how often a user actually jumps from an L1/L2 sentence to its L3 source — a trust/verification signal, and also flags which generated sentences people don't trust by default.

## Contradictions and caveats

- The Chain-of-Density "sweet spot around step 3" finding is from **one paper, one dataset (CNN/DailyMail news), 4 annotators** — it may not generalise to the kind of long-form technical/personal content (emails, PDFs, articles) Riemann targets. Don't treat "density ≈ 0.15 entities/token" as a universal constant.
- Hallucination-rate statistics (28.6% single-doc, 20–45%+ multi-doc) vary enormously by model, dataset, and year, and none of the papers I opened used content resembling Riemann's actual input mix (emails, personal notes, mixed PDFs/URLs). These numbers establish "this is a real and large risk," not a number to design a specific threshold around.
- The Progressive Summarization layer-retention percentages are self-reported practitioner data from one person's public writing (Tiago Forte), not a controlled study — useful as a design prior, not as evidence.
- Most of the HCI prior art here (stretchtext, Telescopic Text, Semantic Reader) targets either literary/playful reading or scientific papers specifically — none of it was tested on the heterogeneous mixed-source, ADHD-user context Riemann actually targets. Transfer is plausible but unverified.
- I could not open several primary sources due to paywalls (Springer "Comprehending Research Article in Minutes," ACM DL Scim paper) or unreadable binary PDF responses (RAPTOR's own PDF, the human-LLM-collaboration factuality-scores paper) — I relied on secondary summaries/search snippets for those, which I've flagged inline above. Treat those specific claims as lower-confidence than the ones backed by full-text fetches.
- The "refine strategy is consistently more accurate than map-reduce" claim came from a single blog citing an unnamed "RETA" benchmark I could not independently verify — flagged as WEAK, don't cite it as settled.

## Gaps (for the fleet's second wave)

1. **Fixed vs. adaptive tree depth per section** — no research found on whether every section should get the same number of abstraction levels, or whether dense/important sections should get more levels than throwaway ones. Directly relevant to `chunk.py`/`build.py` design; worth a dedicated pass.
2. **Terminology/vocabulary consistency across abstraction levels** — no research found on whether shifting vocabulary between L0 and L3 (e.g., a plain-English L0 gist vs. jargon-heavy L3 original) helps or hurts comprehension when zooming. Relevant to whether Riemann's summariser should be told to preserve source terminology or is free to simplify it.
3. **The NotebookLM teardown** — someone on the fleet (or a follow-up pass) should actually *use* NotebookLM with a real long document and record exactly what its summary levels/citations look like in practice, since it's the closest shipped competitor and I only assessed it from marketing copy.
4. **Task-based comprehension evaluation protocol design** — since no existing work directly measures "did a summary level deliver the promised understanding in the promised time," this is worth a dedicated research/design pass to define Riemann's own eval harness (question sets per test document, scoring against FActScore-style atomic facts, time-boxed reading sessions) rather than assuming something usable already exists.
5. **The "Stretchtext and LLMs" 2025 blog post** ([link](https://www.arj.no/2025/12/27/stretchtext-llm/)) appears to directly address combining stretchtext with LLM summarisation — I found it but didn't have budget to fully mine it; worth a dedicated read, it may already contain design lessons directly applicable to Riemann.
6. **Medical/clinical LLM-summarisation safety framework** ([Nature npj Digital Medicine paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01670-7)) — found but not deeply read; clinical safety frameworks for "don't let a summary silently omit something critical" may transfer usefully to Riemann's omission (not just hallucination) risk, which this pass under-covered relative to hallucination.
7. **Omission rates specifically** (as opposed to hallucination/addition) — most of the faithfulness literature I found focuses on *added* false content; Riemann's zoom-dial use case arguably cares equally or more about *omitted* important content at higher abstraction levels (silently dropping something the user needed). This asymmetry deserves its own targeted search pass.
