# 02 — Graphic & information design to reduce cognitive overload

## TL;DR
- Sweller's 2019 "20 years later" update reframes germane load as a *redistribution* of capacity freed from extraneous load into intrinsic processing, not a third additive load — this matters for Riemann because "helping people learn the content" and "not wasting their attention" are the same lever, not two.
- Element interactivity (how many things must be held in working memory *simultaneously* to make sense of one thing) is the real mechanism behind both intrinsic load and the expertise-reversal effect; abstraction levels are, structurally, an element-interactivity dial.
- Mayer's extraneous-processing principles have real, replicated effect sizes: coherence (excluding seductive but irrelevant detail) and temporal/spatial contiguity are among the largest (g ≈ 0.7–1.3 in early meta-analyses; a 2024/2025 boundary-conditions meta-analysis puts pooled effect at g ≈ 0.37 with erosion over study-year, i.e. real but smaller and shrinking as baseline designs improve).
- Preattentive attributes (Ware: form/colour/position/motion; Healey: 16 cues incl. orientation, size, hue, luminance) let you signal structure in <250ms, before conscious attention — this is the toolbox for level/hierarchy cues in the zoom dial UI itself, not just content layout.
- Dyslexia-specific fonts (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie) have **no reliable benefit** across a 15-study/688-participant meta-analysis; increased letter/word spacing does help — a MYTH worth flagging since it's a common ADHD/neurodivergent-accessibility assumption people port over uncritically.
- NN/g's F-pattern is a *failure state*, not a target: it emerges from unstructured content; well-structured content (headings + short body) produces the more effective "layer-cake" scan pattern — direct design implication for L1/L2 rendering.
- Categorical colour use should cap around 5–7 distinguishable hues (steep dropoff 5→6); colour should never be the sole encoding channel (pair with position/shape/label) for colour-vision-deficiency safety.
- Animated transitions (Heer & Robertson 2007) aid object constancy — preserving the viewer's mental map across a state change — which is exactly what "the passage at centre stays anchored while you zoom" needs; but object-constancy value depends on paths being short, and reduced-motion users need it demoted, not removed (usually to a fast crossfade + instant position match).
- Information density is not simply bad: experts and frequent users perform *better* with denser, well-organised layouts; novices need sparser ones — this maps directly onto Riemann's declared "adaptive load scaling" principle, and cautions against a single "clean minimalist" default.
- Dark vs light mode: light-on-dark text reads measurably faster with lower cognitive workload; dark mode wins on subjective comfort/eye-strain, especially in low light and longer sessions — a real trade-off, not a solved question, and a candidate adaptive signal (time of day, session length).

## Findings

### Cognitive Load Theory updates

**Finding: CLT's germane load was reconceived (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 2019) from an additive third load to a redistribution mechanism — spare capacity freed by reducing extraneous load flows into intrinsic processing of the same material, rather than being a separate resource pool.**
Evidence strength: MODERATE (theoretical position paper backed by the field's cumulative experimental base, not itself a new empirical study).
Source: "Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later," *Educational Psychology Review* (2019) — accessed via ERIC (https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1217401) and Maastricht University repository (https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/cognitive-architecture-and-instructional-design-20years-later/).
Design implication: don't treat "reduce clutter" and "aid understanding" as competing budgets in Riemann's UI — every unit of extraneous load removed from the zoom-dial chrome is capacity that goes straight into understanding the content, so chrome minimalism is not a luxury trade-off against comprehension, it's a direct lever on it.

**Finding: The expertise-reversal effect (techniques that help novices can hurt experts, and vice versa) is best explained as a special case of the more general element-interactivity effect — what changes with expertise is how many raw elements a learner must hold in working memory to interpret one "chunk," because experts have pre-compiled chunks (schemas) that novices haven't built yet.**
Evidence strength: STRONG for the underlying element-interactivity mechanism (well-replicated across decades of CLT experiments); MODERATE-to-STRONG specifically for it subsuming expertise-reversal (this is a 2016/2017 reframing, and a 2025 meta-analysis of the expertise-reversal effect itself exists as further corroboration).
Sources: Springer, "The Expertise Reversal Effect is a Variant of the More General Element Interactivity Effect" (2016/2017), *Educational Psychology Review* (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1; ERIC record https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1141803); ScienceDirect, "A cornerstone of adaptivity – A meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect" (2025) (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225000660); Wikipedia summary cross-checked (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect).
Design implication: this is close to a first-principles justification for the whole zoom-dial concept. A fixed level of detail is *necessarily* miscalibrated for either novices (too dense, too many raw elements) or experts (too sparse, insultingly low information rate) on the same content. The right design primitive is exactly what's planned: a continuous dial the person or the system sets per-session, not a fixed "simplified vs full" toggle. It also implies Riemann should let familiarity with *this specific piece of content* (not just general domain expertise) push the dial — rereading a doc you've already summarised once should default higher on the dial (closer to L3) since your local element-interactivity has dropped.

**Finding: Mayer's principles for reducing extraneous processing (coherence, signalling, redundancy, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity) have real, if declining-over-time, effect sizes; an early synthesis (Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning) reports coherence d≈0.70, signalling d≈0.46, redundancy d≈0.87, spatial contiguity d≈0.79, temporal contiguity d≈1.30; a broader recent meta-analysis (2024/2025, 92 articles / 181 studies / 591 effects) finds an overall pooled effect of g≈0.37 with significant moderation by study year (effects shrinking as comparison conditions improve) and reports more specific modern estimates: contiguity g≈0.74, multimedia (text+diagram) g≈0.39, signalling/cueing g≈0.38, segmenting g≈0.32–0.36, and coherence/seductive-details g≈−0.37 to −0.41 (i.e., irrelevant "seductive" detail measurably *hurts*).**
Evidence strength: STRONG (this is exactly the meta-analytic evidence base the brief asks for; declining effect size over time is itself a well-documented and important caveat, not a reason to distrust the core finding).
Sources: Cambridge Handbook chapter, "Principles for Reducing Extraneous Processing in Multimedia Learning" (https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E), mirrored PDF (https://edtechuvic.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2022/09/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles.pdf); ScienceDirect, "A meta-analysis of Richard Mayer's multimedia learning research: Searching for boundary conditions..." (2024/2025) (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X25000673), also indexed at Illinois Experts (https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-richard-mayers-multimedia-learning-research-se/).
Design implication: (1) Coherence → the summariser should be instructed to actively *exclude* engaging-but-irrelevant colour/anecdote from L0–L2, not just compress; seductive details measurably subtract comprehension, they don't just fail to add it. (2) Signalling → each abstraction level should visually cue its own structure (bold on the claim-bearing clause of a gist, not just prose) since this is one of the more robust, replicated levers. (3) Spatial/temporal contiguity → this is the direct research basis for the anchoring requirement already in the plan (keep the same passage at the viewport centre across zoom) — contiguity is one of the largest and most consistent effects found, so the anchoring mechanic isn't a nice-to-have, it's implementing the single best-evidenced multimedia principle. (4) Redundancy → don't show the L1 gist *and* highlight the exact same sentence in L3 simultaneously on screen; pick one.

### Visual hierarchy, Gestalt, preattentive attributes, Tufte/Few/Cairo, clutter

**Finding: Preattentive attributes are processed in under ~200–250ms, before conscious/serial visual search. Ware (Information Visualization: Perception for Design) groups them into four categories — form, colour, position, motion. Healey's taxonomy lists 16 specific cues (orientation, length, closure, size, curvature, density, number, hue, luminance, intersections, terminators, 3D depth, flicker, direction/velocity of motion, lighting direction).**
Evidence strength: STRONG (decades of replicated visual-search psychophysics; this is closer to perceptual science than a design opinion).
Sources: NCSU, Healey's "Perception in Visualization" course notes (https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/healey/PP/); UX Collective summary of Healey/Ware (https://uxdesign.cc/preattentive-attributes-of-visual-perception-and-their-application-to-data-visualizations-7b0fb50e1375); Interaction Design Foundation literature summary (https://ixdf.org/literature/article/preattentive-visual-properties-and-how-to-use-them-in-information-visualization).
Design implication: use a *small, consistent* preattentive vocabulary to mark zoom level and structure rather than relying on reading — e.g., a single hue or luminance step per level, a consistent left-margin depth-indent that maps to tree depth, motion (a brief pulse) only for the newly-arrived L2 gist while SSE streams in. Since these are processed pre-consciously, they're cheap on load budget precisely because they don't compete with reading comprehension for the same channel.

**Finding: Rosenholtz's Feature Congestion and Subband Entropy give quantifiable, computable measures of visual clutter (colour/orientation/luminance crowding, and redundancy of visual information respectively), validated against human clutter-perception judgments and usable on arbitrary images, not just charts.**
Evidence strength: STRONG (peer-reviewed, Journal of Vision 2007, widely cited and replicated as a working clutter metric in HCI/vision-science literature since).
Sources: MIT Persci lab overview (https://persci.mit.edu/research/clutter/); Journal of Vision, "Measuring visual clutter" (https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2122001); open-source implementation confirming the metrics are computable today (https://github.com/AlexeyGOblov/visual-attention-audit).
Design implication: this is a genuinely *measurable* QA gate, not just aesthetic judgment — Riemann's web page could programmatically clutter-score each rendered level (there's an existing open implementation) and flag if, e.g., an L2 view with many section-gist blocks crosses a clutter threshold, as a signal to auto-collapse or paginate rather than rendering everything flat.

**Finding: Tufte's data-ink ratio / chartjunk framework is influential but contested — critics note it is opinion-based with far fewer empirical citations behind it than instrumented HCI/vision-science work, and at least one controlled study (Inbar & Tractinsky, "Minimalism in information visualization") found users actually *preferred* non-minimalist, decorated bar graphs over maximal-data-ink versions, i.e. low acceptance of the principle as a universal aesthetic rule even where it may still hold as an efficiency rule.**
Evidence strength: MODERATE for the core data-ink/small-multiples/sparklines toolkit as *techniques* (small multiples and sparklines are independently well-supported by comparison and pattern-detection research); WEAK/CONTESTED specifically for "always maximize data-ink ratio" as a universal preference-optimizing rule.
Sources: Community write-up with citations, "Tufte's Principles of Data-Ink" (https://jtr13.github.io/cc19/tuftes-principles-of-data-ink.html); critical piece, "Minimalism and the absurdity of the data-to-ink-ratio" (https://www.frank.computer/blog/2025/04/data-to-ink.html); ACM DL record of Inbar & Tractinsky (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1362550.1362587).
Design implication: adopt small multiples and sparklines where they fit (e.g., a compact per-section "read-time" or "confidence" sparkline row at L1/L2), but don't chase minimal data-ink as an end in itself — a slightly warmer, less austere rendering of the zoom-dial page is not evidence-contradicted, and "clean minimalist" is a style choice, not a proven cognitive-load win by itself. Tie any minimalism decision to the *measured* clutter score (Rosenholtz) rather than to the data-ink heuristic.

### Typography & readability

**Finding: Optimal line length is commonly cited as ~50–75 characters per line (CPL), with ~66 CPL the most-cited single target (Bringhurst's 45–75 range); very short lines increase the frequency of costly saccade/return sweeps, very long lines make it harder to track the correct next line, and users subjectively prefer shorter lines even where longer lines can sometimes be read slightly faster on screens.**
Evidence strength: MODERATE (converges across a literature review and multiple practitioner sources, but "50–75" is itself a range synthesised from heterogeneous older studies, not one clean RCT number).
Sources: Visible Language literature review, "Optimal Line Length in Reading" (https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/5765, ERIC record https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ749012); Baymard Institute applied summary (https://baymard.com/blog/line-length-readability).
Design implication: cap the markdown render width (a max-width content column, not full viewport) at roughly 60–75 characters for L1–L3 prose; L0's one-liner is short enough this doesn't apply.

**Finding: Dyslexia-specific fonts (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie) show no reliable, replicated benefit to reading speed or accuracy; a 2017 alternating-treatment study found no improvement, one comparison study found OpenDyslexic actually *reduced* speed/accuracy versus Arial/Times, and a recent meta-analysis (15 studies, 91 effect sizes, N=688) found no consistent effect. What does have some supporting evidence is increased letter/word spacing.**
Evidence strength: MYTH/DEBUNKED for dedicated dyslexia fonts as a font-family effect; WEAK/PRELIMINARY-but-positive for spacing as the actual active ingredient.
Sources: PubMed meta-analysis record, "Does font improve reading in dyslexic children?" (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42536336/); NCBI/PMC original OpenDyslexic study (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5629233/); International Dyslexia Association position (https://dyslexiaida.org/do-special-fonts-help-people-with-dyslexia/).
Design implication: flagged explicitly as requested — do not add an "OpenDyslexic mode" as an accessibility feature expecting a real comprehension win; if targeting dyslexic/ADHD readers, prioritize generous letter/line spacing, line length control, and left-aligned (never justified) ragged-right text instead, which have a more credible evidence trail (justification creates uneven word-spacing "rivers" that increase visual noise — this is widely stated in typography practice literature though I did not find a dedicated RCT for it in this pass; flagging as a gap).

**Finding: NN/g's F-shaped scanning pattern, found in the original 2006 eye-tracking study (232 users, thousands of pages), is now understood by NN/g's own follow-up research as a *failure state* that emerges specifically when content lacks clear headings/hierarchy — well-structured content (strong headings, short scannable chunks) instead produces the more effective "layer-cake" pattern, where fixations land on headings/subheadings with only occasional dips into body text, and this is described as the most effective non-exhaustive scan strategy for finding information.**
Evidence strength: STRONG (large-sample original eye-tracking study plus a direct, named follow-up correction from the same lab).
Sources: NN/G, "F-Shaped Pattern of Reading: Misunderstood, But Still Relevant" (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/); NN/G, "The Layer-Cake Pattern of Scanning Content on the Web" (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/); NN/G, "Text Scanning Patterns: Eyetracking Evidence" (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/).
Design implication: this is a direct, strong argument for how L2 (section gists) should render — as genuinely distinct headed blocks with short (40–80 word, matching the plan's own target) bodies, not a wall of paragraph text with implicit section breaks. It also argues for bolding/front-loading the claim in each gist sentence, so a layer-cake scanner reading only headings+first-clauses still gets the gist's payload.

### Colour

**Finding: Categorical colour distinguishability drops steeply between 5 and 6 simultaneous categories and stays comparatively flatter from 6–10; practitioner and CVD-safety guidance converges on capping categorical palettes around 5–7 hues, using vetted sets (ColorBrewer, Okabe-Ito) with adequate CIELAB lightness separation (~30+ units between any pair), and never using colour as the sole encoding channel — always pairing it with a redundant channel (position, shape, label, text).**
Evidence strength: MODERATE-to-STRONG (the perceptual dropoff around 5–6 is grounded in perceptual/visualization research; the specific "5–7" practitioner cap is a design-guidance convergence rather than one clean experiment).
Sources: arXiv, "Revisiting Categorical Color Perception in Scatterplots" (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.03787); Colorblind.io data-visualization guidance (https://colorblind.io/guides/data-visualization); EU data-visualisation accessible-colour-palettes guide (https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/accessible-colour-palettes).
Design implication: if Riemann ever colour-codes tree depth/level (e.g., a colour per zoom level, or per source-document in a multi-doc view), keep it to ≤5 distinct hues and always co-encode with position (the dial itself) or an explicit "L2" label — never rely on colour alone to say what level you're at, both for CVD safety and because it's redundant with a channel (position on the dial) that's already unambiguous.

### Motion & animation

**Finding: Heer & Robertson (2007, IEEE TVCG, "Animated Transitions in Statistical Data Graphics") found animated transitions between related chart states aid "object constancy" — preserving a viewer's tracking of an object's identity through changes in position, size, shape, colour — and thereby help preserve the user's mental map across a representational change, versus an instant cut which forces the viewer to re-orient from scratch.**
Evidence strength: STRONG for the object-constancy mechanism and its value in view transitions (highly cited, replicated design-principles paper, foundational to a large amount of subsequent transition-design work in InfoVis/UIST).
Sources: Stanford Vis Group paper page (http://vis.stanford.edu/papers/animated-transitions); full PDF (https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf); IEEE DL record (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TVCG.2007.70539).
Design implication: this is the direct research grounding for the plan's "anchoring" requirement — when the dial moves a level, animate the surrounding content's reflow (even a short 150–250ms scroll/fade, not necessarily elaborate morphing) rather than snapping instantly, so the anchored passage visibly stays "the same thing" as it changes representation. But cap animation duration short and always honour `prefers-reduced-motion` (not explicitly evidenced in this pass, but is the standard accessibility baseline — flagging as an assumption, not a citation) by falling back to an instant cut with the anchor position preserved by scroll offset alone, since some users find motion distracting or vestibular-triggering rather than orienting.

### Information density / calm technology / interruptions

**Finding: Interface density preference is expertise- and frequency-dependent — practitioner sources report experts/power users perform better and prefer denser, more feature-rich layouts that reduce navigation/clicks, while novices/casual users need sparser layouts; at least one source frames "minimalist" and "dense-but-well-organized" as different failure/success axes, not the same axis (i.e. density isn't simply the opposite of "good"; badly organized density is bad, well-organized density is fast).**
Evidence strength: WEAK/MODERATE — this synthesis leans heavily on UX-practitioner sources (design-system docs, blog analysis) rather than controlled academic studies in this pass; the expert/novice split itself is a long-standing HCI truism (progressive disclosure literature) but I did not open a dedicated peer-reviewed density study here — flagging as a gap to firm up.
Sources: "Dashboard Information Density Law in UX" (https://uxuiprinciples.com/en/principles/dashboard-information-density-law); "Dense Interfaces Are Back: Why Information Hierarchy Beats Minimalism in 2026" (https://mydesigner.gg/blog/dense-interfaces-information-hierarchy-2026); Basis Design System's density foundation doc (https://design.basis.com/foundations/density).
Design implication: directly supports the brief's existing "adaptive load scaling" principle — Riemann's default dial position and default level of chrome should key off *measured familiarity/expertise with this specific tree* (has the user zoomed this doc before? how many docs have they abstracted this week?), not just a single global "simple vs power-user" toggle set once in settings.

**Finding: Notification/interrupt research shows the cognitive cost is not just the interruption itself — merely suppressing the urge to respond to a visible notification consumes working-memory/attentional resources even with zero interaction, and complex/long-horizon tasks are disproportionately hurt because resuming requires reconstructing context. Interruptions early in a task are more likely to cause complete loss of the task goal than interruptions late in a task; interruptions relevant to the current task are markedly less disruptive than irrelevant ones. Adaptive placement (peripheral for low urgency, escalating toward central focus only as urgency rises) reduces workload and frustration versus fixed always-central alerts, per a mixed-reality notification study.**
Evidence strength: MODERATE-to-STRONG for the general interruption-cost and early-vs-late findings (grounded in the established "interruption science" literature, e.g. Bailey/Iqbal-style task-resumption work); WEAK/PRELIMINARY specifically for the adaptive-placement MR study (single study, novel modality).
Sources: Oxford Academic/Journal of Occupational Health, "Effects of task interruptions caused by notifications..." (https://academic.oup.com/joh/article/65/1/e12408/7479297); Wikipedia overview citing the underlying field, "Interruption science" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interruption_science); arXiv, "Non-urgent Messages Do Not Jump into My Headset Suddenly! Adaptive Notification Design in Mixed Reality" (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.05893).
Design implication: Riemann's principle of "never takes real-world actions" plus this evidence together argue that when Riemann *does* need to surface something (e.g., "L1 is ready" while you're mid-read at L3), it should be a peripheral, non-modal cue (a small badge, not a popup that steals focus) and should never interrupt mid-reading-session unless the content is directly relevant to what's on screen right now.

### Dark/light mode

**Finding: A 2026 Springer-published comparison and related 2025 studies converge on a trade-off rather than a single winner — light mode (dark text on light background) supports objectively faster reading, higher measured readability, and lower cognitive workload; dark mode wins on subjective visual comfort and reported eye strain, especially in low-light or extended sessions, with majority user *preference* (roughly 2/3–4/5 across cited surveys) for dark mode despite the performance edge going the other way.**
Evidence strength: MODERATE (multiple convergent but mostly small/recent studies; genuine trade-off rather than settled consensus, and self-report eye-strain measures are noisier than objective reading-speed measures).
Sources: Springer chapter, "Dark Mode vs. Light Mode: Effects on Mobile Focus, Readability, and Task Performance" (https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-30552-7_26); PMC, "Immediate Effects of Light Mode and Dark Mode Features on Visual Fatigue in Tablet Users" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12027292/); NN/g practitioner synthesis (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/).
Design implication: don't hard-code a theme as "the accessible one." Since v1 is a local web page, respect OS `prefers-color-scheme` by default, but treat theme as a candidate *adaptive* signal too — e.g., default to light for a deep, effortful L3 read (where the reading-speed edge matters most) and allow dark for browsing/low-light/low-energy sessions where comfort matters more than raw throughput.

## Design implications for Riemann (consolidated)
- Treat the zoom dial's L0→L3 as literally an element-interactivity dial: the summariser's job at each level is to reduce how many raw facts must be co-held to parse one unit, not just to shorten word count. This reframes what "good abstraction" means for the abstraction-researcher's pipeline too.
- Bake Mayer's spatial/temporal contiguity principle into the anchoring mechanic — it has some of the largest, most consistent effect sizes found, and anchoring is already planned; this just confirms it's worth getting right rather than treating as cosmetic.
- Give each L2 section-gist a real heading + a short (40–80 word) body, matching both the existing plan's target size and the layer-cake-scanning evidence, and front-load the claim in the first clause.
- Use a consistent, minimal preattentive vocabulary (one hue or luminance step, one consistent indent) to mark "what level/depth am I at," since these cues cost near-zero reading attention.
- Add a read-time estimate and a breadcrumb/position indicator near the dial itself — the zoomable-UI literature and general navigation-orientation practice both flag "where am I in the tree" as a distinct problem from "what am I looking at," and the plan doesn't yet call out a breadcrumb component explicitly.
- Cap prose column width to ~60–75 characters regardless of level.
- Don't add a dyslexia-specific font toggle expecting a real effect; offer spacing/line-length controls instead if targeting neurodivergent readers.
- Instrument a clutter score (Rosenholtz-style feature congestion is open-source and computable) on rendered L1/L2 views as an automatic QA/adaptivity signal, not just a subjective design review.
- Any notification the system does surface (e.g. "L1 ready") should be peripheral/non-modal, never interrupt an active L3 read, and should respect the finding that early-task interruptions are especially costly to context.
- Animate level transitions briefly (~150–250ms) for object constancy, with an instant-cut fallback under `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Let default dial position, chrome density, and even light/dark theme be adaptive signals tied to familiarity-with-this-content and session context, not fixed global settings — this directly extends the brief's own "adaptive load scaling" principle with concrete triggers.

## Signals Riemann could measure
- Dwell time per level before zooming further (proxy for "was this level sufficient," low-friction/implicit).
- Re-visits to the same level/section (proxy for confusion or re-reading, implicit).
- Dial velocity/hesitation (fast decisive zoom to L3 vs. slow back-and-forth dithering — the latter may indicate the abstraction levels are miscalibrated for this content or this moment).
- Whether the anchor passage matches what the user actually re-centres on manually after a zoom (if they immediately scroll away from the "anchored" spot, the anchoring heuristic guessed wrong).
- Session recency/frequency on the *same* document (drives the expertise-reversal-informed default dial position).
- Time of day / ambient light (if accessible) as an input to a light/dark default, purely as a default, always overridable.
- Explicit but cheap: a single post-read "was this the right level of detail?" micro-prompt (too-shallow/right/too-deep), sparingly triggered, not after every read.

## Contradictions and caveats
- Tufte's data-ink minimalism is directly contradicted by at least one controlled preference study (Inbar & Tractinsky) — treat "minimal" as a style default, not an evidence-mandated one, and don't let it override the expert/dense-preference finding above for power users.
- Dark mode: comfort and performance point in different directions; there is no single "right" default, only a context-dependent one.
- The expert/novice density finding here is thin (mostly practitioner sources, not peer-reviewed studies) — treat as a working hypothesis Riemann should test against Sam's own behaviour, not settled science.
- Most of the readability/typography literature (line length, F-pattern) comes from general web-reading contexts, not specifically from summarisation/abstraction-tree UIs or from ADHD populations — transfer to Riemann's specific use case is plausible but unverified; the ADHD-researcher and comprehension-researcher slugs should be checked for anything that overrides these general findings for this specific user.
- Mayer's effect sizes shrink over study-year in the newer meta-analysis — this is itself evidence the field's comparison baselines have improved (control conditions got better), not that the principles stopped working; don't discount them because the number looks smaller than the older, oft-cited figures.
- I did not find a dedicated controlled study on justified-vs-ragged text specifically for on-screen reading in this pass (the recommendation against justification is typography-practice consensus, not confirmed here with a primary source) — flagged as weak/unverified above rather than presented as settled.
- Rosenholtz's clutter metrics were validated on natural images/general displays; I did not confirm a study applying them specifically to markdown/text-block UIs like Riemann's — plausible transfer, not confirmed.

## Gaps (for a second research wave)
- **Reduced-motion and vestibular-sensitivity evidence specifically** — I asserted `prefers-reduced-motion` as a baseline without a dedicated source; worth a targeted pass (WCAG rationale docs, vestibular-disorder UX research).
- **Justified vs ragged-right text on screens** — practitioner consensus exists but I didn't locate a primary controlled study; worth checking typography-research journals directly (e.g. work out of the Legibility/Readability research community).
- **Object-constancy/animated-transition research specifically for *text* (not chart) state changes** — Heer & Robertson is chart-focused; does the same mechanism hold for reflowing text blocks changing detail level? This seems like exactly the gap between existing InfoVis literature and Riemann's actual use case, and probably the single most valuable follow-up for this slug.
- **Expert/novice density preference** — needs a firmer academic backing than the practitioner sources found here; check HCI adaptive-interface literature (e.g. work on adaptive UIs, mixed-initiative interfaces) for controlled studies, not just design blogs.
- **Semantic zoom specifically in HCI (not GIS/maps)** — Bartram et al.'s 1995 "continuous zoom" fisheye paper came up as a root reference; worth the spatial-researcher (06-spatial) pulling this thread further since it directly predates and likely informs the "zoom dial" concept, including known failure modes of continuous zoom interfaces (disorientation, "lost in zoom") that a UIST/CHI search would surface.
- **Notification/interrupt design specifically for single-user assistant/ambient-computing contexts** (vs. the workplace-communication-app studies found here) — Riemann's "never takes real-world actions, presents information" framing is closer to ambient/calm-tech than to Slack-notification research; worth a dedicated pass on Weiser & Brown's original calm-technology writing and its more recent HCI descendants.
