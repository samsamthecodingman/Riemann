# 04 — Modalities: modes of information presentation

## TL;DR
- Dual coding (Paivio) is real and mechanistically grounded: verbal and nonverbal systems are separate but interconnected, so pairing a compact visual with concise text can beat either alone — but only if the two aren't just re-saying the same content in the same channel at the same time (that's redundancy, and it hurts). [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-coding_theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-coding_theory)
- Picture superiority is large and replicated in recognition/recall (d≈1.5–2.0 in some meta-analytic estimates) but concrete/imageable content benefits far more than abstract content, and it doesn't reliably speed up problem-solving. [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picture_superiority_effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picture_superiority_effect); [huliru.com meta-analysis](https://huliru.com/publication/Journal_EPR2021_Multimedia_Effect_in_Problem_Solving_A_Meta-Analysis.pdf)
- The modality effect (narration+diagram > on-screen text+diagram) is one of Mayer's best-supported principles (53/61 tests, median d=0.76) — but it **reverses** for long/complex/self-paced material, because transient speech overloads working memory while text can be reread and self-paced. This is the single most important caveat for Riemann's voice channel. [Cambridge Handbook ch.9](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/modality-principle-in-multimedia-learning/30769D7ADEE0EEE1C4CE5A01253C2819); [Leahy & Sweller reversal, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230074023_Cognitive_load_theory_modality_of_presentation_and_the_transient_information_effect); [VR reversal replication, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-022-09611-7)
- Learning styles ("visual learner" / "auditory learner" matching) is DEBUNKED as a matching hypothesis — Pashler et al. 2008 found essentially no crossover-interaction evidence for meshing instruction to a learner's preferred style. What *is* real is content-modality fit (matching modality to the structure of the material, not to the person). [Pashler et al. 2008](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x)
- Concept/knowledge maps have a real, moderate-to-large meta-analytic advantage over text/outlines for recall and transfer (Nesbit & Adesope 2006, 67 effect sizes, 55 studies, n=5,818) — bigger when students *construct* the map themselves rather than just view one made for them. [Nesbit & Adesope 2006, SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/research/NesbitAdesope2006.pdf)
- Listening and reading comprehension are, on average, equivalent (Clinton-Lisell 2022 meta-analysis, g=0.07, n.s.) — but reading wins when self-paced and when the test measures inference rather than literal recall, i.e., exactly the conditions where re-reading and skimming matter. [Clinton-Lisell 2022, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00346543211060871)
- Lecture/podcast playback speed up to ~2x costs essentially nothing in immediate or delayed comprehension; costs appear past ~2.5x (Murphy et al. 2022). This directly supports a user-controlled speed dial on Riemann's audio channel. [Murphy et al. 2022, UCLA PDF](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2021/11/ACP-Lecture-Speed-Murphy-2021-in-press.pdf)
- Animation only beats static diagrams when it conveys information static images genuinely can't (fine-grained motion/microsteps) — Tversky's Congruence Principle. Most animation-vs-static studies favoring animation turn out to differ in *information content*, not just modality. [Tversky "Animation: can it facilitate?" PDF](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)
- Narrative is genuinely "psychologically privileged" — causal, goal-structured story form is easier to encode and recall than expository text of equal content, per Willingham and a meta-analysis of narrative vs. expository memory. This is a real, low-cost lever Riemann could use in L1 summaries. [Willingham, "Privileged Status of Story" PDF](https://kbsgk12project.kbs.msu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/02/Ask-the-Cognitive-Scientist.pdf); [narrative vs expository meta-analysis, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8219577/)
- Switching representational modality is not free — task-switching literature shows real, measurable switch costs even when only response/stimulus modality changes, and a 2025 HCI paper explicitly proposes a "Modality-Switch Cognitive Cost" metric for multimodal interaction design. Riemann's zoom dial should probably treat modality changes (not just abstraction-level changes) as a heavier, more deliberate action than level changes. [MSCC paper, ACM DL](https://doi.org/10.1145/3771882.3773955); [modality-shift cost review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3149918/)

## Findings

### 1. Dual coding theory (Paivio)
**Claim:** Verbal and nonverbal (imagery) information are processed by partially independent but interconnected cognitive subsystems; concrete/imageable material can be dual-coded (stored both verbally and visually), giving it a retrieval advantage over verbal-only material.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG (decades of replication, foundational to both cognitive psychology and Mayer's multimedia learning theory).
**Sources:** [Wikipedia, Dual-coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-coding_theory) (with citations to Paivio 1971/1986); [Clark & Paivio, "Dual coding theory and education" PDF](https://nschwartz.yourweb.csuchico.edu/Clark%20&%20Paivio.pdf) — I opened this PDF link but did not deep-read beyond the search snippet; treat as MODERATE confidence on details beyond what's quoted above.

### 2. Picture superiority effect
**Claim:** Pictures are recognized/recalled more accurately than words for equivalent content; effect is large in recognition memory tasks.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG for memory/recognition tasks; MODERATE-WEAK for whether it speeds *problem-solving* or comprehension of abstract/procedural content — a meta-analysis of "multimedia effect in problem solving" found no significant effect on response time when pictures were added to problem text.
**Sources:** [Wikipedia, Picture superiority effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picture_superiority_effect); [Hu, "Multimedia Effect in Problem Solving: A Meta-Analysis" PDF](https://huliru.com/publication/Journal_EPR2021_Multimedia_Effect_in_Problem_Solving_A_Meta-Analysis.pdf)
**Caveat:** Effect is driven by dual-coding of *concrete* content — abstract concepts (most of what Riemann will summarize: emails, arguments, plans) get little or no picture-superiority boost unless a genuinely apt visual metaphor/diagram is found, which is hard to automate reliably (see LLM diagram generation below).

### 3. Modality effect and its reversal
**Claim:** For short/simple multimedia content, spoken narration + diagram beats on-screen text + diagram (avoids split-attention/visual overload). For long/complex content, this reverses: text + diagram beats narration + diagram, because speech is transient (can't be re-read) and overloads working memory, while text supports self-pacing and rereading.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG for the base modality effect (Mayer & Moreno; 53/61 supporting tests, median d=0.76). MODERATE-STRONG for the reversal — replicated at least twice (Leahy & Sweller's original transient-information-effect work, and a 2022 VR-environment replication).
**Sources:** [Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, ch. 9 (Modality Principle)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/modality-principle-in-multimedia-learning/30769D7ADEE0EEE1C4CE5A01253C2819); [Leahy & Sweller, "Cognitive load theory, modality of presentation and the transient information effect"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230074023_Cognitive_load_theory_modality_of_presentation_and_the_transient_information_effect); [Springer 2022, "modality effect reverses in a VR learning environment"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-022-09611-7)
**This is the single highest-value finding for Riemann's design** — see implications below.

### 4. Redundancy effect
**Claim:** Presenting identical information simultaneously in two channels (e.g., on-screen text that exactly duplicates narration) *hurts* learning relative to narration alone or text alone, because reconciling the two channels wastes working memory.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG, well-replicated Mayer principle.
**Source:** [ResearchGate, "Revising the Redundancy Principle in Multimedia Learning"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232540768_Revising_the_Redundancy_Principle_in_Multimedia_Learning); [Cambridge Handbook ch.10](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/redundancy-principle-in-multimedia-learning/448A5532008EB4B4BA17DBEB5A421920)
**Implication for Riemann:** if a TTS "read this level aloud" feature is added, it should almost certainly *not* simultaneously highlight/scroll identical on-screen text word-by-word at reading pace — captions summarizing structure (headers, current section) are fine; word-for-word redundant captions are the thing to avoid, unless the user is doing dedicated reading-while-listening practice (see finding 6, where RWL has separate, mixed evidence for L2/literacy contexts, not general adult comprehension).

### 5. Learning-styles myth vs. genuine content-modality fit
**Claim (myth):** Matching instructional modality to a learner's self-reported/measured "preferred learning style" (visual/auditory/kinesthetic) improves outcomes.
**Evidence strength:** MYTH/DEBUNKED as a person-matching hypothesis. Pashler et al. 2008 (commissioned by APS) found only one study with the necessary crossover-interaction design, and it didn't clearly support meshing.
**Claim (real):** The *content's structure* (causal, spatial, temporal, procedural) has a genuine best-fit modality — this is a completely different claim from learning styles and is well supported by the other findings in this document (concept maps for hierarchical/relational content, animation for continuous motion, narrative for causal sequences, etc.).
**Sources:** [Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, Bjork 2008, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x); [Nancekivell et al. 2020 follow-up, referenced via Mirjam Glessmer's summary](https://mirjamglessmer.com/2021/06/25/the-learning-styles-myth-based-on-pashler-et-al-2008-nancekivell-et-al-2020/)
**Implication:** Riemann should never ask "are you a visual or audio person" as a setup question or infer a fixed per-user modality preference to lock in. It's fine to let users *choose* a modality moment-to-moment (state-dependent, not trait-dependent), and it's fine (encouraged) to pick modality based on *what the content is*, not who's reading it.

### 6. Listening vs. reading comprehension (Clinton-Lisell 2022)
**Claim:** No reliable average difference between listening and reading comprehension (g=0.07, p=.23, k=46 studies, n=4,687, robust variance estimation). Reading wins when self-paced (g=0.13) and when the test measures inferential/general comprehension rather than literal recall (g=0.36 for inferential). Listening was NOT reliably better in any subgroup reported.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG (large, recent, methodologically careful meta-analysis in Review of Educational Research).
**Source:** [Clinton-Lisell 2022, SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00346543211060871) — I read the abstract and a summarized version; did not obtain full text.
**Implication:** Audio isn't a downgrade from text for Riemann's L1 gist-level content generally, but for anything requiring *inference* (the kind of "so what does this mean" synthesis an L0/L1 gist should support) or anything the user will want to re-scan, text (or user-pausable audio) has a real edge. Audio's comparative disadvantage is entirely about pacing control, not about the ear being an inferior channel per se — so a good scrubbing/rewind/pause UI on the audio channel should close much of that gap.

### 7. Playback speed (Murphy et al. 2022)
**Claim:** Lecture video speed up to 2x (and often 2.5x) causes no measurable loss in immediate or delayed comprehension versus 1x; costs appear beyond 2.5x. Applies to both immediate and one-week-delayed testing.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG for this specific well-designed study; MODERATE for generalizing to *all* audio content — this study used lecture video, not conversational podcasts or dense technical prose, and a companion body of research on "speed-watching" flags metacognitive risk (people's *felt* comprehension can decouple from actual comprehension at higher speeds, i.e. overconfidence).
**Sources:** [Murphy, Dillon, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn & Castel 2022, UCLA PDF](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2021/11/ACP-Lecture-Speed-Murphy-2021-in-press.pdf); [ScienceDirect 2025, "Is speed-watching a good idea? Potential metacognitive complications"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563225000032)
**Implication:** a speed control (1x–2x default-safe range) on any Riemann audio/TTS output is well justified; push past 2x with a light "you may be overestimating how much you're retaining" affordance rather than a hard block.

### 8. Concept maps / knowledge maps (Nesbit & Adesope 2006)
**Claim:** Learning with node-link concept/knowledge maps (viewing or constructing) outperforms text/outline conditions on recall and transfer; 67 effect sizes across 55 studies, Grade 4–postsecondary, multiple domains.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG (large meta-analysis, Review of Educational Research).
**Source:** [Nesbit & Adesope 2006, full PDF (author's site)](https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/research/NesbitAdesope2006.pdf); [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543076003413)
**Nuance found in related search results:** effect sizes vary substantially by *how* the map is used — constructing/modifying a map tends to show larger effects than passively viewing one, and comparison condition matters (concept maps beat outlines more decisively than they beat other visual formats). A follow-up meta-analysis, "Studying and Constructing Concept Maps: a Meta-Analysis," specifically separates studying vs. constructing — I found this via search but did not open the full text, so treat the studying-vs-constructing split as MODERATE confidence pending direct read.
**Implication:** this is strong support for exposing the abstraction *tree itself* as a navigable map/graph (which the plan already intends for the future spatial view), not just as a linear leveled document. If Riemann ever lets users edit or annotate the tree (even lightly — pin a node, rename a section), that active-construction boost may be earnable cheaply.

### 9. Diagrams/visuals by content type (composite finding, several sources; no single meta-analysis)
**Claim:** Different visual forms suit different content structures — this is broadly convergent across HCI/instructional-design literature though not one unified meta-analysis:
- **Causal/process content** → flowcharts, causal-loop diagrams (matches Tversky's Congruence Principle: form should mirror the structure of the content).
- **Hierarchical/taxonomic content** → tree diagrams, concept maps (Nesbit & Adesope above).
- **Comparative content** → tables (rows/columns), side-by-side layouts.
- **Temporal/sequential content** → timelines.
- **Quantitative content** → charts (bar/line/scatter depending on relationship shown).
- **Procedural (how-to) content** → numbered steps + worked examples, sometimes with animation for continuous-motion microsteps only (Tversky).
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE — individually each pairing has instructional-design support, but I did not find one meta-analysis unifying "content type → optimal diagram type" as a single validated taxonomy; this is more expert-consensus/practitioner knowledge (e.g., Congruence Principle) than a single RCT-backed claim. Flag as synthesized guidance, not a single citable finding.
**Sources:** [Tversky, "Animation: can it facilitate?" PDF](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf) (states and applies the Congruence Principle); general convergence with Nesbit & Adesope and Mayer's multimedia principles cited above.

### 10. Infographics
**Claim:** Infographics improve comprehension and recall vs. text-only in several studies (e.g., orthopaedic journal-club study: ~2/3 correct on infographic-paired papers vs. <1/2 for text-only; various education studies show retention gains).
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE — individual positive studies and a narrative "designing infographics" review exist, but search results explicitly note inconsistency across the literature ("previous research has been inconsistent regarding whether graphics benefit overall comprehension") and I did not find a rigorous meta-analysis isolating infographics specifically (as distinct from diagrams/pictures generally) from confounds like added effort/attention or novelty.
**Sources:** [ScienceDirect, "Infographics Improve Comprehension and Recall at the Orthopaedic Journal Club"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1931720420303895); [PMC, "Designing Infographics" review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9274103/)
**Implication:** treat "infographic" as a composite of already-validated principles (dual coding + concept-map structure + comparison tables), not a separate mechanism — Riemann doesn't need a distinct "infographic mode," just good composition of the primitives above.

### 11. Can LLMs reliably generate diagrams/concept maps from text?
**Claim:** Current LLM diagram generation (text→Mermaid/graph) is an active, unsolved research area in 2025–2026: benchmarks exist (MermaidSeqBench, DiagramEval) specifically *because* accuracy is not yet reliable — they score across dimensions like syntax validity, structural accuracy, semantic fidelity, and completeness, implying failure modes are common enough to need multi-axis scoring. One technique found: use vision-capable LLMs to *refine* extracted graphs after an initial pass (merge/dedupe nodes, add missing conceptual nodes, fix ambiguous edges) rather than trusting one-shot extraction. Structured DSLs like Mermaid syntax reduce (but don't eliminate) hallucination versus free-form graph description, because the constrained grammar acts as a partial guardrail.
**Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY — these are 2025 arXiv papers (not yet peer-reviewed venues I could confirm), and none gave a clean "X% accuracy" headline number I can respect as established; the existence of dedicated benchmarks is itself the strongest signal that reliability is still an open problem.
**Sources:** [DiagramEval (arXiv 2510.25761)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25761); [MermaidSeqBench (arXiv 2511.14967)](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2511.14967); ["Accurate and Consistent Graph Model Generation from Text with LLMs" (arXiv 2508.00255)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.00255)
**Implication — this directly affects the plan's architecture.** Riemann's `build.py` currently asks the summariser to produce leveled text (L0–L2 gists). If a future version adds auto-generated diagrams/concept maps of the tree, don't trust a single LLM pass to produce a correct graph — validate structurally (e.g., pydantic schema plus a check that every edge references a real node id, similar to the existing "retry once if validation fails" pattern already in the plan) and consider a second LLM pass specifically to critique/repair the graph, mirroring the "vision-refinement" pattern found above. Given the current evidence, an auto-generated node-link view of the abstraction tree is a very plausible near-term win (the tree already *is* a graph — no extraction problem, no hallucinated edges, since parent/child links come from the chunker, not from an LLM inferring a graph from prose). Auto-summarizing arbitrary *external* prose into a novel concept map (not backed by the existing tree structure) is the risky direction and should be treated as WEAK/experimental if attempted.

### 12. Animation vs. static graphics (Tversky)
**Claim:** Animation is not inherently superior to static sequences for learning; apparent wins for animation are usually confounded by animation conveying *more information* (continuous motion, microsteps) rather than modality being different per se. Per the Congruence Principle, animation should be reserved for genuinely continuous/dynamic content.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG/foundational (highly cited, 1861+ citations per one index) though from 2002 — a later meta-analysis ("Does animation enhance learning? A meta-analysis," ScienceDirect 2016) exists and I did not open its full text; flagged as a corroborating but unread source.
**Source:** [Tversky & Morrison, "Animation: can it facilitate?" full PDF (Stanford HCI)](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf) — opened.
**Implication:** Riemann's zoom-dial "continuous" animated transition between levels (the anchoring/scroll behavior described in the plan) is itself defensible under Congruence: the *content being conveyed by the animation* is "this passage's position in the abstraction hierarchy is preserved across a zoom," which is inherently continuous/spatial information — a good match for animation, not decoration.

### 13. Explorable explanations / interactive & dialogic formats
**Claim:** Interactive, manipulable explanations (Bret Victor's "reactive documents," Nicky Case's work, "Parable of the Polygons") aim to convert passive reading into active exploration by letting the reader manipulate variables and see consequences live.
**Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY as *controlled learning-outcomes evidence* — what I found is design philosophy and practitioner/essay-level argument (Victor's own essays, Wikipedia's "Explorable explanation" page), not RCTs comparing explorable-explanation comprehension/retention against static text. This is a case where the idea is influential and intuitively grounded in active-learning theory generally, but the *specific format's* efficacy isn't meta-analytically established the way worked examples or concept maps are.
**Sources:** [Wikipedia, Explorable explanation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explorable_explanation); [Bret Victor bio, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bret_Victor); [Nicky Case bio, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicky_Case)
**Implication:** treat "make the abstraction tree explorable" (e.g., a slider that live-updates a small example, or a draggable node graph) as a promising but unvalidated bet — worth doing because it's cheap and aligned with active-construction effects seen in concept-map research (finding 8), not because there's a specific RCT proving explorable text beats static text.

### 14. Worked examples
**Claim:** For novices, studying worked examples produces better learning and transfer than solving equivalent problems from scratch, because it offloads extraneous problem-solving search and leaves capacity for schema formation (germane load). Effect shrinks or reverses as expertise increases ("expertise reversal effect," referenced in the same literature though not deeply pursued in this search pass).
**Evidence strength:** STRONG, one of the best-replicated CLT-derived effects.
**Source:** [Wikipedia, Worked-example effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect); [ERIC PDF, "Cognitive Load Theory and the Use of Worked Examples"](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1161818.pdf)
**Implication:** for procedural content (e.g., a task/how-to email or a technical doc Riemann ingests), an L2/L3 rendering that preserves a full worked example intact (rather than abstracting it into a gist) may be more valuable than compressing it — the plan's chunker should probably treat a worked example as an atomic, low-priority-to-summarize unit, similar to how code blocks are often protected from summarization.

### 15. Narrative / story structure
**Claim:** Narratively structured content (causal, goal-driven, character/agent-centered) is recalled and comprehended better than expository content of equivalent informational content — described as "psychologically privileged" (Willingham 2004/2009), because story schemas are highly familiar and causal chains create a natural retrieval web.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG for the general narrative-vs-expository memory advantage (a dedicated meta-analysis exists, PMC8219577); MODERATE for Willingham's specific "privileged" framing, which I found only in secondary summaries (a Substack post and an AFT reprint of his "Ask the Cognitive Scientist" column), not the original academic source directly.
**Sources:** [Meta-analysis, "Memory and comprehension of narrative versus expository texts," PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8219577/); [Willingham, AFT reprint PDF](https://kbsgk12project.kbs.msu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/02/Ask-the-Cognitive-Scientist.pdf)
**Implication:** for content that has a natural causal/temporal arc (an email thread's back-and-forth, a project's timeline, a decision's rationale), Riemann's L0/L1 gist generation could be explicitly prompted to preserve causal/narrative connectives ("X happened, which led to Y") rather than flattening to a bag of facts — likely a cheap prompt-engineering win with real cognitive-science backing, distinct from and additive to the abstraction-level compression itself.

### 16. Modality switching cost
**Claim:** Switching between response/stimulus modalities carries a measurable cognitive cost distinct from pure motor cost — this is well established in the task-switching literature generally (modality-shift costs persist even for purely response-modality changes), and a late-2025 HCI paper explicitly proposes a "Modality-Switch Cognitive Cost" (MSCC) metric for multimodal *interaction design* specifically (not just lab task-switching), citing large performance variance explained by modality condition.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG for the underlying cognitive-psychology phenomenon (long, replicated task-switching literature); WEAK/PRELIMINARY for the specific MSCC interaction-design framing, which is a single very recent (2025) conference paper I have not been able to verify has been widely cited or replicated.
**Sources:** [PMC, "The role of response modalities in cognitive task representations"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3149918/); [ACM DL, "Mind the Switch: Measuring Modality-Switch Cognitive Costs (MSCC)"](https://doi.org/10.1145/3771882.3773955) — I read the abstract/listing only, not the full paper.
**Implication — important for the "multimodal switching" question in the brief:** letting a user flip modality (text↔audio↔diagram) at *any* abstraction level is plausible and desirable for adaptivity, but each flip is not free — it's more analogous to a level-change-plus-something than a pure level change. Practical design consequence: (a) don't auto-switch modality without the user asking (e.g., don't silently swap to audio because load looks high — that's a bigger disruption than just moving the zoom dial); (b) if offering a persistent "modality track" per level (e.g., always show a small diagram alongside L1 text, an option to toggle TTS on any level), prefer *stable, low-frequency* modality choices per session over rapid per-node switching; (c) the zoom dial (level change) and a modality toggle should be visually/interactionally distinct controls, and the modality toggle should probably persist across dial movements rather than reset, to avoid stacking two costly transitions.

## Design implications for Riemann

1. **Default TTS/audio to short content; gate long-form audio behind an explicit user choice, and always give pause/rewind/scrub controls.** The modality-effect reversal (finding 3) means audio's advantage is real only for short/simple material or when the user can control pacing. An L0 gist (≤25 words) or even L1 (1–2 min) is squarely in "audio is fine or better" territory; reading the full L3 aloud unbroken is exactly the "long transient complex" case where the effect reverses and text/self-paced control wins. Practically: make TTS-on-L0/L1 a one-tap default, and make TTS-on-L3 require the user to explicitly start it and always show a scrubber with pause.

2. **Never simultaneously narrate and highlight identical on-screen text word-for-word (redundancy effect, finding 4).** If Riemann adds "read this section aloud," either (a) narrate without displaying the identical text prominently (dim it, or show only the current L1/L2 gist as a caption, not full transcript), or (b) if displaying full text, don't force word-level synchronized highlighting as the primary UI — a light "currently reading paragraph N" indicator is fine, literal karaoke-style redundant captioning risks the documented redundancy penalty for actual comprehension (though it might still be fine for accessibility use cases — separate design goal).

3. **Expose the abstraction tree as a navigable graph/map, not only as a linear leveled document, especially at L2 (section gists).** Concept-map evidence (finding 8) is some of the strongest and most directly applicable evidence in this report: it's an existing, well-supported technique, and Riemann's data model (parent/child tree with ids) already *is* a concept map — no LLM-graph-extraction risk (finding 11) is even needed, since the structure comes from the chunker/build pipeline, not from asking an LLM to infer relationships from prose. This is a strong candidate to pull forward from "future spatial view" into a v1.x feature: even a simple collapsible/zoomable node-link rendering of the L2 section tree (in addition to the linear markdown view) could give a measurable comprehension boost for free.

4. **Let users lightly interact with the tree (pin, rename, or reorder a node) to capture some of the "constructing > viewing" concept-map boost (finding 8) and explorable-explanations engagement (finding 13),** without requiring them to build the tree from scratch (avoid adding germane load that competes with reading). Cheap version: an editable label on any L2 node, or drag-to-reorder within a level.

5. **Treat modality choice (text/audio/diagram) as a separate, sticky control from the zoom dial, not something that resets or auto-switches on every level change (finding 16).** The zoom dial already asks the user to process a level transition; stacking an unrequested modality transition on top compounds cost. Auto-adapting modality based on inferred load state (a stated Riemann principle) should probably only ever *offer* a modality change (e.g., a gentle "want this read aloud instead?" prompt) rather than silently switching it.

6. **Default playback speed control on any TTS/audio output up to ~1.5–2x with no comprehension-loss warning, and a soft warning past 2x (finding 7).** This is genuinely free efficiency for an ADHD user who wants speed; ship it as a first-class control, not an afterthought in settings.

7. **Prompt L0/L1 summarization to preserve causal/narrative connectives when the source content has them (finding 15), rather than only compressing to a flat list of facts.** E.g., "Sam asked for X, which prompted Y's reply that Z" beats "topics: X, Y, Z" at equal word count, per the narrative-privilege literature. This is a prompt-engineering change to `summarise.py`'s system prompt, not an architecture change.

8. **Preserve worked examples and procedural content as protected/atomic units rather than compressing them (finding 14).** When the chunker/summarizer encounters a clearly procedural passage (numbered steps, a walked-through calculation, code), bias toward showing it closer to verbatim at L2 (not just at L3), since compressing worked examples destroys exactly the information that makes them pedagogically effective.

9. **For any content-type-aware auto-formatting (e.g., rendering a detected comparison as a table, a detected timeline as a timeline), this is well-motivated by the Congruence Principle (finding 9/12) but should be scoped conservatively** — the evidence here is expert-consensus/practitioner-level, not meta-analytically proven per content type, so treat it as a good default heuristic to implement opportunistically (e.g., if the summarizer already emits structured JSON with a "type" hint per section, use it to pick a rendering primitive), not a core v1 requirement.

10. **Do not build a "preferred modality per user" profile or onboarding question ("are you a visual learner?") — this is the debunked learning-styles hypothesis (finding 5).** Any personalization should be about *state* (what did this user pick last time, under what load) and *content type* (what does this passage structurally need), never a fixed trait label.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Modality dwell/switch events:** which modality (text/audio/diagram) the user is in when they open a doc, how often they switch modality per session, and whether switches correlate with self-reported or inferred load state. Cheap: just log toggle-button presses with a timestamp and current zoom level.
- **Playback speed chosen and whether it's changed mid-session** (a rising or falling speed trend across a session may be a load-state signal worth surfacing elsewhere in the app, per the fluctuation/adaptive-signals subtasks).
- **Scrub/rewind frequency on audio** (frequent rewinds = comprehension isn't keeping up at current speed/content density — an implicit "this text needs a slower modality or lower level" signal).
- **Whether users who are offered a diagram/concept-map view of L2 actually open it, and how long they dwell there vs. the linear view** — directly tests finding 3's practical payoff for Riemann specifically, cheaply, via view-time logging, no survey needed.
- **Node-edit engagement (pin/rename/reorder) as a proxy for active engagement** — if this correlates with better later recall/return-to-document behavior, that's a cheap within-product test of the "constructing beats viewing" claim (finding 8) for Riemann's own tree structure specifically, rather than relying on 2006-era classroom-concept-map generalization.
- **Time-to-first-interaction and level at first interaction** — if users almost always jump straight past L0 audio to L2/L3 text for anything they then keep open, that's evidence the audio-first default (implication 1) isn't matching actual behavior and should be revisited.

## Contradictions and caveats

- **Picture superiority (finding 2) vs. abstract email/task content:** Riemann's primary content (email, notes, docs) is mostly abstract/verbal, not concrete/imageable. The picture-superiority literature's strongest effects are for concrete nouns/objects — don't over-generalize "add a picture, get a memory boost" to Riemann's actual content mix without a genuinely apt diagram (a bar chart of concrete quantities, a real flowchart of a real process) — a generic decorative icon next to an abstract paragraph is exactly the kind of "multimedia effect doesn't move response time" case (finding 2's problem-solving caveat).
- **Listening-vs-reading equivalence (finding 6) may not transfer to a single ADHD adult using Riemann in short, interrupted bursts** — the Clinton-Lisell meta-analysis aggregates across ages, tasks, and (likely) mostly neurotypical or unspecified populations in structured study conditions. Distractibility/interruption resilience of audio vs. text for this specific population is a genuine open question this meta-analysis doesn't answer (see Gaps below, and cross-reference the ADHD-focused subtask 03).
- **Concept-map effect sizes (finding 8) come mostly from classroom/study contexts (recall on a later test), not from "fastest path to a chosen depth of understanding in a live reading session"** — Riemann's actual use case (skim now, maybe never take a "test") is a different transfer target than most of this meta-analysis's outcome measures. The relevance is plausible but not proven for Riemann's specific goal.
- **LLM diagram-generation reliability (finding 11) is genuinely unresolved as of this research (2025–2026 benchmarks still being built)** — don't assume any LLM call to "turn this into a Mermaid diagram" will be trustworthy without structural validation; this directly contradicts an implicit temptation in the plan to eventually have the summariser emit diagrams freely.
- **Narrative privilege (finding 15) can conflict with fidelity/neutrality goals** — pushing causal/narrative framing onto content that Riemann is summarizing (e.g., someone else's email) risks *inserting* a causal interpretation the source didn't actually assert. This is a correctness/trust risk, not just a UX one — flag for the contrarian/gemini worker (10-contrarian-gemini) as a specific failure mode: narrative-biased summarization could misrepresent ambiguous source material as more causally certain than it is.
- **Explorable explanations (finding 13) is philosophically compelling but the evidence base I found is design-essay-level, not RCT-level** — don't over-weight this relative to concept maps or worked examples, which have real meta-analyses behind them.

## Gaps (for the second wave)

1. **ADHD-specific modality preference/tolerance data.** This subtask found general-population evidence; subtask 03 (ADHD) should specifically hunt for whether ADHD readers show a *different* modality-effect or listening/reading equivalence pattern than the Clinton-Lisell general-population meta-analysis — audio's transience and inability to skim could interact badly with ADHD attention-regulation difficulties in ways the general literature wouldn't surface. Worth a direct RPUSH to claude-adhd-researcher if not already covered.
2. **Interruption recovery by modality.** Given Riemann's ADHD/fluctuating-attention context, a genuinely under-researched (by me) question is: after an interruption, which modality lets someone resume fastest with least re-orientation cost — text (visually re-scannable) vs. audio (must rewind and re-listen) vs. diagram (spatially re-locatable)? This seems highly relevant to the "anchor" mechanic in the zoom dial and to subtask 08 (fluctuation).
3. **Empirical studies specifically on auto-generated concept maps' *accuracy* (not just LLM benchmark scores) as judged by domain experts or against human-made gold-standard maps** — I found benchmarks measuring internal consistency/syntax, not head-to-head studies of auto-map vs. human-map comprehension outcomes. Worth a targeted follow-up search restricted to education/HCI venues (CHI, IUI, LAK) rather than general arXiv ML papers.
4. **Podcast/conversational-summary format specifically** (as opposed to lecture-style narration) — the brief asks about "conversational/spoken summaries" and I did not find dedicated research comparing monologue TTS summary vs. a synthesized two-voice conversational summary (the NotebookLM-style format); this is a fast-moving 2024–2026 product-driven area that may have more industry writeups than peer-reviewed studies, worth a targeted search.
5. **Icon/symbol comprehension speed for UI chrome specifically** (not full diagrams) — the brief mentions icons; I did not find dedicated research here and it's more relevant to subtask 02 (cognitive-load design) than to this one; flagging so it isn't dropped.
6. **The "studying vs. constructing" concept-map follow-up meta-analysis** referenced in finding 8 (title: "Studying and Constructing Concept Maps: a Meta-Analysis") should be read in full by whoever picks up the second wave on this — I only saw it in a search snippet, and its effect-size split is directly relevant to implication 4's build-vs-view design choice.
