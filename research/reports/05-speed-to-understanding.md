# 05 — Speed to Understanding

## TL;DR
- "Levels of understanding" is not one dial — Bloom, SOLO, and Kintsch's surface/textbase/situation-model measure different things (verb complexity, response structure, and mental representation depth respectively). Riemann's L0–L3 abstraction tree most closely tracks Kintsch: L0/L1 aim for gist/situation-model, L3 is the only level that can support verbatim recall or SOLO "extended abstract" reasoning.
- Speed reading is debunked at the level of "read 3x faster with equal comprehension" (Rayner et al. 2016, STRONG) — the eye-movement and foveal-vision constraints are physiological, not a skill gap. What actually works is *skimming with a lower comprehension target*, which is a legitimate strategy, not cheating.
- Reading rate: ~238 wpm silent reading of non-fiction, 260 wpm fiction, from a 190-study meta-analysis (Brysbaert 2019, STRONG). This is the number Riemann should use for time estimates, not the commonly-cited 300 wpm.
- Pre-organisers work, but effect sizes vary hugely by type — graphic organizers (~1.24) far outperform plain text/expository organizers (~0.2–0.3) (MODERATE, old meta-analyses with heterogeneity issues). This argues for L2 (section gists) functioning as a graphic/structural map, not prose.
- Pretesting/prequestions boost learning even when the learner gets most of it wrong at first, provided they see the answer afterward — but the effect is mostly for factual, not conceptual, material (MODERATE-STRONG, Pan & Carpenter 2023 review). This is a candidate germane-load technique for Riemann to inject cheaply.
- Dunlosky et al. 2013's ten techniques give Riemann a menu with real cost/benefit data: practice testing and distributed practice are high-utility and cheap; summarization and highlighting are low/moderate utility; elaborative interrogation and self-explanation are moderate utility but need domain knowledge to generate good questions.
- Gist vs verbatim (fuzzy-trace theory, Reyna & Brainerd) is a genuinely different construct from "summary vs full text" — gist traces are the *meaning-based, durable* representation people actually reason and remember with; verbatim decays fast. This is a strong theoretical argument that L0/L1 (gist) may be the level users should return to for retention, not just for initial speed.
- Expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga) means anything Riemann does to reduce load for a novice (scaffolding, extra structure, worked context) can actively hurt or annoy an expert user on the same material — the abstraction tree needs to adapt not just depth-of-content but depth-of-scaffolding to the user's prior knowledge.
- No single technique or level is "correct" — the actual design problem is a **budget allocation problem**: given a time budget and a target depth, choose a path through L0→L3 and decide which of the cheap active techniques (prequestion, retrieval check, self-summary) to insert, given diminishing/nonlinear returns.

## Findings

1. **Claim:** Speed reading (radically faster reading, e.g., 500–1000+ wpm) does not preserve comprehension; the tradeoff between speed and comprehension is a hard constraint from visual/cognitive architecture (foveal acuity, saccade/fixation limits), not a trainable skill gap.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG (comprehensive review across eye-tracking, RSVP, and applied speed-reading-program studies).
   **Sources:** Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter & Treiman (2016), "So Much to Read, So Little Time: How Do We Read, and Can Speed Reading Help?", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100615623267 ; summary at https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/speed_reading.html ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/01/160114163035.htm

2. **Claim:** If the goal is not thorough understanding, skimming legitimately trades comprehension for speed and is a valid strategy, not a failure mode — the same paper explicitly separates "get through it fast with moderate comprehension" from "speed-read with full comprehension" (the latter being the debunked claim).
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG (same source as above; the authors' own framing).
   **Source:** same Rayner et al. 2016 paper.

3. **Claim:** Average adult silent reading rate for non-fiction English text is ~238 wpm, ~260 wpm for fiction; oral reading is ~183 wpm. Word length is a significant moderator (longer words in non-fiction lower the rate).
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG (meta-analysis, 190 studies, 18,573 participants, over a century of data).
   **Source:** Brysbaert (2019), "How many words do we read per minute? A review and meta-analysis of reading rate," *Journal of Memory and Language* — PDF at https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf ; abstract at https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/How-many-words-do-we-read-per-minute-A-review-and-Brysbaert/35bf5854514ee63095a6a3dc4a7bdaa2eb18929f

4. **Claim:** Bloom's revised taxonomy (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) is a taxonomy of *task/verb complexity a learner can perform*, not directly a taxonomy of representation depth. It's a two-dimensional grid (knowledge type × cognitive process).
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG as a widely-used, stable educational framework, but it is a classification scheme, not an empirically validated causal/psychological model — treat cautiously as "theory," not "law."
   **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom%27s_taxonomy ; https://www.coloradocollege.edu/other/assessment/how-to-assess-learning/learning-outcomes/blooms-revised-taxonomy.html

5. **Claim:** SOLO taxonomy (Biggs & Collis) — prestructural, unistructural, multistructural, relational, extended abstract — measures the *structural quality of a response* (how many relevant aspects are used, and whether they're integrated), independent of Bloom's verb list. It was explicitly designed as a more observable/practical alternative to Bloom.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (widely used in education, but less quantitatively validated than cognitive-science models like Kintsch's).
   **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_of_observed_learning_outcome ; https://www.structural-learning.com/post/what-is-solo-taxonomy

6. **Claim:** Kintsch's construction-integration model posits three levels of mental representation during reading: surface form (exact wording, decays fast), textbase (propositions/ideas as structured by the text), and situation model (integration of textbase with the reader's prior knowledge — the level associated with true, durable comprehension and transfer).
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG (foundational, replicated cognitive-science model, decades of follow-up work).
   **Sources:** https://sites.pitt.edu/~perfetti/PDF/Kintsch%20&%20Rawson.pdf ; https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2022/02/22/understanding-ci/ ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/psychology/textbase

7. **Claim:** Fuzzy-trace theory (Reyna & Brainerd) holds that gist (semantic/meaning) and verbatim (surface-form) memory traces are encoded, stored, and forgotten in parallel and largely independently — gist traces are more durable and are what people actually use for reasoning and decisions, while verbatim traces decay quickly.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG (well-established dual-process memory theory, extensive replication in memory/decision-making literature).
   **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuzzy-trace_theory ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/1467-8721.00192

8. **Claim:** Advance organizers (material presented before the content, at a higher level of abstraction/generality) improve learning and retention, but effect sizes vary enormously by organizer *type*: graphic organizers show the largest effect (~1.24), while plain expository-text organizers are much smaller (~0.2–0.3 in different meta-analyses).
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (meta-analyses are from the 1980s, with known heterogeneity and inconsistent operationalization of "advance organizer" across studies — a caveat the original meta-analysts themselves note).
   **Sources:** https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ290791 (Journal of Experimental Education 1983 meta-analysis) ; https://stars.library.ucf.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=4114&context=etd

9. **Claim:** Pretesting / prequestions (answering questions on material *before* reading it, then seeing correct answers) improves subsequent learning — even though learners get most pretest items wrong — and works across text, video, and lecture formats, provided feedback/study of correct answers follows. Effect is attentional (prequestions direct visual attention/fixations toward relevant content, shown via eye-tracking) and appears robust across adulthood, but conceptual (vs. factual) pretesting shows weaker transfer.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE-STRONG (multiple converging studies, a 2023 review, and a 2025 paper on robustness across adulthood — but built on a smaller literature than Dunlosky's headline techniques, and the conceptual/factual asymmetry limits generalization).
   **Sources:** Pan & Carpenter (2023), "Prequestioning and Pretesting Effects: A Review of Empirical Research, Theoretical Perspectives, and Applications" — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373821649 ; robustness-in-adulthood: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608025000597 ; eye-tracking mechanism (Lewis & Mensink 2012, cited within the review) — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334468513_Using_prequestions_to_enhance_learning_from_reading_passages

10. **Claim:** Of Dunlosky et al.'s (2013) ten learning techniques, practice testing and distributed practice are rated **high utility** (broad age ranges, material types, robust effects); elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice are **moderate utility** (promising but need more boundary-condition research, and elaborative interrogation/self-explanation require some domain knowledge to execute well); summarization, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery, and rereading are **low utility** for general use (rereading and highlighting especially — cheap but weak).
    **Evidence strength:** STRONG (this is itself a synthesis of decades of research, published in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*); a 2021 follow-up meta-analysis (Frontiers in Education) using quantitative effect sizes broadly confirms the ranking, finding distributed practice and practice testing most effective and underlining/summarization least effective among the (still net-positive) techniques.
    **Sources:** Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013) — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1529100612453266 ; https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/learning-techniques.html ; 2021 meta-analysis: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/full

11. **Claim:** The expertise reversal effect: instructional supports that help novices (worked examples, integrated/redundant explanations, heavy scaffolding) become neutral or actively harmful for learners with higher prior domain knowledge, because the scaffolding itself becomes extraneous cognitive load (redundancy effect) once the learner already has schemas to process the raw material directly.
    **Evidence strength:** STRONG as a robust, repeatedly-replicated effect within cognitive load theory; a 2025 meta-analysis reportedly quantifies it directly (title: "A cornerstone of adaptivity – A meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect").
    **Sources:** Kalyuga (2007), "Expertise Reversal Effect and Its Implications for Learner-Tailored Instruction," *Educational Psychology Review* — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3 ; PDF: https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf ; 2025 meta-analysis: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225000660

## Design implications for Riemann

- **Map L0–L3 to Kintsch, not to Bloom.** L0 (one-line gist) and L1 (2-min summary) should be built to preserve *situation-model* content (meaning, relations, "why it matters") over *textbase* content (exact propositions). L2 (section gists) is closer to textbase — structured but not yet integrated with the user's own knowledge. L3 (original) is the only level with surface/verbatim fidelity. Concretely: L0/L1 generation prompts should optimize for gist quality (a reader who only sees L0 should be able to reason about the content, not just recite a label), and L2 should preserve document structure/signposting rather than compress it away.
- **Default time estimates should use ~230–260 wpm, not 300+.** Any "this will take you N minutes" UI element in Riemann should be calibrated to Brysbaert's numbers, with the caveat that complexity (jargon density, sentence length, unfamiliarity) shifts this down further — worth exposing a "difficulty-adjusted" multiplier rather than a flat wpm.
- **Never advertise "read faster, understand the same."** If Riemann offers a fast pass through a document (skim mode / RSVP-style word flashing), be explicit in the UI that this trades comprehension depth for speed — framed as a legitimate choice ("skim for gist," not "speed read the whole thing"). This matches user expectations rather than promising something the science says is impossible.
- **Use L2 (section gists) as a structural/graphic pre-organiser before a user commits to L3.** Given graphic organizers meta-analytically beat prose organizers, render L2 with visible hierarchy/structure (headers, indentation, spatial layout — ties directly into the planned spatial view) rather than as another paragraph of prose.
- **Insert a single prequestion at the L0→L1 or L1→L2 transition, cheaply.** Before expanding a section, Riemann could surface a 1-line question ("what do you expect this section resolves?") — low cost, and the evidence favors factual/specific prequestions over broad conceptual ones, so auto-generated prequestions should target specific claims/numbers in the section rather than "what is this about."
- **Treat retrieval practice and spaced return as the highest-leverage optional add-on for durable understanding**, but keep it strictly opt-in / separate from the "understand this now" path — Dunlosky's data says it's high-utility but it's also the one technique that adds real time cost, so it belongs behind a "remember this later" toggle, not the default zoom-dial flow.
- **Adapt scaffolding depth to detected prior knowledge, not just requested depth.** Per expertise reversal, if Riemann can infer the user already knows the domain (past interaction history, or a quick calibration question), it should skip L1/L2 hand-holding and let them land closer to L3 directly — extra structure for an expert is friction, not help.
- **Model "target depth + time budget" as a search over a small path space**, not a single number: define depth targets (e.g., "gist," "working understanding," "can teach it") aligned loosely to SOLO levels (unistructural ≈ gist, relational ≈ working understanding, extended abstract ≈ can generalize/teach), each with an estimated base reading time (from wpm) plus optional technique add-ons (prequestion: seconds; retrieval check: ~1 min; self-summary: ~1–2 min) with known incremental payoff, and let the dial pick the shortest path hitting the target.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Implicit dwell/zoom time at each level** — time spent at L0 vs L1 vs L2 before zooming further or bailing, as a proxy for whether that level satisfied the user's need (fast bailout at L1 suggests L1 was sufficient; long dwell at L2 with no zoom to L3 suggests L2 hit the target depth).
- **Zoom direction and reversals** — zooming back out after going deep is a signal of overload or "wrong altitude"; repeated back-and-forth around one level suggests that level is miscalibrated for the content.
- **One-sentence user summary (teach-back), optionally prompted** — cheap, well-established as a comprehension check (self-explanation / generation effect literature), and gives Riemann a real signal instead of inferring from behavior alone; could be entirely optional/low-friction (a single text box, skippable).
- **Confidence rating alongside a summary or after finishing a level** — a 1-tap "how sure are you" check is a known calibration tool; large confidence/actual-performance gaps flag the "illusion of understanding from reading summaries" risk directly.
- **Response to an auto-generated prequestion** — right/wrong plus latency is both a comprehension signal and (per finding 9) itself improves learning, so it's a rare case of a "free" measurement that also helps.
- **Reread/re-expand of the same L2 section** — a strong implicit signal that section wasn't absorbed the first time, distinct from simple zoom navigation.

## Contradictions and caveats

- Bloom, SOLO, and Kintsch are **not measuring the same thing**, and conflating them is a real risk: Bloom is about what task a learner can *do*, SOLO is about the *structure* of an observed response, Kintsch is about the *mental representation* underlying comprehension. Riemann's L0–L3 currently reads as a Kintsch-style depth ladder, but if Sam wants Bloom-style capability targets (e.g., "I need to be able to apply this," "I need to critique this") the ladder would need a second, orthogonal axis — level of representation vs. task the user needs to perform with it.
- Advance-organizer meta-analyses are old (1983) and effect sizes are reported with wide variance depending on organizer type and study inclusion criteria — treat the 1.24 graphic-organizer number as suggestive, not a number to design a UI performance target around.
- Pretesting evidence is real but narrower than it first appears: works well for **factual** material, weaker/unclear for **conceptual** material — most of what a "large chunk of content" (an article, a paper, a spec) contains is a mix, so auto-generated prequestions need to specifically target checkable facts/claims, not "big picture" questions, or the technique may not transfer.
- Speed-reading and skimming findings come from controlled reading-of-prose experiments; they may transfer imperfectly to structured/technical content (code, specs, dense technical writing), which is a likely part of Riemann's real workload and wasn't directly covered by Rayner et al.
- All of this evidence is about *general* learners/readers, not adults with ADHD specifically — this report doesn't establish how attention-fluctuation interacts with e.g. the pretesting effect's attentional mechanism (it could matter more, or be harder to trigger reliably, for a distractible reader). Flagged as a gap below.
- Dunlosky's ratings are for **durable, long-term learning** (the classroom/exam use case) — Riemann's primary goal per the brief is often "understand *now*, in the shortest time," which is a different objective function. High-utility-for-retention techniques (spaced practice) are actively the *wrong* choice when the goal is fast one-off comprehension, and the report above already separates these, but it's worth stating explicitly: don't import Dunlosky's utility ratings wholesale as "what Riemann should always do" — they answer "what should I do if I want to remember this in a month," not "what's the fastest path to today's understanding."

## Gaps

- **ADHD-specific reading/comprehension literature**: this report used general reading-science and educational-psychology sources; a dedicated pass on ADHD and reading comprehension, working-memory load under fluctuating attention, and time-on-task decay curves would sharpen the "flexes with fluctuating attention" part of Sam's goal specifically (relevant to whichever worker is covering ADHD/attention — check 00-roster.md).
- **Illusion-of-understanding / metacognitive miscalibration research** (e.g., work by Lauren Leotti, or the broader "fluency = perceived understanding" literature, Bjork's "desirable difficulties") deserves its own deeper pass — this report touched confidence ratings as a signal but didn't dig into *why* summaries create false confidence, which is directly relevant to whether L0/L1 could be actively harmful (making people feel done when they aren't).
- **RSVP/Spritz-style rapid word presentation**: the brief asked for this but this pass only confirmed the general speed-reading debunking; a dedicated look at RSVP-specific studies (loss of ability to re-fixate/regress, which normal reading uses ~10-15% of the time for comprehension repair) would directly inform whether Riemann should ever offer a flashing-word mode.
- **Signposting/discourse-marker research in NLP-generated summaries**: whether LLM-generated section gists (L2) can reliably preserve the "signposting" that makes advance organizers effective (this report covered the human-learning literature on organizers, not whether automatically generated summaries retain those structural cues) — relevant to whoever is covering the abstraction-tree generation/NLP side.
- **Time-cost measurement of the techniques themselves**: Dunlosky et al. rates *learning* utility but not *time cost* per technique in a comparable unit; a targeted search for time-cost/efficiency (learning-per-minute) comparisons across retrieval practice, self-explanation, and rereading would let Riemann's budget-allocation model (proposed above) use real numbers instead of estimates.
