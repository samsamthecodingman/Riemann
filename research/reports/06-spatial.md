# 06 — Spatial representation of information: maps, networks, zoomable & spatial interfaces

## TL;DR
- The brain reuses hippocampal/entorhinal navigation machinery (place cells, grid cells) for *abstract, non-spatial* knowledge — this is now replicated fMRI evidence, not just a metaphor, and it's the strongest single justification for Riemann's "proximity = relatedness, depth = importance" spatial view.
- Method-of-loci training measurably restructures memory-network connectivity and roughly doubles free recall in weeks — but this is evidence for *deliberate, effortful* spatial encoding of arbitrary lists, not automatically for browsing UIs; it doesn't obviously transfer to passive reading.
- 2D vs 3D spatial document interfaces: spatial position helps recall (Data Mountain), but adding a third dimension purely for realism makes retrieval *slower*, not faster — depth/perspective must encode real structure, not decoration.
- Zoomable UIs (Pad++, space-scale diagrams) formalized zoom as continuous camera movement through a space-scale volume — this is close to a ready-made mathematical model for Riemann's "zoom dial."
- The overview+detail vs. zooming vs. focus+context literature (Cockburn, Karlson & Bederson 2008) is the single most load-bearing survey here: overview+detail is consistently favored for spatial memory and accuracy; pure zooming is favored for smooth, continuous transitions but costs orientation; focus+context is a wash and often confuses novices.
- "Desert fog" — losing your bearings after a scale change because the new view shares no visible landmarks with the old one — is a named, well-documented failure mode of zoomable interfaces, with known mitigations (home button, overview map, persistent landmarks, breadcrumbs).
- Force-directed graph/network views degrade into unreadable "hairballs" past a few hundred nodes; the fix in practice is filtering and local (ego-centered) views, not better global layout — this directly warns against a naive "show the whole abstraction graph" spatial view.
- Preserving the "mental map" (Misue et al. 1995) across layout updates — not the specific layout — is what lets users keep orientation in a changing graph; stability of relative position matters more than layout optimality.
- Kevin Lynch's five urban-legibility elements (paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks) map surprisingly cleanly onto a knowledge-space UI and give Riemann a concrete vocabulary for what its spatial view needs beyond just "dots and lines."
- Embedding-based semantic maps (UMAP/t-SNE "maps of documents," e.g. Nomic Atlas) are the most direct existing analogue of what an abstraction-tree spatial view could look like, but they inherit a serious caveat: 2D projections of high-dimensional similarity are locally meaningful and globally misleading (distances between distant clusters are close to arbitrary).

## Findings

### Neuroscience of spatial cognition for abstract knowledge

**Cognitive maps are a real, general-purpose neural coding scheme, not just a spatial-navigation quirk.** Tolman's original 1948 "cognitive maps in rats and men" argued rats build a comprehensive internal map rather than chained stimulus-response habits, based on shortcut/sunburst-maze behavior. Evidence strength: **MODERATE, with a live replication problem** — a 2024/2025 meta-analysis of the sunburst maze specifically ("Tolman's Sunburst Maze 80 Years on") found poor replicability and little consistent evidence for shortcutting across replications (PMC12766676, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12766676/). So the founding behavioral demonstration is shakier than its textbook status suggests; the modern case for cognitive maps rests much more on the neural recordings below than on Tolman's original behavioral paradigm.

**Grid-cell-like coding for entirely non-spatial, abstract 2D conceptual spaces.** Constantinescu, O'Reilly & Behrens (2016, *Science*, "Organizing conceptual knowledge in humans with a grid-like code," https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.aaf0941, PubMed 27313047) had participants learn to navigate a 2D space defined by two continuous, non-spatial dimensions (e.g., bird neck-length/leg-length), and found the same hexagonal, 60°-periodic grid-cell signal in entorhinal cortex and a related fronto-parietal network that is normally seen during physical spatial navigation. The gridlike signal replicated within-session and across a week. Evidence strength: **STRONG** (published in Science, independently replicated in follow-up studies, e.g. Nature Communications 2019 non-spatial account of grid/place cells, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-13760-8).

**"What is a cognitive map?" (Behrens et al. 2018, *Neuron* 100:490–509, https://www.cell.com/neuron/fulltext/S0896-6273(18)30856-0) is the field's synthesis paper.** It argues the hippocampal-entorhinal system implements a general-purpose relational/structural code — not literally spatial, but reused for spatial navigation as one special case among many (social hierarchies, task structure, abstract concept spaces). Grid cells are framed as doing vector computation (path integration / relational inference) over whatever space the animal is currently structuring. Follow-up work ("Map making: constructing, combining, and inferring on abstract cognitive maps," Neuron 2020, https://www.cell.com/neuron/fulltext/S0896-6273(20)30484-0) extends this to how separately learned maps get stitched together. Evidence strength: **STRONG** as a theoretical synthesis backed by multiple independent empirical results, though the *mechanistic* claims (vector-based inference, specific circuit models) are more MODERATE — still an active research area.

**Method of loci: robust behavioral effect, real underlying network change, but for deliberate memorization, not incidental browsing.** Dresler et al. (2017, *Neuron*, "Mnemonic training reshapes brain networks to support superior memory") compared 23 top memory athletes to matched controls and separately ran a 6-week method-of-loci training study in memory-naive people: memory athletes recalled 71/72 words after 20 minutes vs. ~40 for controls; trained novices roughly doubled recall (from ~30 to ~65 words) and shifted resting-state connectivity toward the athlete-like pattern, with effects still detectable 4 months later. Sources: QBI summary (https://qbi.uq.edu.au/blog/2017/06/mnemonics-help-memory-formation-and-make-lasting-brain-changes), ScienceDaily (https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/03/170308131236.htm), and a 2025 bioRxiv follow-up on the neural representations underlying loci training (https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.02.24.639840v1.full). Evidence strength: **STRONG for the behavioral effect and its durability, MODERATE for the specific network-connectivity mechanism** (fMRI connectivity claims are correlational and the sample of "grandmaster" athletes is small/self-selected). Important limitation: this is about *actively constructing* an imagined spatial walk to memorize an *arbitrary, unstructured* list — it says little about whether *passively viewing* content arranged in a 2D UI space produces the same benefit.

**Spatial memory for real UI layouts: positive but conditional, and 3D-for-its-own-sake backfires.** Data Mountain (Robertson et al. 1998, UIST, "Data Mountain: Using Spatial Memory for Document Management," https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/data-mountain-using-spatial-memory-for-document-management/) let users place web-page thumbnails at arbitrary positions on a tilted 3D plane and found retrieval was faster and better retained than a conventional favorites list, i.e., self-chosen spatial position is a usable retrieval cue. But Cockburn & McKenzie (2002, CHI, "Evaluating the Effectiveness of Spatial Memory in 2D and 3D Physical and Virtual Environments," https://www.csse.canterbury.ac.nz/andrew.cockburn/papers/chi02DM.pdf) directly compared physical, 2D, 2.5D and full 3D virtual layouts and found participants were *slower* in 3D than in 2D, and slowest of all in 3D virtual (vs. physical); a companion piece "Revisiting 2D vs 3D Implications on Spatial Memory" (https://www.csse.canterbury.ac.nz/andrew.cockburn/papers/auic-2d3d.pdf) reinforces that the added dimension doesn't pay for itself once you can't use vestibular/physical cues (leaning, walking) that ground 3D memory in the real world. Evidence strength: **MODERATE-STRONG**, consistent across the Cockburn line of work, but note these are 1998-2002-era studies (small screens, non-VR) — the VR-specific literature below partially updates this.

**VR/immersive learning: small-to-moderate positive effect, domain- and dose-dependent, weak on long-term transfer.** A 2022 meta-analysis (Ferrer-Torregrosa et al./cited via ScienceDirect S1747938X22000215, "The potential of immersive virtual reality to enhance learning") reports an overall effect of immersive VR on learning of roughly g≈0.38 vs. non-VR conditions, larger in K-12 (g≈0.61) than higher-ed (g≈0.31), largest in architecture (g≈1.90) and engineering (g≈0.79), and larger for full immersion than semi/non-immersive and for shorter (<2h) interventions; immediate vs. delayed testing showed similar gains, but the underlying literature it draws on skews heavily toward short-term outcomes, with few studies on long-term retention or far transfer. (I could not open the ScienceDirect full text directly — 403 — so this is via the search-engine-summarized abstract; treat the exact effect sizes as **secondhand, MODERATE confidence**, not independently verified against the primary PDF.) This is a caveat Riemann should weight only lightly, since a desktop/2D "spatial view" is not immersive VR and the effect may not transfer.

### Zoomable interfaces, focus+context, overview+detail

**Zoom has a clean mathematical model: the space-scale diagram.** Furnas & Bederson (1995, CHI, "Space-Scale Diagrams: Understanding Multiscale Interfaces," http://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/hcil/pad++/papers/chi-95-spacescale/chi-95-spacescale.pdf) stack copies of a 2D space at every possible magnification into a 3D "inverted pyramid" (x, y, scale); a pan+zoom trajectory is literally a path through this volume, and "keeping a point anchored while zooming" is a well-defined operation (a vertical line at fixed x,y, sweeping scale). This is directly applicable: Riemann's abstraction-tree levels (L0–L3) are a *discrete*, tree-structured special case of this continuous scale axis, and the "anchor the passage at viewport centre while zooming" requirement in the plan is exactly a space-scale-diagram query. Evidence strength: this is a formal/analytic framework, not an empirical claim — treat as **STRONG design tool**, not an evidence-tagged finding.

**Pad++ / zoomable user interfaces (ZUIs)** (Bederson, Hollan, Perlin et al. 1996, J. Visual Languages & Computing 7(1):3-32, https://worrydream.com/refs/Hollan_1995_-_Pad++.pdf) proposed zoom itself as the primary navigation primitive, replacing windows/icons with objects placed at any size/position in an infinite plane, with an "informational physics" for how objects should behave as you approach them (this is effectively "semantic zoom" avant la lettre: objects can change representation, not just size, as you zoom in). Evidence strength: **foundational systems paper**, influential but not a controlled study — the empirical payoff/cost of ZUIs was tested later (below).

**Generalized fisheye views / focus+context** (Furnas 1986, CHI, https://cspages.ucalgary.ca/~saul/581/exer.eps/4furnas86.pdf) formalizes "Degree of Interest" (DOI) as combining a priori importance with distance-from-focus, giving a principled way to show your immediate neighborhood in detail and distant structure only as landmarks — directly the "L0 gist visible everywhere, L3 detail only near focus" pattern. Furnas's 2006 follow-up "A Fisheye Follow-up" (http://vis-ucb-maneesh.stanford.edu/files/chi06/Furnas_p999.pdf) revisits 20 years of focus+context work and is more circumspect about how well it actually performs in practice.

**The decisive empirical comparison: Cockburn, Karlson & Bederson (2008), ACM Computing Surveys 41(1), "A Review of Overview+Detail, Zooming, and Focus+Context Interfaces"** (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2008/01/cockburn-ComputingSurveys09.pdf). This is the field's authoritative synthesis. Key conclusions:
- **Overview+detail** (spatially separate global-context and local-focus views, e.g. minimap + main view) reliably supports spatial orientation and lets users keep a stable mental map, at the cost of split attention between two views.
- **Zooming** (temporal separation — one view that changes over time) gives continuous, comprehensible transitions (good for the "one continuous dial" Riemann wants) but *costs* orientation/navigation efficiency — users get lost more, especially without landmarks.
- **Focus+context** (fisheye-style distortion, single view) minimizes seams but the distortion itself is often confusing, especially for novices, and empirical support for its superiority is mixed/weak.
- Cue-based techniques (highlighting) are a lighter-weight fourth category.
Evidence strength: **STRONG** — this is a peer-reviewed synthesis of dozens of empirical studies, the standard citation in the field (2226+ citations on the underlying fisheye paper alone per Semantic Scholar).

**"Desert fog"**: the named failure mode where a zoomed/panned view shares no recognizable content with the prior view, leaving the user with no cue to reconstruct where they are (https://www.mapuipatterns.com/desert-fog-problem/; also "Critical Zones in Desert Fog: Aids to Multiscale Navigation," https://www.researchgate.net/publication/2806120_Critical_Zones_in_Desert_Fog_Aids_to_Multiscale_Navigation; recent formal treatment "Where Am I Now? Modelling Disorientation in Pan-Scalar Maps," https://doi.org/10.3390/ijgi12020062). Documented mitigations: a **home button** (always-available reset to a known state), **overview map** (shows current viewport within the whole), **locate/recenter** affordance, **persistent landmarks/labels** that survive scale changes, and **placemarks/breadcrumbs**. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** — well-documented as a design pattern and named failure mode across multiple sources, but the *quantified* size of the effect is thinner than the OD/zoom/F+C survey above.

**Zooming presentations (Prezi) as a real-world test of continuous zoom for narrative content.** A study by Lichtschlag, Hess, Karrer & Borchers compared canvas/zooming presentations (Prezi-style) against slide decks for audience retention and macrostructure understanding, found them roughly equally effective at conveying content, but participants rated the canvas presentation as **better for staying oriented** during the talk — provided the presenter didn't over-zoom (design advice from practitioners converges on "arrive and dwell" rather than continuous motion, and warns that excessive zoom/pan literally induces motion-sickness-like discomfort in some viewers) (https://www.ned-potter.com/blog/how-to-stop-your-prezis-making-people-sick). Evidence strength: **MODERATE** (single study for the orientation claim; the motion-discomfort reports are anecdotal/practitioner-level, i.e. WEAK but consistent and physiologically plausible given vestibular/optic-flow literature).

### Network and graph visualization

**Preserving the mental map matters more than optimizing any single layout.** Misue, Eades, Lai & Sugiyama (1995, J. Visual Languages and Computing 6:183-210, "Layout Adjustment and the Mental Map") first formalized "mental map preservation" for dynamic graphs (preserving orthogonal ordering, proximity, and/or topology across redraws) as a goal separate from static layout aesthetics. This has since been empirically tested: "The 'Map' in the mental map: Experimental results in dynamic graph drawing" (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S107158191300102X) and "Mental Map Preservation Helps User Orientation in Dynamic Graphs" (https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-36763-2_42) provide direct behavioral evidence that users track updated graphs faster/more accurately when relative node positions are preserved across changes, even at some cost to the new layout's static aesthetic quality. Evidence strength: **MODERATE-STRONG** — foundational concept is near-universally cited, and there is direct experimental confirmation, though the exact trade-off curve (how much aesthetic quality to sacrifice for how much stability) is still debated in the graph-drawing literature.

**Force-directed layouts degrade into unreadable "hairballs."** This is both a well-known empirical/practitioner problem and confirmed in HCI literature on knowledge-graph tools ("Knowledge Graphs in Practice: Characterizing their Users, Challenges, and Visualization Opportunities," https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.01311) and vividly documented in the Obsidian community's own experience with its graph view: past a few hundred notes the global graph becomes "genuinely beautiful and makes for a great screenshot, but tells you almost nothing you did not already know" (https://www.eleanorkonik.com/p/its-not-just-a-pretty-gimmick-in-defense-of-obsidians-graph-view; forum discussion at https://forum.obsidian.md/t/whats-the-point-of-the-graph-view-how-are-you-using-it/71316). The convergent practitioner advice: **the global graph is a diagnostic you open occasionally to spot orphans/hubs, not a daily navigation tool; the useful view is a local/ego-centered graph (depth 1-2 from the current note) kept open while working, driven by filters** (https://danholloran.me/posts/making-obsidians-graph-view-actually-useful). Evidence strength: **MODERATE** — this is convergent practitioner/community consensus plus one HCI paper characterizing knowledge-graph tool users' actual struggles, not a controlled experiment, but it is highly consistent across independent sources and matches the graph-drawing literature's "hairball problem."

**Edge bundling and clustering reduce visual clutter but don't solve the underlying scaling problem.** Force-directed edge bundling (Holten & van Wijk, 2009, Eurographics/VGTC, https://classes.engineering.wustl.edu/cse557/readings/holten-edgebundling.pdf) groups edges with similar trajectories to reveal high-level flow structure; it helps at a given density but standard versions ignore edge direction/weight and can themselves obscure directional patterns (per newer critiques found via ResearchGate summaries). This is a rendering-layer mitigation, useful but secondary to reducing the number of visible nodes/edges in the first place (filtering, clustering, level-of-detail). Evidence strength: **MODERATE**.

### Cartography as a design vocabulary

**Cartographic generalization**: real maps at small scale don't just shrink — they *selectively* simplify, aggregate, displace, exaggerate and smooth (Wikipedia "Cartographic generalization," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartographic_generalization; MapTiler's practitioner guide, https://docs.maptiler.com/guides/maps-apis/maps-platform/generalization-in-maps/). This is a direct analogue for what "zooming out" in Riemann's abstraction tree should mean: not literally shrinking L3 text, but *substituting* a genuinely different, coarser representation (L2/L1/L0), exactly as a country-scale map substitutes a dot-and-label for a city rather than rendering tiny streets.

**Visual hierarchy**: "a map with a strong visual hierarchy carries an aesthetic of being clear because it appears at first glance to contain less data than it really does" — the corollary is that a map/UI with no hierarchy reads as cluttered even at moderate information density (ESRI's cartographic design principles, https://www.esri.com/arcgis-blog/products/arcgis-pro/mapping/design-principles-for-cartography; general treatment at https://thevisualcommunicationguy.com/2026/09/08/what-gis-maps-get-right-about-visual-hierarchy/). Five classic principles: legibility, visual contrast, figure-ground, hierarchical organization, balance.

**Kevin Lynch, *The Image of the City* (1960)**: legible environments are built from five element types — **paths** (routes of movement), **edges** (boundaries), **districts** (areas with common character), **nodes** (junctions/focal points), and **landmarks** (external point references) — and "imageability" is the property of an environment that makes it easy to form a strong, well-structured mental image of it (https://www.kaarwan.com/blog/architecture/kevin-lynchs-5-elements-that-shape-cities-urban-design). This vocabulary transfers well to an abstraction-tree spatial view: paths = zoom/pan trajectories or breadcrumb trails, edges = boundaries between topic clusters, districts = topic/document clusters, nodes = summary junctions (an L1/L2 gist that many things link through), landmarks = the fixed "home anchor" plus any recurring, visually distinct high-level gists a user comes to recognize by shape/color, not just by reading the label. Evidence strength for Lynch: **foundational urban-design theory, widely applied outside its origin domain (information visualization, VR, games) but the "does it work for abstract knowledge spaces" transfer is largely assumed by analogy rather than separately tested** — flag as an assumption, not a finding.

### Semantic/embedding maps as a concrete existing analogue

**Embedding-based "maps of documents"** (UMAP/t-SNE dimensionality reduction of text embeddings into 2D, rendered as an explorable map — e.g. Nomic Atlas, https://docs.nomic.ai/atlas/embeddings-and-retrieval/guides/how-to-visualize-embeddings, and the related "Embedding Atlas" system, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.06386) are the closest existing production analogue to what an abstraction-tree spatial view for Riemann might look like: cluster proximity ≈ topical relatedness, pan/zoom ≈ exploring the corpus, hover/search ≈ landmarks. Important, well-known caveat from the dimensionality-reduction literature generally (not specific to Nomic): **UMAP/t-SNE preserve local neighborhood structure much better than global distances** — two clusters that look close on the 2D map are not reliably "more related" than two that look far apart; only within-cluster proximity is trustworthy. This is a critical design caveat if Riemann ever computes node positions from embedding similarity rather than from the tree structure itself: a *literal* embedding map would systematically mislead users about macro-relatedness even though it looks authoritative. Evidence strength: **STRONG as a known limitation of the underlying math** (this is textbook UMAP/t-SNE behavior, not a contested finding), **MODERATE** for how much it matters in practice for Riemann specifically, since Riemann's tree is not proposed to be laid out by raw embedding projection.

## Design implications for Riemann

1. **Model the zoom dial as a literal space-scale diagram, not four disconnected screens.** Treat L0-L3 as four horizontal slices of one continuous (x, y, scale) volume (Furnas & Bederson). The "anchor the passage at viewport centre" requirement in 00-plan.md is precisely "hold x,y fixed, sweep scale" — this gives a principled algorithm (not just an ad hoc scroll-position hack) for computing where to scroll to after a level change: project the currently-centered node's `source_span` through the tree's parent/child links, which are literally the diagram's vertical fibers.

2. **Default to overview+detail, not pure zooming, once the spatial view exists — because the survey evidence says zooming alone costs orientation.** For the plain-markdown v1 this is moot, but when the spatial view (canvas) is built, keep a persistent small overview (even just "you are here" dot on a mini abstraction-tree map) alongside the main zoomed view, rather than relying on the zoom trajectory itself to convey position. This directly operationalizes Cockburn/Karlson/Bederson's strongest empirical conclusion.

3. **Guard against desert fog explicitly at every level transition.** Concretely: (a) a fixed, always-reachable **home** control (matches Sam's existing "one fixed home anchor" principle — this literature independently arrives at the same design pattern from a totally different problem, which is a good cross-check); (b) at least one visually stable element that survives every zoom level — e.g., the L0 gist rendered as a persistent header/breadcrumb even while viewing L3, so the user never loses the "what is this document, in one line" anchor; (c) breadcrumbs recording the zoom path, not just current depth.

4. **Do not build a literal force-directed "graph of everything" as the primary spatial view.** The hairball evidence (Obsidian community, knowledge-graph HCI paper) says a global all-nodes graph is a diagnostic at best, useless at worst, past a few hundred items. If/when the spatial view shows relationships between multiple documents' trees, default to a **local/ego view** (the current document plus its immediate neighbors) with the global view as an occasional, filterable overview — not the main interaction surface.

5. **When the spatial view updates (new content added, tree regenerated), preserve relative position, don't re-optimize layout from scratch.** Apply Misue et al.'s mental-map-preservation principle: if node B was northeast of node A last session, keep it northeast even if a fresh layout algorithm would put it somewhere "more optimal." This is a concrete, testable engineering constraint on whatever layout function eventually positions tree/graph nodes.

6. **Use "depth = importance" (already a stated principle) but implement it as cartographic generalization, not just scaling.** Zooming out shouldn't shrink L3 text — it should *substitute* L2/L1/L0 content, exactly as a map substitutes symbols for detail rather than rendering tiny buildings. This is already what the abstraction tree does; the cartography literature just confirms it's the right mental model and gives vocabulary (selection, simplification, aggregation) for how to design L2 gists (aggregate several L3 leaves' content, don't just truncate).

7. **If node position in the spatial view is ever derived from embeddings, do not let 2D distance imply relatedness across clusters.** Either (a) keep node layout driven by the tree's own parent/child structure (which has honest, interpretable semantics) rather than a raw embedding projection, or (b) if embeddings are used for cross-document layout, visually distinguish "same local cluster" (trustworthy proximity) from "different cluster" (untrustworthy relative distance) — e.g., discrete districts/boundaries (Lynch) rather than one continuous scatter, so users don't over-read macro-distance.

8. **Borrow Lynch's five elements as a literal design checklist for the future spatial view**: does every screen have a path (how did I get here / where can I go), at least one edge (a legible boundary between this topic-cluster and the next), districts (visually distinct regions per document/topic), nodes (junctions — e.g. an L1 summary that many L2 gists roll up into), and landmarks (something recognizably-shaped, not just text, that the user can rely on for "I've been here before")?

9. **Treat "spatial = better" as conditional, not automatic.** Cockburn & McKenzie's 3D result is a direct warning against adding depth/perspective for aesthetic reasons alone — only add a dimension (including the "depth = importance" z-axis) if it encodes real, usable structure, and test retrieval speed, not just subjective preference, before assuming it helps.

10. **For zoom transitions/animation, pace matters as much as geometry.** The Prezi evidence (equal comprehension, better orientation, but only with restrained pacing; discomfort with excessive zoom/pan) suggests the dial's *animated* transitions (if any) should be slow enough to update the user's mental model, and should be interruptible/skippable — continuous zoom is a feature for orientation, not for show.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Anchor-drift error**: after a level change, distance (in scroll position or source-span offset) between where the anchoring algorithm predicted the centered passage would land and where the user immediately scrolls to correct it — a direct, low-friction proxy for "did the zoom keep me oriented."
- **Home-button/reset usage rate**: frequency of returning to a known state is a classic disorientation proxy (borrowed from desert-fog mitigation literature) — rising use suggests the current view isn't self-orienting enough.
- **Re-visit latency**: time to re-find a previously-viewed node/passage in the tree on a second visit — if spatial/structural position is doing real memory work (Data Mountain's core claim), this should drop faster over repeated sessions than a flat search baseline would predict.
- **Dwell time per zoom level vs. transition frequency**: rapid back-and-forth zooming (many small dial movements) may indicate the current level isn't matching the desired granularity — a level-seeking signal distinguishable from settled reading.
- **Breadcrumb/back usage**: how often users retrace steps rather than zoom directly to a target — a proxy for whether direct navigation ("go straight to L0 of section 3") is legible enough, or whether they need to zoom out then back in every time (a desert-fog-adjacent signal).
- **Scroll-position variance across sessions on the same document**: if a user reliably returns to roughly the same passage/level without searching, that's evidence spatial/structural memory is forming (echoes method-of-loci's core mechanism, adapted to a passive-reading context).

## Contradictions and caveats

- **Tolman's foundational behavioral evidence for cognitive maps has a real replication problem** (PMC12766676) even though the neural evidence built on top of the concept (Constantinescu, Behrens) is strong. Don't cite "rats prove cognitive maps exist" as settled; cite the grid-cell fMRI work instead, which stands on its own.
- **Method-of-loci evidence is about active, effortful spatial *construction* for arbitrary list memorization** — it is not evidence that *passively browsing* a pre-built spatial layout gives the same memory boost. Riemann's spatial view is closer to Data Mountain (self-placed or system-placed persistent layout for documents) than to loci training (imagined walk through a constructed palace); these are related but distinct mechanisms and shouldn't be conflated in the product narrative.
- **3D and immersive VR results are highly context-dependent and partly undercut a naive reading of "spatial is always better."** Cockburn & McKenzie found 3D slower than 2D for simple retrieval; the VR meta-analysis shows real but modest effects (g≈0.38) concentrated in specific domains (architecture, engineering, K-12) and short interventions, with weak long-term-retention evidence. A 2D-with-depth-cues desktop view (which is what Riemann's spatial view will likely be, not headset VR) is not directly covered by either literature — treat both as suggestive, not decisive.
- **The Cockburn/Karlson/Bederson survey is from 2008.** It predates modern multitouch, pinch-zoom-native mobile interaction, and infinite-canvas tools (Muse, Heptabase, Kinopio, tldraw, Figma/FigJam-style canvases) that have since accumulated a lot of practitioner experience but comparatively little rigorous HCI evaluation — I could not find controlled comparative studies of these newer tools specifically (see Gaps).
- **The Obsidian graph-view "hairball" evidence is community/practitioner consensus, not a controlled study** — it's consistent and convergent across many independent sources, which raises confidence, but it's not peer-reviewed experimental data.
- **WebFetch on several primary sources (Behrens Neuron full text, Constantinescu Science abstract, ScienceDirect VR meta-analysis) returned HTTP 403** — those claims above are sourced from search-engine-generated summaries of the abstract/landing pages, not a direct read of the full text. I've flagged confidence accordingly; a follow-up worker with access to a paywall-bypassing fetch (e.g. institutional proxy, Sci-Hub-adjacent tooling if permitted, or the PDF mirrors I did find for some) could firm these up.
- **Lynch's five-element framework and the cartography-generalization analogy are theoretical transfers, not empirically tested for abstract information spaces.** They're widely reused by analogy in infovis/games/VR (worth citing as "practitioner-validated" at most), but I didn't find a controlled study testing whether Lynch's elements measurably improve navigability of a non-geographic knowledge space specifically.
