# 07 — Adaptive signals: what to read, and how software can read it

## TL;DR
- Confusion is *good* for learning (it signals productive cognitive disequilibrium); boredom and frustration are *bad* — this is the single most load-bearing finding for any system trying to react to a user's state (D'Mello & Graesser).
- The strongest, most replicated lever in all of teaching research isn't detecting state at all — it's **frequent, low-stakes feedback loops that close quickly** (Black & Wiliam's formative-assessment effect size of 0.4–0.7, among the largest in education research).
- Human one-to-one tutoring beats classroom instruction by ~2 standard deviations (Bloom's 2-sigma problem), but VanLehn's 2011 meta-analysis found well-designed step-based ITS get most of the way there (~0.76 vs 0.79 for human tutors) — the gap that matters most is *granularity of interaction*, not humanness.
- A 2025 Harvard RCT (Kestin et al., *Scientific Reports*) found an AI tutor produced roughly double the learning gains of active-learning classroom instruction, in less time, with higher engagement — the strongest causal evidence yet that adaptive, interactive software tutoring works, at least for STEM problem sets.
- The cheapest, most privacy-respecting, and honestly most robust signal for a single-user local app is **interaction-log behavior** (dwell time, re-reading, idle gaps, scroll/zoom patterns) — not webcam/physiological sensing, which is higher-fidelity but costlier and creepier for a personal tool.
- Open learner models (Bull & Kay) — showing the user their own model of themselves — improve trust, self-regulation, and error-correction of the model, and map directly onto Riemann's "show your abstraction tree" ethos.
- Contextual bandits are the right *class* of algorithm for picking presentation variants per user (which zoom level, which modality) under a noisy, low-N, single-user regime, but need deliberate cold-start priors and decay so yesterday's bad mood doesn't get baked in as a permanent user preference.
- Learner control research is a genuine cautionary tale: giving users the wheel has an average effect that is roughly zero to slightly negative in meta-analyses — control helps confident/skilled learners and actively hurts novices navigating without guidance. This directly challenges "let the user always drive the zoom dial" as an unqualified good.
- Metacognitive calibration is generally poor (people are overconfident, especially before they've been tested), so self-report ("how well do you understand this?") is a weaker signal than it sounds, and should be triangulated with behavior, not trusted alone.

## Findings

### Human teaching signals

**Formative assessment / responsive teaching.** Black & Wiliam's 1998 review ("Inside the Black Box," *Phi Delta Kappan*) synthesized ~250 studies and found formative-assessment interventions raised achievement with effect sizes of 0.4–0.7, disproportionately helping lower performers. — STRONG (large, frequently-replicated synthesis, though the original review has been critiqued for effect-size heterogeneity across included studies).
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/003172171009200119 , https://fairtest.org/value-formative-assessment-pdf/

**Zone of proximal development, scaffolding, fading.** Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976, "The Role of Tutoring in Problem Solving") define scaffolding as the tutor controlling task elements beyond the learner's current capacity so the learner can focus only on what's in reach, via six functions: recruitment, reducing degrees of freedom, direction maintenance, marking critical features, frustration control, demonstration. Van de Pol et al. (2010) later distilled this into three empirically-tracked properties: **contingency** (responsiveness to the learner's current state), **fading** (withdrawal of support as competence grows), and **transfer of responsibility**. — STRONG as a foundational construct, though the original study (n=30, ages 3–5, block-building task) is small and the construct has been stretched far beyond its original scope in 50 years of reuse.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/932126/ , https://laurenmarg.com/2018/12/09/article-summary-wood-et-al-1976-role-of-tutoring-in-problem-solving/

**Contingent tutoring in computational form.** "Contingency" as an algorithmic principle (give the minimum help that unblocks the learner, calibrated to their last response, not a fixed sequence) is what separates good ITS help systems from static hint sequences — this is a direct throughline from Wood's tutoring observations to modern ITS help-graph design. (Same sources as above.)

**Expert human tutor behavior (Lepper; Graesser).** Lepper, Woolverton, Mumme & Gurtner (1993, "Motivational Techniques of Expert Human Tutors") found expert tutors spend as much effort on motivation and emotional support as on content — captured in their INSPIRE framework (Intelligent, Nurturant, Socratic, Progressive, Indirect, Reflective, Encouraging). Expert tutors favor indirect correction, historical/real-world framing, and are deliberately Socratic — drawing answers out rather than imposing them. — MODERATE (influential, widely cited, but based on observational/qualitative tutoring-session analysis rather than a controlled trial).
https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Motivational-techniques-of-expert-human-tutors%3A-for-Lepper-Woolverton/f7741af65bad659574280a61e540c4d19093b445

**What signs good teachers/tutors read.** Across the D'Mello/Graesser body of work (see below) and the AutoTutor line of research, the behavioral tells that correlate with learning-relevant states include: hesitation before answering, self-correction attempts, question-asking (especially "why" questions signal engagement, not confusion-avoidance), gross body movement and facial action units (brow furrow = confusion, gaze aversion = boredom), and response latency patterns. — MODERATE, drawn from lab studies with instrumented sensing, not naturalistic classroom observation at scale.

### Intelligent tutoring systems

**Bloom's 2-sigma problem.** Bloom (1984, *Educational Researcher*) reports 1:1 human tutoring under mastery-learning conditions moved the average tutored student to the 98th percentile of a conventionally-taught control group — a ~2 SD effect, "roughly two whole grades." He explicitly frames this as the challenge: find methods of group instruction as effective as 1:1 tutoring, because 1:1 doesn't scale economically. — STRONG as a foundational, oft-cited result, though modern re-analyses (e.g., the Education Next "Two-Sigma Tutoring" piece) argue the *original* 2-sigma figure is inflated relative to what real-world tutoring programs (not the small idealized Anania/Burke dissertation studies Bloom cites) actually achieve — more realistic estimates for good tutoring programs cluster around 0.3–0.4 SD. Treat "2 sigma" as an upper-bound aspiration, not an expected effect.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom%27s_2_sigma_problem , https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/1984-bloom.pdf , https://www.educationnext.org/two-sigma-tutoring-separating-science-fiction-from-science-fact/

**VanLehn 2011 meta-analysis.** VanLehn ("The Relative Effectiveness of Human Tutoring, Intelligent Tutoring Systems, and Other Tutoring Systems," *Educational Psychologist* 46(4)) reanalyzed decades of studies (1975–2010) and found: no-tutoring baseline vs. human tutoring effect size ≈ 0.79 (much lower than Bloom's 2.0); step-based ITS (systems that give feedback at each reasoning step, not just the final answer) reached ≈ 0.76 — nearly matching human tutors; answer-based/substep-based systems performed markedly worse (≈0.40). The key variable isn't "AI vs human," it's **interaction granularity** — systems that can see and respond to intermediate steps do almost as well as a human watching over your shoulder. — STRONG (widely-cited meta-analysis, though built from a modest and heterogeneous set of ~10 comparisons/28 studies, so confidence intervals are wide).
https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369

**Knowledge tracing.** Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) models each skill as a binary latent variable updated via Bayesian inference from correct/incorrect responses — interpretable, works with little data, but assumes independent skills. Deep Knowledge Tracing (Piech et al., Stanford) replaces this with an RNN over response sequences, often more accurate but far less interpretable and data-hungry — a bad fit for a single user with a thin history. — MODERATE/STRONG depending on which claim: BKT's interpretability tradeoff is well established; DKT's superiority is contested in later replications (some show BKT-family models close the gap when properly tuned).
https://stanford.edu/~cpiech/bio/papers/deepKnowledgeTracing.pdf

**Spaced repetition / forgetting models (relevant to Riemann only if content is revisited).** FSRS (used in Anki since v4) fits a per-user forgetting curve and reportedly beats the classic SM-2 algorithm on prediction accuracy for ~99.6% of users in benchmark comparisons; it explicitly personalizes decay-curve shape per user rather than using one fixed formula. — MODERATE (benchmark from the algorithm's own open-source community, not an independent peer-reviewed trial, but methodologically transparent and reproducible).
https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark , https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html

**Mastery learning.** The instructional design paired with Bloom's tutoring comparison — periodic testing, corrective feedback, retesting to an 80%+ threshold before advancing — is the "mastery learning" condition that (along with tutoring) outperformed conventional instruction in the underlying Anania/Burke studies. — STRONG as a construct, contested in magnitude for the reasons above.

**Adaptive hypermedia (Brusilovsky).** Brusilovsky's 1996/2001 reviews formalize two adaptation axes: **adaptive presentation** (vary content — conditional text, variant fragments/pages depending on user model) and **adaptive navigation support** (vary the link structure — hiding, annotating, or sorting links by relevance/readiness, e.g. graying out links to material whose prerequisites aren't yet met). This is close kin to Riemann's zoom-level tree: rather than one fixed document, the system holds a superset of content and a policy for which slice to surface. — STRONG as an influential taxonomy (widely reused for 25+ years), though "adaptive" in most of these systems means rule-based on an explicit, hand-authored user/domain model, not learned from behavior.
https://sites.pitt.edu/~peterb/papers/UMUAI96.pdf

**Open learner models (Bull & Kay).** The SMILI:) framework (Bull & Kay, 2007, *IJAIED*) classifies open learner models along how much of the model is shown, and how much control the user has over it: purely *scrutable* (view only), *cooperative/negotiable* (discuss/adjust together), fully *editable*. Two purposes are named: pedagogical (prompts self-assessment and reflection) and technical (lets the user correct a wrong model, improving system accuracy). — MODERATE/STRONG as a taxonomy; empirical learning-outcome evidence for OLMs specifically is thinner than for formative assessment broadly, but the design framework itself is the standard reference in the field.
https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/Open_learner_model , SMILI:) framework, IJAIED 17(2):89-120

**LLM tutors — recent evidence.**
- Kestin, Miller et al., "AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning" (*Scientific Reports*, June 2025): a crossover RCT with 194 Harvard undergrad physics students comparing an in-class active-learning session vs. a custom AI tutor ("PS2 Pal") on the same content. AI-tutored students showed roughly double the learning gains, in less time, with higher self-reported engagement and motivation; effect sizes reported at 0.73–1.3 SD. — STRONG for a single study: proper RCT with crossover design controlling for student ability, published in a peer-reviewed journal, but it's one course, one tutor system tightly authored by the instructor, and a short intervention (single-topic sessions) — generalization to open-domain, long-horizon tutoring is unproven.
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6 , https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2024/09/professor-tailored-ai-tutor-to-physics-course-engagement-doubled/
- Google LearnLM (Dec 2024, arXiv:2412.16429): reframes "pedagogy" as *instruction-following* — the model is trained/evaluated on whether it follows explicit pedagogical instructions (Socratic questioning, withholding the answer, adjusting difficulty) rather than having one baked-in teaching style. In blind expert evaluation (248 pedagogy experts, ~10k assessments across 49 scenarios), LearnLM was preferred over GPT-4o (+31%), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (+11%), and base Gemini 1.5 Pro (+13%). — MODERATE (industry paper, evaluators are pedagogy experts rating transcripts, not a learning-outcome RCT — it measures "is this good pedagogy" not "did people learn more").
https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16429

### Affect & engagement detection

**D'Mello & Graesser — confusion, frustration, boredom, flow.** Across multiple studies with AutoTutor, confusion was positively correlated with learning outcomes; frustration and boredom were negatively correlated. The theorized mechanism ("confusion → cognitive disequilibrium → effortful reasoning → resolution → learning," sometimes framed via the affective-dynamics / flow models) suggests systems should *tolerate or even induce mild confusion* but actively intervene on boredom and frustration before they set in. Affect is sensed via conversational cues, gross body movement, and facial features (later work added posture sensors, EDA). — STRONG for the confusion-learning correlation specifically (replicated across several D'Mello/Graesser studies and independently cited widely); the causal "confusion → learning" direction is more MODERATE — correlational data dominates, with some contradiction-induction experiments providing causal-ish evidence.
https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/dmello-arl14-affect.pdf , https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1190004.pdf

**Mind-wandering detection from reading behavior / eye-tracking.** Gaze-based detectors (fixation duration, dispersion, blink rate, pupil diameter) predict mind-wandering during computerized reading with reported accuracy ~67-72% (page-end vs. mid-page probes) in Hutt et al.'s line of work; combining eye-tracking with electrodermal activity (EDA) pushed F1 to 0.83 in one multimodal study. Real-time interventions (inserting a comprehension check when the detector fires) measurably reduced further mind-wandering and improved comprehension in at least one study ("Zone Out No More"). — MODERATE: real effects, but detector accuracy in the 67-83% range means meaningful false-positive/false-negative rates, and all of this requires eye-tracking hardware most single-user local apps won't have.
https://visualcognition.nd.edu/assets/424949/hutt_chi21.pdf , https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7248717/ , https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED596617.pdf

**Interaction-log proxies (dwell time, idle, disengagement).** Cocea & Weibelzahl's log-file-analysis line of work models disengagement from raw behavioral logs alone — reading time on pages, quiz-taking behavior — and explicitly note this data is privacy-friendly ("log data do not include direct visual or biometric student data ... don't require any additional equipment"). Baker et al.'s "Disengagement Detection in Online Learning" (IEEE TLT, 2011) formalizes categories like gaming-the-system, wheel-spinning (unproductive persistence on a task without progress), off-task behavior, and defines "idle disengagement" operationally as no interaction for ≥2 minutes. — STRONG as an established, low-cost detection paradigm; the specific accuracy numbers are dataset-specific (mostly K-12 ITS logs) and may not transfer directly to a reading/abstraction tool.
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11257-009-9065-5 , https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1109/TLT.2010.14

**Webcam/physiological sensing.** Exists (facial action units, posture, EDA, even fNIRS in lab settings) and gives richer, more real-time affect signal than logs alone, but at a steep privacy and creepiness cost for a personal always-on assistant, plus hardware/compute overhead. Given Riemann's principle of minimal-friction, zero-to-useful setup, this category is probably out of scope except as a possible opt-in later. (Design judgment, not a separate citation — synthesized from the D'Mello/Graesser multimodal papers above, which all use it in controlled lab conditions, not deployed products.)

**Self-report micro-probes / experience sampling.** Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) captures in-the-moment self-report, avoiding retrospective recall bias. Microinteraction EMA (μEMA) pushes this further: single-question, 1-tap prompts answerable via a glanceable UI (originally demonstrated on smartwatches), which increases response rate and lowers burden versus multi-item surveys. — MODERATE/STRONG: EMA methodology itself is well-established in psychology; μEMA's specific low-burden framing is newer (2020-2022 papers) but directly transferable to a 1-tap "was this helpful?" affordance in Riemann.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8867293/ , https://formative.jmir.org/2022/2/e32772

### Combining signals, picking variants, avoiding overfit

**Explicit + implicit feedback fusion.** Standard recommender-systems wisdom: explicit feedback (thumbs up/down, "not helpful") is a clean, low-noise but *sparse* signal (users rarely bother); implicit feedback (dwell time, scroll, re-read, abandonment) is abundant but noisy and ambiguous (a click isn't necessarily interest). Practical systems weight explicit feedback more heavily per-observation but rely on implicit signal for volume, and use explicit feedback to calibrate/validate the implicit model. — STRONG as general recommender-systems practice, though the education-specific literature applying this exact framing to tutoring is thin — mostly generalized from e-commerce/media recommendation research.
https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.12770

**Contextual bandits for presentation-variant selection.** Multiple papers (Lan & Baraniuk's "A Contextual Bandits Framework for Personalized Learning Action Selection"; a Springer 2022 paper on adaptive-curriculum bandits raising completion rates in an RCT) frame "which content/format to show next" as a contextual-bandit problem: context = user + task state, arms = candidate presentations, reward = a proxy like completion/correctness/engagement. Bandits are explicitly favored over full RL for this because they handle the cold-start / sparse-reward / non-stationary regime better and are far more sample-efficient — relevant since Riemann has exactly one user and no historical corpus. — MODERATE: education-specific RCT evidence (the Springer paper) shows real gains, but n of published studies applying bandits specifically to single-user personalization (vs. platform-scale multi-user learning) is small.
https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Contextual-Bandits-Framework-for-Personalized-Lan-Baraniuk/a19e4e14c424597df1d11f2cf99d09452b3da25b , https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-11644-5_74

**Cold start & noisy-day overfitting.** Not a single citation but a structural point that follows directly from the bandit and knowledge-tracing literature above: with one user and thin history, a naive bandit or knowledge-tracer will either (a) do nothing useful for a long time (cold start) or (b) overfit hard to a single bad/atypical session (e.g. a low-energy ADHD day) and wrongly "learn" that the user always wants the shallowest level. Mitigations used in the literature: informative priors seeded from population data or explicit onboarding questions (solves cold start), decayed/windowed reward averaging or hierarchical Bayesian pooling across sessions (dampens single-day overfit), and treating "state today" (fluctuating) as a separate variable from "preference" (slower-moving) — which matches Riemann's own design principle of separating load capacity/emotional state from other signals.

### Metacognition & learner control

**Calibration / judgments of learning.** People are systematically overconfident about what they understand, especially before being tested — poor calibration correlates with weaker self-regulation (overconfident learners disengage from review/reflection because they believe they don't need it). Calibration *can* be trained (a CBE–Life Sciences Education study found 2 weeks of structured self-evaluation opportunities improved calibration accuracy). — STRONG for the general overconfidence finding (large, old, well-replicated literature in cognitive psychology); MODERATE for the specific training-intervention result (single study, non-blinded classroom design).
https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.18-10-0202

**Learner control — when it helps vs. hurts.** This is the most important corrective to naive "just let the user pick their zoom level" thinking. Niemiec, Sikorski & Walberg's earlier review-of-reviews and later meta-analyses (Karich, Burns & Maki 2014, updated meta-analysis in *Review of Educational Research*) converge on: the *average* effect of learner control is close to zero, sometimes slightly *negative*. It's not that control never helps — it interacts strongly with learner characteristics: **control helps more skilled/confident learners** (who can navigate wisely) and **hurts less able or younger learners**, who tend to under-challenge themselves or get lost without guidance. Effects also vary by content type — better for skill training than for knowledge acquisition, and stronger in some subject domains than others. — STRONG (this is a genuinely well-replicated, "surprising" null/negative result across three generations of meta-analysis, 1996 → 2014 → 2016).
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2190/JV1U-EQ5P-X2PB-PDBA , https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654314526064 , https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-016-9452-y

**Transparency and agency in adaptive systems.** The open-learner-model literature (Bull & Kay, above) is the direct evidence base here: letting users see and negotiate with the system's model of them is generally positive for trust and self-regulation, and is a middle path between "system decides everything silently" (which the learner-control research suggests may actually be *better* for raw performance in novices) and "user has full manual control" (which the same research shows underperforms for novices). The synthesis across both literatures: **the system should default to steering (especially early / for a novice user or novice topic), but always show its reasoning and offer an easy override** — auto-pilot with a visible, correctable model, not a locked cockpit.

## Design implications for Riemann

1. **Default to system-chosen zoom level, but always let the user grab the dial.** The learner-control literature says unqualified "user always drives" is not obviously good, especially when Riemann doesn't yet know the user's familiarity with the material. Concretely: on first open of a new document, Riemann should open at a level it estimates as appropriate (e.g., L1 by default per the plan) and treat any manual dial movement as a strong feedback signal, not the system's decision, ever being wrong to require.
2. **Show the reasoning, not just the output — this is Riemann's built-in open learner model.** Since the abstraction tree already has `source_span` and level metadata, exposing "why you're seeing this" (e.g., a subtle affordance like "this section expanded because you paused here twice") turns the zoom dial into a scrutable/cooperative OLM per SMILI:), which the literature ties to trust and better self-correction, essentially for free given the existing data model.
3. **Treat confusion signals as "give more, don't back off" and boredom/frustration signals as "the actual trigger to intervene."** Concretely: repeated re-reads or dwell-time spikes on a *specific* passage (candidate confusion) should surface a deeper L2/L3 excerpt or an inline clarifying gist — not simplify further. But long idle gaps, rapid dial-flicking, or repeated closing without re-opening (candidate boredom/frustration) should trigger the opposite: offer to jump straight to L0/L1 or suggest stopping. These need different UI reactions even though both look like "user is struggling" on the surface.
4. **Build the presentation-variant chooser as a contextual bandit from day one, not a fixed heuristic.** Arms = {default level, whether to pre-expand a flagged section, which section order to surface first}; context = document features + recent session behavior; reward = a cheap proxy (did the user keep reading past this point / did they manually zoom back in / explicit thumbs). Seed with a sane rule-based prior (word-count-based L1 target, as the plan already does) so the bandit only nudges around a reasonable baseline rather than learning from nothing.
5. **Use windowed/decayed reward, and separate "state today" from "preference."** Store two different rolling estimates: a fast-moving one for the current session/day (maps to Riemann's existing "load capacity, fluctuating") and a slow-moving one for stable content-type or topic preferences. Never let one bad ADHD-low day permanently bias the model toward "this user always wants L0."
6. **The cheap, low-friction signal stack for a local single-user app, ranked by cost/privacy tradeoff:** (a) interaction logs — dwell time per node, scroll velocity/regressions, re-visits to a node id, idle gaps, dial-manipulation patterns — free, already implied by the existing `data-node-id` tagging in the plan; (b) 1-tap micro-probes at natural transition points (e.g., a single "still going / lost me" tap when leaving a document) — cheap, μEMA-style, low burden; (c) explicit thumbs on a level/section — sparse but high-value, use to calibrate (a); (d) eye-tracking/webcam affect — explicitly deprioritize for v1+, revisit only as an opt-in later given accuracy (67-83%) doesn't yet justify the privacy cost for a personal tool.
7. **Knowledge tracing is probably premature for v1** (no repeated exposure to the same facts yet — Riemann ingests documents, it doesn't yet quiz or space-repeat), but the *architecture* is worth pre-wiring: if Riemann ever adds quizzing/review, a lightweight BKT-style per-topic mastery estimate (not DKT — too data-hungry for one user) fits the interpretability and low-data needs far better.
8. **Borrow contingent tutoring's fading principle for the abstraction tree itself.** Early in a session with a new/unfamiliar document, offer more scaffolding (auto-expand key L2 gists, surface open-learner-model hints). As the user demonstrates command of a topic (repeated fast, confident navigation, no re-reads), fade this — show less commentary, trust them at L2/L3 by default. This operationalizes Wood's "reduction in degrees of freedom" and "fading" directly.

## Signals Riemann could measure

**Implicit, low-friction, already-feasible from the existing architecture:**
- Dwell time per node (`data-node-id`) — proxy for engagement or confusion, ambiguous without a second signal.
- Re-visit count to the same node within a session — strong confusion/interest proxy.
- Dial velocity and direction changes (fast back-and-forth = searching/uncertain; slow steady descent = confident deep-reading).
- Idle gaps (no scroll/keypress for N seconds) — disengagement proxy, per Baker et al.'s ≥2-minute idle threshold as a starting default.
- Scroll regressions (scrolling back up) — re-reading proxy, cheap to compute from existing scroll events.
- Session abandonment point (where in the tree did they stop, and did they come back) — longitudinal engagement signal.
- Time-of-day / session-length patterns — correlate with the fluctuating load-capacity signal Riemann already models (feeds worker 08's territory).

**Explicit, low-friction:**
- Single-tap "this level is right / too shallow / too deep" at any point (μEMA-style, not a full survey).
- A binary "helpful / not helpful" per generated summary node — sparse but high-signal, good for calibrating the implicit model per the recommender-systems fusion pattern.
- Optional, never required: a one-tap mood/energy check-in at session start (ties into worker 08's fluctuation signals, not this file's core scope, but the *mechanism* — μEMA — is the same).

**Deliberately out of scope for v1 / needs more justification before building:**
- Webcam/facial affect detection — real signal exists (D'Mello/Graesser) but accuracy (moderate) and privacy cost are both worse than the log-based alternative for a personal always-on tool.
- Eye-tracking — best mind-wandering detector in the literature, but requires hardware nobody has for a local web app.
- Physiological (EDA, heart rate) — same issue, plus medical-device-adjacent privacy sensitivity.

## Contradictions and caveats

- **Bloom's "2 sigma" is probably an overestimate of what's achievable** — later reanalyses put realistic tutoring-program effects closer to 0.3–0.4 SD. Don't anchor product ambitions on the 2-sigma number; VanLehn's more sober 0.76-vs-0.79 comparison (ITS vs. human tutor) is the safer planning number.
- **The confusion-is-good finding is correlational in most studies.** The causal claim ("inducing confusion improves learning") has real supporting work (contradiction-induction studies) but is much thinner than the correlational base. Don't build a feature that deliberately manufactures confusion without much stronger evidence — the downside (frustration spillover) is well-documented and the upside is comparatively unproven for real users outside lab settings.
- **Learner-control findings and open-learner-model findings are in tension** and this file doesn't fully resolve it: OLM research says showing/negotiating the model helps, but if the user negotiates their way into pure manual control, the learner-control literature says that specific outcome (for a novice) may underperform. The likely reconciliation — "transparent but system-led by default" — is a design hypothesis, not something directly tested in a single study found here.
- **Almost none of this evidence is about a single, self-directed adult user reading their own documents on their own schedule.** The vast majority of cited studies are K-12 or undergrad classroom/lab settings with external assessment (tests, grades) as the outcome measure. Riemann has no "test score" — its success criterion is closer to "time to a chosen understanding level" and subjective satisfaction, which none of these studies measure directly. Transfer is plausible but unverified.
- **Detector accuracy numbers throughout (mind-wandering 67-83%, disengagement models) are dataset- and population-specific**, mostly drawn from K-12 ITS or lab reading-comprehension tasks — they will not transfer 1:1 to a solo ADHD adult engineering student reading technical/varied material, and should be treated as order-of-magnitude plausibility, not a target to hit.
- **The ITS-vs-human-tutor comparisons predate LLM tutors almost entirely** (VanLehn 2011, Bloom 1984). The Harvard 2025 RCT is the best modern evidence but is one study, one domain (physics), one short intervention design — genuinely exciting but not yet a base rate to plan around.

## Gaps (for the second research wave)

- **How does confusion/frustration detection interact with ADHD specifically?** This file found general affect-detection literature but nothing ADHD-specific; worker 03 (adhd) should check whether attention-regulation differences change what "productive confusion" looks like or whether idle/dwell thresholds calibrated on neurotypical populations are miscalibrated for ADHD users (plausibly need shorter idle thresholds, different baseline dwell variance).
- **Interruption science / optimal moment to intervene.** This file touched idle-gap thresholds but didn't dig into the HCI literature on *when* an interruption is least costly (task-switching cost research, e.g., work descending from Bailey & Konstan on interruption timing) — directly relevant to deciding when Riemann should ever proactively suggest a zoom change vs. staying silent.
- **Long-horizon user modeling across many documents/sessions**, vs. the single-session focus of most bandit/knowledge-tracing literature reviewed here — Riemann will accumulate a history across many different pieces of content over months; almost none of the cited work addresses that timescale for a single user (it's mostly multi-user platform-scale, or single-session lab studies). Worth a targeted search on personal-informatics / quantified-self longitudinal modeling literature.
