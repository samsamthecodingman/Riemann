# 11 — Illusion of Understanding & AI-Summary Trust Calibration

## TL;DR
- The illusion of explanatory depth (Rozenblit & Keil 2002) is real and specific: people overrate their *explanatory* understanding far more than their factual/procedural/narrative knowledge, and the illusion collapses the moment they're forced to generate an explanation themselves — which is exactly the mechanism an LLM summary bypasses.
- Metacomprehension research (Thiede & Dunlosky and successors) shows people are bad at judging their own comprehension from immediate feel, but a **delayed** generative act (writing keywords or a summary, after a gap, not immediately after reading) reliably improves the accuracy of that self-judgment — this is close to a ready-made mechanism for Riemann's zoom dial.
- Generative learning strategies (Fiorella & Mayer's eight: summarizing, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, enacting) have real, uneven effect sizes — summarizing is one of the *weaker* ones on its own; testing/retrieval and drawing/mapping outperform it — which argues against treating "here's an AI summary" as equivalent to the learning benefit of "you wrote a summary."
- 2025 evidence on AI and cognition is trending toward "AI assistance measurably reduces effortful engagement and independent skill," across three different methods: an EEG/behavioral study (MIT Media Lab, preprint, NOT peer-reviewed), a field RCT in high-school math (Bastani et al., PNAS, peer-reviewed), and a large self-report survey of knowledge workers (Microsoft Research + CMU, CHI 2025, peer-reviewed). The self-report study's core finding is specifically relevant to Riemann: higher trust in the AI output predicted *less* critical thinking, and higher self-confidence predicted *more*.
- Explanations and uncertainty markers in AI outputs do not reliably produce appropriately calibrated trust — Vasconcelos et al. (CHI 2023) found explanations reduce overreliance only when they lower the *cost of verifying* the claim; otherwise they become a heuristic trust cue that increases blind reliance rather than scrutiny.
- Buçinca et al.'s "cognitive forcing functions" (CHI 2021) are the most directly actionable HCI result: withholding the AI's answer until the user commits to their own guess measurably cuts overreliance, but it comes at a real cost — users rate the friction-adding designs as *less* satisfying, and the effect is bigger for people high in Need for Cognition, i.e. it's not free and not uniformly liked.
- "Showing what's missing" is an emerging, validated design pattern: DiSCo (2026 preprint) shows that a summary which explicitly surfaces expected-but-omitted content is rated significantly more detailed and decision-useful (d=0.62) at a moderate cost to perceived ease of understanding (d=0.51) — a direct, costed precedent for a Riemann "what this level leaves out" feature.
- Automation bias (Parasuraman & Manzey 2010) is not fixable by training or disclaimers alone, and hits experts and novices roughly equally — the fix has to be structural (forced friction, visible cost of trust) not informational (just adding a warning label).
- People are bad at detecting subtle factual errors in AI summaries specifically because they're plausible, not wholesale nonsense — trained clinicians took ~92 minutes on average to properly fact-check one AI summary against source notes, which is a strong argument against relying on users to "just check" without tooling.

## Findings

1. **Claim:** People systematically overestimate how well they understand the causal mechanisms of everyday things ("illusion of explanatory depth"); the illusion is much stronger for *explanatory* knowledge than for facts, procedures, or narratives, and is reliably punctured by forcing the person to generate an explanation (not just re-read one).
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG (original finding replicated widely; foundational in metacognition literature).
   **Sources:** Rozenblit & Keil (2002), "The Misunderstood Limits of Folk Science: An Illusion of Explanatory Depth," *Cognitive Science* — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1 ; full text PDF: https://time.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/ioed_proofs.pdf_1.pdf ; overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth ; https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/the-illusion-of-explanatory-depth

2. **Claim:** People's felt sense of understanding right after reading (metacomprehension) is poorly correlated with actual test performance — but generating keywords or a summary *after a delay* (not immediately) meaningfully improves the accuracy of that self-judgment, because the delay forces reliance on the durable situation-model representation rather than fading surface memory.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG for the core "immediate judgments are poorly calibrated" finding (decades of metacomprehension literature); MODERATE-STRONG for the specific delayed-keyword-generation fix (a well-cited, mechanistically-explained result, though from a narrower set of studies than the base finding).
   **Sources:** Thiede & Dunlosky et al., "Understanding the Delayed-Keyword Effect on Metacomprehension Accuracy" — https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/cifs_facpubs/8/ ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7378587 ; Dunlosky & Lipko (2007), "Metacomprehension: A Brief History and How to Improve Its Accuracy," *Current Directions in Psychological Science* — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8721.2007.00509.x

3. **Claim:** Of Fiorella & Mayer's eight generative learning strategies, effect sizes are uneven and none of them is "read an AI-generated version" — concept mapping ~d=0.62 (25 studies), drawing ~d=0.69 (53 studies), teaching/learning-by-teaching ~d=0.30–0.40 (28 studies). Summarizing specifically is a weaker, more context-dependent strategy than testing/retrieval-based methods.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE-STRONG (book-length synthesis and a well-cited Educational Psychology Review article; individual effect sizes come from sub-meta-analyses of varying quality, and I could not access the publisher's full table directly — paywalled — so these numbers are drawn from secondary summaries of the same source, not the primary table itself).
   **Sources:** Fiorella & Mayer (2016), "Eight Ways to Promote Generative Learning," *Educational Psychology Review* — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-015-9348-9 (paywalled, not directly opened — summarized via ERIC/secondary sources) ; https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1120458 ; book: Fiorella & Mayer, *Learning as a Generative Activity* — https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/learning-as-a-generative-activity/A7E1D7F80C8078AF9E4418ABFF459589

4. **Claim:** Summarizing a text yourself is not reliably better than rereading, and its benefit depends heavily on summary *quality* — readers who produce high-quality summaries make more/longer eye-movement fixations and regressions on question-relevant text; low-quality self-summarizers can underperform even rereaders. This means "summarizing helps" is not unconditionally true even for self-generated summaries, and an AI-generated one (bypassing the search/fixation process entirely) likely captures even less of the benefit.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (mixed findings across studies; effect depends on task and summary quality, a genuine contradiction in the literature, not settled).
   **Sources:** eye-tracking study — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7898009/ ; summarizing vs rereading (sun-exposure text study showing summarizers scored *lower*) found via search of https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361476X23000929 and https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254905905_Summarizing_as_retrieval_strategy_versus_re-reading_Which_learning_activity_works (abstract-level, not fully opened — flagged as not deeply verified).

5. **Claim (flagged preprint, not peer-reviewed):** In a controlled essay-writing study, participants who used an LLM assistant showed markedly lower EEG-measured prefrontal/parietal engagement, and could recall only ~17% of their own just-written essay content after 24 hours, versus ~46% for a no-tool "brain-only" group; when LLM users were later asked to write without the tool, they underperformed.
   **Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY — explicitly an un-peer-reviewed arXiv preprint (arXiv:2506.08872) with a small sample (54 participants, only 18 completing the crossover session 4). Treat the EEG/"cognitive debt" framing as suggestive, not established; multiple critical commentaries and at least one formal comment/rebuttal exist.
   **Sources:** MIT Media Lab project page — https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/ ; critical commentary/rebuttal preprint — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.00856 ; explainer with caveats — https://www.transparencycoalition.ai/news/learn-about-the-this-is-your-brain-on-chatgpt-study-results-limitations-risks-and-more

6. **Claim:** In a field experiment with ~1,000 high-school students, unrestricted GPT-4 access during math practice improved in-session performance but *reduced* performance on a subsequent test taken without AI access (evidence of a "crutch" effect / reduced skill acquisition); a version of the tutor restricted to give hints rather than answers avoided this harm.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG (peer-reviewed, published in PNAS, real field RCT with a large N, a genuine causal design — not self-report).
   **Sources:** Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı, Mariman (2025), "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics," *PNAS* — https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122 ; PMC full text — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12232635/ ; correction notice — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12403119/

7. **Claim:** In a survey of 319 knowledge workers describing 936 real AI use cases, self-reported critical thinking effort was *lower* when the worker had higher confidence in the AI's ability to do the task, and *higher* when the worker had higher confidence in their own ability — i.e. trust in the tool and trust in oneself pull critical-thinking effort in opposite directions. Qualitatively, "critical thinking" with GenAI shifted from generation-time effort to output-verification effort (checking against sources/expertise) — but this shift doesn't always happen when the worker feels the AI is competent.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG as a self-report finding at CHI 2025 (peer-reviewed, large qualitative+quantitative sample) — but it is self-reported cognitive effort, not a behavioral/performance measure, so treat the *causal* "less critical thinking → worse outcomes" link as an inference, not directly demonstrated.
   **Sources:** Lee et al. (2025), "The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking," Microsoft Research / CMU, CHI 2025 — PDF: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf ; ACM DL: https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3706598.3713778 ; coverage: https://campustechnology.com/articles/2025/02/21/study-generative-ai-could-inhibit-critical-thinking.aspx

8. **Claim:** Cognitive offloading (using external tools/actions to reduce internal processing demand, e.g. writing down an appointment instead of remembering it) is a well-established, general strategy people use rationally under many conditions — but Risko & Gilbert's review frames the open question as how offloading *changes* the underlying cognitive processes and what the long-term consequences of habitual offloading are, not that offloading is inherently harmful.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG as a review/theoretical framework (Trends in Cognitive Sciences, well-cited), but it is explicitly a review raising open questions, not a single strong causal claim about harm.
   **Sources:** Risko & Gilbert (2016), "Cognitive Offloading," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20:676-688 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1364661316300985 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/

9. **Claim:** "The Knowledge Illusion" (Sloman & Fernbach) generalizes the illusion of explanatory depth into a claim that people routinely fail to distinguish knowledge stored in their own head from knowledge stored in other people/tools around them ("community of knowledge") — which is directly the mechanism by which an always-available AI summarizer could make someone feel more knowledgeable than they are, because the boundary between "I understand this" and "this is available to me" blurs.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE — this is a popular-science synthesis book built on real underlying research (including Rozenblit & Keil, credited by the authors themselves), not itself a new controlled study; treat the "community of knowledge" framing as a well-supported theoretical lens, not a directly tested causal claim about AI tools specifically (the book predates modern LLMs).
   **Sources:** https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/knowledge-illusion ; https://www.philipfernbach.com/the-knowledge-illusion ; https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/533524/the-knowledge-illusion-by-steven-sloman-and-philip-fernbach/

10. **Claim:** Explanations attached to an AI's output can reduce overreliance, but only under specific conditions — Vasconcelos et al.'s maze-solving studies (731 participants across 5 studies) found that explanations reduced overreliance in *hard* tasks specifically when they lowered the cost of independently verifying the claim; when verification stayed costly, explanations became a general trust heuristic that people leaned on *without* actually verifying, increasing (not decreasing) overreliance.
    **Evidence strength:** STRONG for the core "cost of verification" mechanism (CHI 2023 paper, 5 studies, large combined N) — I could not extract full text (PDF didn't parse), so numeric effect sizes are not independently confirmed here beyond what search summaries reported.
    **Sources:** Vasconcelos et al. (2023), "Explanations Can Reduce Overreliance on AI Systems During Decision-Making" — https://cicl.stanford.edu/papers/vasconcelos2023explanations.pdf (fetched, but content did not render as text) ; abstract/summary via search: https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Explanations-Can-Reduce-Overreliance-on-AI-Systems-Vasconcelos-J%C3%B6rke/e8c2f818ec941005f705a1de26597690420c534a

11. **Claim:** "Cognitive forcing functions" — UI designs that force the user to commit to their own answer *before* seeing the AI's recommendation, or that slow down / add friction to accepting the AI's answer — significantly reduce overreliance compared to simply showing an explanation alongside the AI's output (199 participants). But the forcing functions that reduced overreliance the most also received the *least* favorable subjective/satisfaction ratings, and benefited people high in Need for Cognition more than people low in it — i.e. the fix works but is disliked and not equally effective for everyone.
    **Evidence strength:** STRONG (CHI 2021 paper, controlled experiment, well-cited in the overreliance literature; a 2025 follow-up paper "Cognitive Forcing for Better Decision-Making" extends this with partial explanations).
    **Sources:** Buçinca, Malaya, Gajos (2021), "To Trust or to Think: Cognitive Forcing Functions Can Reduce Overreliance on AI in AI-assisted Decision-making," *CSCW/CHI* — https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692 ; full PDF: https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2021/bucinca21trust.pdf (fetched, did not render as text — summary drawn from abstract page and search results) ; 2025 follow-up: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3710946

12. **Claim:** A summarization interface that explicitly generates and surfaces content the source material implies but the plain summary would omit (comparing each item's topic coverage against domain-level expectations from similar items) produces summaries users rate as significantly more detailed and decision-useful (Cohen's d=0.62) — but at a moderate cost to perceived ease of understanding (d=0.51). The authors recommend concise-by-default summaries with the omission-aware detail available via a secondary interaction (hover/click), not shown by default.
    **Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY — this is a 2026 arXiv preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, single-domain (hotel/travel reviews), and evaluated for decision-support rather than reading comprehension. The mechanism (compare against domain-level norms to flag omissions) is a genuinely new, directly relevant idea for Riemann even though the evidence base is thin.
    **Source:** "DiSCo: Making Absence Visible in Intelligent Summarization Interfaces" — https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07229v2

13. **Claim:** Automation bias — following an automated/AI recommendation even against one's own better judgment or contrary evidence — is not reliably prevented by training or instructions alone, and affects both expert and novice users; the central cognitive mechanism is misallocated *attention* to the automation's output rather than "trust" as a discrete belief, and appropriate trust requires calibration to the system's actual (not assumed) reliability.
    **Evidence strength:** STRONG (foundational, highly-cited review integrating decades of human-automation-interaction research).
    **Sources:** Parasuraman & Manzey (2010), "Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration," *Human Factors* — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055 ; systematic review extending this — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751/

14. **Claim:** Errors in AI-generated summaries (e.g. clinical notes) are hard for humans to catch specifically because most errors are *subtle* — plausible-sounding factual inaccuracies or unsupported inferences, not obvious nonsense — and thorough fact-checking is slow: one study found it took a well-trained clinician ~92 minutes on average to fully verify a single AI-generated summary against its source notes, with automated checking tools catching errors human experts missed.
    **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (specific numbers come from a single cited study in a clinical/high-stakes domain, found via secondary search summary rather than the primary paper being opened directly — flagged as not independently verified at the primary-source level).
    **Source found via search, primary not opened:** referenced in https://www.clinicaltrialsarena.com/news/hallucinations-in-ai-generated-medical-summaries-remain-a-grave-concern/ and related arXiv summarization-faithfulness papers surfaced in search (e.g. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.15422, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-31075-1) — treat the "92 minutes" figure as indicative of the general problem (verification is slow and expensive) rather than a number to cite precisely, since the primary source wasn't opened.

15. **Claim:** The generation effect (self-generated information is remembered better than read information) does not require generating the whole answer — it has been demonstrated for minimal acts like inferring a single missing word, and a meta-analytic review finds the effect is sensitive to "generation constraint" (how much the learner has to produce) — implying small, constrained generative acts (fill-in-the-blank, one-word prediction) can recruit much of the effect at low time cost, though the meta-analysis itself is about *how much* constraint matters, not a claim that tiny acts get "most" of the benefit for free.
    **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (the core generation effect is STRONG/foundational — Slamecka & Graf 1978, cited by 10-contrarian-gemini's report already — but the specific claim that *minimal* single-word generation captures most of the benefit is inferred from a meta-analysis about constraint effects, not a direct "minimal dose-response" study; treat the "tiny acts are enough" framing as a reasonable but not fully proven design hypothesis).
    **Sources:** Bertsch et al., meta-analytic review of generation effect and generation constraint — https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01762-3 ; secondary explainer — https://www.structural-learning.com/post/generation-effect-active-learning

## Design implications for Riemann

- **"What this level leaves out" line, at L1/L2, default-off / one line only.** Directly modeled on DiSCo (finding 12): generate it by diffing what L1/L2 includes against L3 (or against what a domain-typical summary of this content-type would cover), and surface it as a single collapsed line ("this summary skips: [caveats/exceptions/2 named entities/etc.]"), not full text. DiSCo's own recommendation matches this: concise by default, detail on demand — expand via a tap, don't dump it in the flow. **Cost: ~1–3 seconds to read the collapsed line; 0 seconds if ignored; ~10–15s if expanded.**
- **Optional one-tap predict-before-reveal at a zoom transition.** Before expanding L2→L3 (or L1→L2) for a section, show the section heading/first clause only and ask "what do you expect this covers?" as a single free-text or even single-tap ("I have a guess" / "just show me") — grounded in the generation effect (finding 15) and the delayed-keyword metacomprehension result (finding 2: the *delay* matters, so this should not be immediately followed by the answer — insert a beat, even a fake-load moment, before revealing). This is Riemann's cheapest version of a cognitive forcing function (finding 11) — note the tradeoff explicitly: 10-contrarian and Buçinca both show forcing functions reduce overreliance/false-fluency *and* reduce satisfaction, so it must be skip-optional and probably suggested only occasionally (e.g. once per document, or when a "long dwell then bail" pattern suggests skimming-as-comprehension is happening), not on every transition. **Cost: ~3–8 seconds for a one-tap guess; ~15–30s for a one-sentence prediction; near-zero if skipped.**
- **Source-span hover/highlight linking summary claims to originating text (provenance highlighting).** 10-contrarian-gemini already proposed this for hallucination mitigation; this report adds the trust-calibration angle from Vasconcelos (finding 10): the value of this feature depends entirely on whether it lowers the *actual cost of verifying* a claim. A static highlight that just shows "here's roughly where this came from" is closer to the failure mode (a trust heuristic people lean on without reading) than the fix. To actually calibrate trust rather than just soothe it, the hover should show enough of the source span that a user can genuinely judge the claim in under ~5 seconds — a full sentence or two of L3, not just a citation marker. **Cost: ~3–5 seconds per hover if the span is well-scoped; adds no cost if never hovered.**
- **A visible, low-effort confidence/calibration check, not a passive disclaimer.** Per automation bias (finding 13) and the critical-thinking survey (finding 7), a static "AI-generated, may contain errors" label is close to useless — it doesn't change attention allocation. Instead, borrow the metacomprehension design (finding 2): after reading L1, ask a single specific factual prequestion drawn from L2/L3 content ("did the article say X or Y happened first?") — right/wrong is itself informative to the user (closing the illusion-of-understanding gap) and to Riemann (signal). This should target checkable facts, not vague "did you understand," matching 05's caveat that pretesting works for factual not conceptual content. **Cost: ~5–10 seconds.**
- **Route the "which content-type is this" decision through Bastani's guardrail insight (finding 6): give hints, not full answers, when the user is trying to learn vs. trying to retrieve.** If Riemann can distinguish "I need to act on this now" from "I'm trying to build lasting understanding of this domain" (this maps onto 05's reference-tool-vs-learning-tool distinction, which 10-contrarian also raised), the learning-mode path should bias toward generative touches (predict-before-reveal, retrieval checks) and away from a straight zoom-to-answer, while the retrieval-mode path should stay frictionless. This is a single mode toggle, not per-interaction friction.
- **Never present L0/L1 fluency as a proxy for depth of understanding in the UI's own language.** Per Rozenblit & Keil (finding 1) and the knowledge-illusion framing (finding 9), avoid phrasing like "you now understand X" after an L0/L1 read — that's precisely the false signal the research warns about. Prefer "you have the gist of X" / "quick take on X," reserving "understand" language for after a generative or retrieval-check interaction has actually happened.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Confidence-vs-correctness gap on prequestion/retrieval checks** — direct operationalization of metacomprehension miscalibration (finding 2); large gaps flag both a user this feature should help and (aggregated) whether a given L1/L2 generation is producing false fluency for many users on that document.
- **Hover/expand rate on the "what this leaves out" line** — near-zero expand rate across many documents suggests the feature is being ignored (a labeling/automation-bias problem, finding 13) rather than that nothing is ever omitted; near-100% expand rate might mean the collapsed line itself is insufficiently informative to act as a real signal, prompting a redesign.
- **Skip rate on predict-before-reveal prompts, segmented by session state** — high skip rate under low load/energy (if Riemann has adaptive-load signals from other workers' research) is expected and fine; high skip rate under high load/energy suggests the prompt is miscalibrated as friction rather than useful engagement.
- **Verification-follow-through** — does the user who hovers a source-span actually change their zoom behavior afterward (e.g., zoom to L3 for that section) versus just dismiss it? This distinguishes "using the hover as real verification" from "using it as a trust heuristic," directly testing whether Riemann has avoided the Vasconcelos failure mode (finding 10).
- **Self-reported trust vs. self-reported effort, sampled occasionally** — a lightweight adaptation of the Microsoft/CMU survey instrument (finding 7) could be run as an occasional 1-question in-app pulse ("how much did you double-check this yourself?") correlated against confidence in the AI summary, to detect the same trust/effort tradeoff at the individual level over time.

## Contradictions and caveats

- **Cognitive forcing functions work but are disliked** (finding 11) — this is a genuine, unresolved tension for Riemann's ADHD-focused brief: adding friction to reduce false fluency directly fights the "zero cognitive load, works with fluctuating capacity" design principle from the shared brief. There is no evidence here that this tension resolves cleanly; the most defensible position is to make every friction-adding feature (predict-before-reveal, retrieval checks) opt-in/occasional rather than default/constant, accepting that this means most users most of the time will not get the calibration benefit.
- **The summarizing-specific evidence is weaker and more mixed than the "generative learning is good" headline suggests** (finding 4) — even *self*-generated summaries sometimes underperform rereading, and quality matters enormously. This weakens any assumption that "let the user write their own L1 instead of the AI" would straightforwardly outperform an AI-generated one; it depends on whether the user is capable of writing a *good* summary, which for an overloaded/fluctuating-attention user is not guaranteed.
- **Most AI-cognition evidence is about generation/writing tasks, not reading/summarization tasks.** The MIT preprint (finding 5) and Bastani et al. (finding 6) both study people producing output *with* AI help (essays, math problem-solving), not people *consuming* AI-produced summaries, which is Riemann's actual use case. The inference from "AI helping you write reduces retention/skill" to "AI summarizing for you reduces understanding" is directionally plausible but not the same causal claim, and I found no study that directly tests "reading an AI abstraction-tree summary vs. reading the original, on later comprehension/retention" — flagged explicitly as a gap below.
- **The MIT "Your Brain on ChatGPT" study is widely cited in the press as if settled; it is not** (finding 5) — small sample, unreviewed preprint, and at least one critical rebuttal exists. Riemann's design shouldn't lean on its specific numbers (17% vs 46% recall) even though its general direction agrees with more rigorous evidence (Bastani et al.).
- **The critical-thinking survey (finding 7) is self-report**, not behavior — people's account of how much they scrutinized an AI output is not the same as measured scrutiny. Use it as directional evidence for trust/effort tradeoff, not as a precise effect size.
- **DiSCo (finding 12) is single-domain (travel reviews), unreviewed, and about decision support, not comprehension** — the "what this leaves out" line for Riemann is a reasonable adaptation but untested for text summarization/abstraction specifically; worth a cheap internal pilot rather than treating the d=0.62/d=0.51 numbers as transferable.
- **The clinical-summary "92 minutes to verify" figure (finding 14) was not independently confirmed** — flagged in the finding itself; I could not open the primary source and this number comes from a secondary press summary. Treat it as illustrative of "verification is slow," not a number to put in a Riemann spec.

## Gaps

- **A direct study comparing "read an AI abstraction-tree/summary" vs. "read the original" on later comprehension and retention** does not appear to exist yet in a form I could find — this is the single most on-point missing piece of evidence for Riemann's whole premise, and worth flagging to Sam directly: the zoom dial's core bet (fast path to real understanding) is currently supported by adjacent evidence (generation effect, metacomprehension, AI-and-writing studies) but not by a study of the exact task.
- **Whether uncertainty/confidence markers on AI text (not just AI decisions/recommendations) get read or acted on** — the automation-bias and explanation-reliance literature (findings 10, 13) is mostly about AI *decisions* (medical diagnosis, hiring, maze-solving), not AI *text summaries*. Whether a hedge like "the source is ambiguous about X" in an L1 summary gets noticed and acted on the same way a numeric uncertainty score does in decision-support UIs is untested as far as I found — worth a dedicated look, possibly by whoever ends up prototyping the "what this leaves out" feature.
- **Interaction between illusion-of-understanding and ADHD-specific self-monitoring deficits** — Barkley's executive-function model (referenced in 10-contrarian-gemini) suggests self-monitoring/metacognition is itself often impaired in ADHD; if metacomprehension calibration is already harder for this population at baseline, the "delayed keyword generation improves calibration" fix (finding 2) may need a different delay/format to work reliably for Sam specifically. This is squarely in 13-adhd-deep's territory — worth a direct message to that worker if their report doesn't already cover metacognitive/self-monitoring calibration under ADHD.
- **Longitudinal/habituation effects of "what this leaves out" or predict-before-reveal features** — all the friction-reducing-overreliance studies found here are single-session lab experiments; whether users habituate to (and start ignoring) a forcing function or omission-line after repeated exposure, the way people habituate to cookie banners, is unknown and directly relevant to whether these mitigations stay effective over Riemann's actual long-term daily use.
