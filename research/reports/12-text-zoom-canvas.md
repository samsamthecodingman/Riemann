# 12 — Transitions for text-detail changes, spatial hypertext, and infinite canvases

Wave-1's `06-spatial.md` already covers the space-scale diagram (Furnas & Bederson), the Cockburn/Karlson/Bederson overview+detail/zoom/focus+context survey, desert fog, mental-map preservation, and Lynch's five elements. This report does not repeat those; it goes narrower and deeper on (a) whether transition research generalizes from *charts* to *reflowing text*, (b) spatial hypertext's lessons about informal structure, (c) why people abandon infinite-canvas tools specifically, (d) landmark selection mechanics, and (e) minimap parameters — then gives concrete specs for the v1 dial and the later spatial view.

## TL;DR

- Heer & Robertson's animated-transitions work (2007) is about *charts*, not text, and its core mechanism — object constancy via staged, ~1s transitions that change one visual property at a time — has no direct equivalent for reflowing paragraphs, because text has no stable per-glyph identity to animate between levels. This is a real gap, not a solved problem (MODERATE/GAP).
- Stretchtext (Ted Nelson, 1970) is *exactly* Riemann's zoom-dial concept, proposed 55 years ago, and it never reached mainstream adoption — not because the idea is wrong but because authoring multiple nested versions of the same content by hand is expensive; Riemann's LLM-generated levels sidestep the original blocker (MODERATE).
- Telescopic Text (telescopictext.org) is a shipped, working implementation of word-level stretchtext: click a highlighted span, it expands in place. It's a good reference for the *interaction*, but operates at word/clause granularity, not Riemann's paragraph/section granularity (WEAK evidence — no published usability study found, just a live tool).
- Outliner "zoom into bullet" (Workflowy, Logseq) is the closest shipped analogue to Riemann's level-changing dial: zooming in collapses ancestors into a breadcrumb trail, not into nothing — this is the concrete mechanism Riemann should copy for "what happens to content above the current level" (MODERATE — widely used, converged on independently by two products).
- Marshall & Shipman's VIKI/spatial-hypertext work (1994-95) found that visual/spatial arrangement lets people express *emerging, ambiguous, partial* structure that they can't or won't commit to formal categories for — directly relevant to a future spatial view that must tolerate half-formed organization (STRONG within its niche — a foundational, well-cited body of work, though based on qualitative/observational studies, not controlled experiments).
- Every infinite-canvas product researched (Muse, Heptabase, Scapple, tldraw, Kinopio) converges on the same design move: constrain the canvas with an underlying structured layer (cards/notes that exist independently of position) rather than trusting position alone — this is the practitioner-level answer to "canvas rot" (MODERATE, convergent practitioner evidence, not controlled studies).
- Landmark research (Vinson 1999; Sorrows & Hirtle 1999) gives three necessary landmark properties: **permanence** (always in the same place), **uniqueness/visual distinctiveness**, and **identifiability** (recognizable at a glance, not by reading) — none of which "a bolded heading" alone satisfies (MODERATE, VE-navigation literature applied by analogy).
- Nielsen's progressive-disclosure guidance explicitly warns that **more than two disclosure levels tends to have low usability** because users lose track of how many levels exist and how to get back — this directly contradicts a naive 4-detent (L0-L3) dial and needs a mitigation (STRONG within UX practice, though it's expert guidance, not a controlled study).
- Hornbæk & Frøkjær's controlled study of overview+detail vs. fisheye vs. detail-only for reading electronic documents found overview+detail produced significantly better essays (comprehension/synthesis) but was ~20% *slower* for targeted lookup — the "best" interface depends on task type, which matters for whether Riemann should default to a stable side overview or a single changing view (MODERATE — one study, but a controlled comparison).
- Motion-triggered discomfort ("vection") from continuous zoom is real and has practitioner-established mitigations: cap zoom-step size, use crossfades instead of large continuous scaling for level jumps, and always respect `prefers-reduced-motion` (MODERATE for the mechanism, STRONG for the CSS mitigation which is a settled web standard).

## Findings

### Do animated transitions work for reflowing text the way they work for charts?

**Heer & Robertson (2007), "Animated Transitions in Statistical Data Graphics," IEEE TVCG 13(6):1240-1247** (https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf, abstract/summary corroborated via https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TVCG.2007.70539 and https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5878335_Animated_Transitions_in_Statistical_Data_Graphics). Their taxonomy: transitions should preserve **object constancy** (each visual mark keeps a continuous identity across the transition, e.g., a specific bar tracks to become a specific point) via **staging** (change one design dimension at a time — e.g., first re-position points to x-coordinates, *then* morph them into bars) at roughly **1 second per stage**. Two controlled experiments found staged, object-constant animated transitions significantly improved viewers' ability to track what changed, versus a jump-cut. Evidence strength: **STRONG within its domain** (controlled experiments, > 1000 citations), but its domain is marks in a coordinate space with a natural 1:1 correspondence between "before" and "after" elements.

**Why this doesn't transfer cleanly to text**: a bar chart's bars have stable identity (bar for "2023" is always "the 2023 bar"). Reflowing text from L2 (a 60-word section gist) to L3 (the full 800-word section) has no such 1:1 mapping — L3 is not "L2 with more decimal places," it's substantively different prose that L2 was *generated from* by summarization, not derived from by simple transformation. There is no natural glyph-level or even sentence-level correspondence to animate between. I found **no published study** that applies Heer & Robertson-style object-constancy transitions to natural-language text reflow specifically (searched directly; came up empty — this is a genuine, not just under-searched, gap). The nearest things that exist are:

- **FLIP (First-Last-Invert-Play)** (Paul Lewis, popularized via https://aerotwist.com/blog/flip-your-animations/ and https://css-tricks.com/animating-layouts-with-the-flip-technique/): a *implementation technique*, not a psychological finding, for animating DOM elements (e.g., an accordion or card) between two known layout states cheaply, using compositor-only transforms. It's how you'd *build* a smooth expand animation, but it says nothing about whether animating text reflow helps comprehension — it only makes the animation performant. Evidence strength: **engineering pattern, not a UX finding.**
- **Stretchtext** (Ted Nelson, coined 1970, described as early as 1967) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stretchtext, https://eclecticlight.co/2016/09/03/stretchtext-a-hidden-gem-in-real-hypertext/. Nelson's original vision: text expands/contracts *in place* as the reader adjusts a "throttle," preserving surrounding context rather than replacing the whole view. This is conceptually identical to Riemann's dial. It was implemented in a handful of hypertext-fiction works (Stuart Moulthrop's *Victory Garden*, 1991; Judd Morrissey's *The Jew's Daughter*, 1998) and one JS library (Brad Neuberg's stretchtext.js, http://codinginparadise.org/ebooks/html/blog/stretchtext.html) but **never reached mainstream tools**. No source I found documents *why* in usability terms — the consistent explanation across sources (Wikipedia; Bill Wadge's essay https://billwadge.com/2022/02/24/stretchtext-or-bust-ted-nelsons-unrealized-vision/) is an **authoring cost problem**: "you need to write multiple versions of your content and lay them out so the user can expand or shorten them at will" — a human author had to write every level by hand. Evidence strength: **WEAK/PRELIMINARY as a causal claim** (no controlled study of why it failed to spread; this is historical/essayistic consensus) but **directly relevant**: Riemann's LLM-generated levels remove exactly this blocker, which is a genuine structural reason to expect stretchtext-style interaction to work now when it didn't in 1995-2010.
- **Telescopic Text** (https://www.telescopictext.org/, https://github.com/jackyzha0/telescopic-text) — a live, shipped implementation: click a highlighted word/phrase and the sentence expands in place with more detail, at **word/clause granularity** (a sentence becomes a paragraph), used mostly for teaching descriptive writing. No published usability data found — evaluate as a working reference implementation of the *interaction*, not as validated UX. Evidence strength: **WEAK** (no study), but useful as an existence proof that in-place text expansion is buildable and legible at small granularity.
- **"Coeffects" and Arbital's Bayes' rule explainer** are cited by Andy Matuschak's notes (https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zPLUdgxrXo5z24ccuyz8YQ4) as contemporary stretchtext-adjacent examples ("readers can control the depth or level-of-detail of a written work") but the notes are a bare definition with no design analysis — I could not find deeper Matuschak writing on this specific topic despite searching.

**Bartram, Ho, Dill & Henigman (1995), "The Continuous Zoom: A Constrained Fisheye Technique for Viewing and Navigating Large Information Spaces," UIST '95, pp. 207-215.** I located the citation (via search) but could not retrieve full text (not open-access; ACM DL paywalled, no PDF mirror found). Based on the abstract-level description available, it addresses continuous fisheye zooming as a navigation technique for large 2D information spaces generally, not text reflow specifically — flagging this as **unverified beyond the abstract**; a worker with ACM DL access should confirm details before citing it for anything beyond the existence of the technique.

**Practical implication**: since there's no validated "morph the words" transition for text, the honest options for a level change are (1) **hard cut** (no animation — jump-cut is the current baseline that stretchtext/telescopic text and outliners actually ship), (2) **crossfade** (old text fades out, new text fades in, ~150-300ms — standard, cheap, doesn't imply false correspondence), or (3) **FLIP-style layout animation of container/heading elements only** (the *heading* of a section can visually morph position/size using FLIP since headings *do* have 1:1 identity across levels — a section heading at L2 is the same heading at L3 — while the *body text under it* crossfades). Option 3 is the closest legitimate analogue to Heer & Robertson's object constancy: **animate the elements that have real identity continuity (headings, node boundaries) with FLIP-style movement; crossfade the elements that don't (prose body)**. This is my own synthesis, not a cited finding — flagged as a **design recommendation, not evidence**.

### Fold/unfold outliners as the closest shipped analogue to a level-changing dial

**Workflowy** (https://workflowy.com/learn/bullets/, corroborated by https://blog.workflowy.com/workflowy-has-a-new-design/): clicking a bullet "zooms" it to become the page's new root; everything outside it disappears from view, and the ancestor chain collapses into a **breadcrumb bar at the top**, not into nothing. Users reported (via the Workflowy blog's own retrospective) that when the breadcrumb was hidden behind a menu or aggressively truncated in a redesign, they lost the "bigger picture" workflow the feature was built for — i.e., **the breadcrumb doing its job is load-bearing, not decorative**, and truncating it breaks orientation. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** — company's own design retrospective plus long-running, widely-used product behavior, not a controlled study.

**Logseq** independently converged on the same pattern (zoom into any block, treat it as its own page, breadcrumb shows the path back) — per Logseq's own blog and GitHub issue tracker (e.g. issue about breadcrumb re-render performance, https://github.com/logseq/logseq/pull/13268; a filed usability complaint about focus not being preserved when zooming out, https://github.com/logseq/logseq/issues/8780). Evidence strength: **MODERATE** — two independent products, built years apart, arrived at "zoom collapses ancestors into a breadcrumb, doesn't hide them entirely" — this is convergent design consensus, which is meaningfully stronger evidence than either product alone.

**Direct implication for Riemann**: when the dial moves from, say, L1 to L3 focused on section 3, the level-1 gist and level-2 section-gist for the ancestor chain shouldn't vanish — they should collapse into a small persistent breadcrumb ("Document gist → Section 3 gist → [reading L3]") that stays clickable. This also directly satisfies wave-1's already-stated "persistent L0 header" recommendation in `06-spatial.md` finding #3 — this wave's outliner research gives it a concrete, shipped UI pattern (breadcrumb, not just header text) rather than just a principle.

### Spatial hypertext: VIKI/VKB and evolving informal structure

**Marshall & Shipman, "Spatial Hypertext: Designing for Change," Communications of the ACM 38(8), 1995** (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/208344.208350 — abstract accessible, full text paywalled) and **Marshall & Shipman, "VIKI: Spatial Hypertext Supporting Emergent Structure," ECHT '94** (abstract at https://people.engr.tamu.edu/shipman/abstracts/echt94-abstract.html; also indexed at https://dl.acm.org/citation.cfm?id=192759). Core argument: formal hypertext (typed nodes/links, rigid taxonomies) forces users to commit to structure *before* they understand their material well enough to structure it correctly. Spatial hypertext instead lets people express structure through **visual/spatial arrangement** — proximity, clustering, alignment, color — which can be **ambiguous, partial, or emerging**, without forcing early commitment to formal categories. VIKI's **spatial parser** was a specific mechanism: it detects visually apparent groupings (things placed near each other, aligned, or similarly styled) and offers to promote them into formal structure once a pattern stabilizes — i.e., informal-to-formal is a *gradual, detected* transition, not a manual upfront decision.

**Shipman & Marshall, "Finding and Using Implicit Structure in Human-Organized Spatial Layouts of Information," CHI '94** (full text: https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/223904.223949). This is the empirical companion piece: they studied how people actually organize items spatially (on a 2D canvas) in naturalistic tasks and found consistent implicit-structure cues (spatial proximity, alignment, and *color/shape similarity*) that a program could detect and act on. Evidence strength: **STRONG within its niche** — this is a foundational, heavily cited (100s of citations) line of qualitative + system-building HCI work from Xerox PARC, though it predates modern statistical/ML methods and its "detect the pattern" claims are from 1990s prototypes, not rigorously benchmarked against ground truth at scale.

**Direct implications for Riemann's future spatial view**:
1. Don't force users to manually tag/categorize nodes to get spatial organization — if node position or grouping is ever user-adjustable, treat manually-created clusters as *signal* (like VIKI's spatial parser) rather than requiring formal tags first.
2. The "ambiguous, partial, emerging" framing is a strong argument for letting the spatial view tolerate a half-organized state indefinitely, rather than nudging users toward a "finished" canvas — this connects directly to the canvas-rot findings below, where forcing tidiness is exactly what makes canvases feel like work rather than thought.
3. Since Riemann's tree structure is *generated*, not manually spatially arranged, VIKI's core mechanism (inferring structure from spatial arrangement) is inverted in Riemann's case: Riemann already has the formal structure (the tree) and needs to *lay it out* spatially, which is closer to automatic graph layout than to VIKI's structure-detection problem. This is a genuine disanalogy to flag, not a place to naively reuse VIKI's algorithm.

### Infinite-canvas tools: design rationale and why people abandon them

**Muse** (https://www.inkandswitch.com/muse/, Ink & Switch's own retrospective essay — the richest primary source found). Design goals: repository for mixed media without silos, **user-controlled spatial arrangement** (explicitly *not* algorithmic layout), universal inking, and excerpting. Key findings reported by the team themselves: users adopted **continuous pinch-to-zoom navigation quickly and without training**, borrowing the mental model directly from map apps; a PDF-overview mode (many pages visible at once, like flipping through a physical book) was received better than linear one-page-at-a-time scrolling. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** — this is the design team's own retrospective with real usage observations, not a controlled study, but it's unusually candid about open problems (they explicitly list unsolved: cross-device sync, scaling the gesture vocabulary, text editing).

**Heptabase** (https://wiki.heptabase.com/fundamental-elements, https://www.goedel.io/p/unleashing-the-mind-heptabase). Explicit design thesis: **cards (atomic notes) are decoupled from their position on any given whiteboard** — the same card can appear on multiple "maps" without duplication. This is the practitioner answer to a specific canvas-rot failure mode: if position *is* the only record of a note's existence/meaning, losing track of where something is on a sprawling canvas effectively loses the note. By keeping notes as first-class objects independent of any one spatial arrangement, a whiteboard becomes a disposable *view* of the notes rather than their only home.

**tldraw** (https://tldraw.dev/, multiple docs pages). As an SDK rather than an end-user product, tldraw's own positioning is explicit: **"a canvas, not a knowledge base"** (per third-party comparison at https://omnicanvasnotes.com/blog/reviews/tldraw-alternatives) — no folders, no tags, no cross-board organization by design. This is presented as a feature (unopinionated primitive) but is also identified by the same source as the reason people outgrow raw canvas tools once they accumulate many boards.

**Scapple** (https://www.literatureandlatte.com/scapple/overview, forum discussion at https://www.outlinersoftware.com/topics/viewt/5185). Deliberately the *minimal* end of the spectrum: no forced node types, no forced connections — "every note is equal." Explicitly scoped by its own maker as good for a single scene/memo, "not recommended for outlining an entire book." This is a useful calibration point: Scapple's own positioning acknowledges that freeform spatial tools have a **scale ceiling** past which they should hand off to more structured tools (an outliner, a full book manuscript tool) — i.e., the product itself concedes that infinite canvas is not the right representation past a certain size, which is direct evidence against treating "canvas" as Riemann's default spatial view for large content.

**Why users abandon canvases — synthesized across sources** (no single controlled study found; convergent from https://omnicanvasnotes.com/blog/reviews/tldraw-alternatives, https://omnicanvasnotes.com/blog/reviews/miro-alternatives, and the "second brain graveyard" essays below):
1. **No structure independent of position** — if position is the only organizing principle and the canvas grows, users can no longer relocate things, so the canvas becomes "write-only."
2. **Maintenance burden falls entirely on the human** — nothing tidies, re-links, or resurfaces stale material automatically (this is the "graveyard" argument below).
3. **Too heavyweight for solo use** — tools built for team workshops (Miro) carry toolbar/feature overhead that personal use doesn't need.
4. **No cross-canvas search/organization** — once you have many boards, finding the right one becomes its own navigation problem (a second-order version of the desert-fog problem, one level up).

**"The Second Brain is a Graveyard" (Amit Das, essay, https://dobragworthywork.substack.com/p/the-second-brain-is-a-graveyard)**. Central claim, in the author's words (quoted under 15-word limit): tools "optimize for the moment of capture, not the moment six months later." Argues that clipping/filing produces a **false sense of comprehension** — the act of saving *feels like* the act of understanding, so the actual cognitive work (wrestling with the idea) gets skipped, and automated backlink graphs "cannot distinguish thought-based connections from coincidence." Evidence strength: **WEAK/PRELIMINARY as science** (single opinion essay, no citations to empirical research, explicitly a personal argument) but **worth taking seriously as a design warning**, and it converges with this wave's illusion-of-understanding research (see roster slug 11) and with wave-1's cognitive-offloading concerns — flag as a **cross-cutting risk**, not an isolated claim.

**Direct implication for Riemann**: this is the single most important caution for the "later spatial view." Riemann's abstraction tree is *generated automatically*, so it sidesteps failure mode #2 above (the tree doesn't rot because nothing needs manual re-linking) — but it does NOT sidestep #1 or the "false comprehension" risk, both of which apply just as much to an auto-generated spatial layout as to a manually curated one. A spatial view where nodes are laid out once and never require maintenance is a genuine structural advantage over Muse/Heptabase/Scapple (which all put maintenance on the user) — this is worth stating as a differentiator, not just a caveat.

### Automatic landmark selection

**Vinson (1999), "Design Guidelines for Landmarks to Support Navigation in Virtual Environments," CHI '99, pp. 278-285.** I could not get readable full text (WebFetch returned raw PDF binary from the NRC mirror both times attempted) — treat the following as **search-summary level, not directly verified**, and flag for a follow-up worker with better PDF extraction. Reported guideline themes (from search-result summaries of the paper): landmarks should be grounded in guidelines derived from **real-world navigation literature**, addressing the fact that virtual environments carry less sensory/locomotive detail than the real world and so need *compensating* landmark design.

**Sorrows & Hirtle (1999)** (cited secondhand via https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10339-021-01012-x, a 2021 review of landmark literature in *Cognitive Processing*) established three landmark categories — **visual** (stands out from surroundings), **structural** (sits at a decision point/junction), and **cognitive** (personally or culturally meaningful) — and the review confirms **permanence, uniqueness, and identifiability** as the convergent core criteria across the field. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** — a 2021 peer-reviewed literature review synthesizing landmark research, though I read it secondhand via search summary rather than the full review text.

**Concrete translation to Riemann's abstraction tree** — what would make an L1/L2 node function as a landmark rather than just a heading:
1. **Permanence**: the same gist text/id for a given node must not change on every regeneration — if the tree is rebuilt, a node the user has seen before should keep a stable identity (same `id`, same wording where possible) so it's recognizable next time, not just semantically similar.
2. **Structural**: landmark candidates are naturally the nodes at "decision points" — i.e., any node with multiple children (a branching point in the tree) is structurally more landmark-like than a leaf, mirroring Sorrows & Hirtle's "structural" category.
3. **Visual distinctiveness beyond text**: per Vinson/Sorrows & Hirtle, a landmark must be recognizable *without reading it* — a heading in the same font/size as everything else fails this. Concretely: assign each L1/L2 node a small, stable, non-text visual signature (e.g., a deterministic color or icon derived from a hash of its id, not from content — so it doesn't change on regeneration) that persists across zoom levels and in any future spatial view. This operationalizes wave-1's "distinct visual landmarks, not just labels" principle with a specific mechanism (deterministic hash → color/icon), not just a restatement of the goal.
4. **Automatic selection criterion**: given the tree already has depth (importance) and branching factor (structural salience) as available signals, a landmark set for a document = nodes at L1 (small, always visible) plus any L2 node whose child-count is above the section median (branching salience) — this is a first-cut heuristic proposal from me, not from the literature, since I found no source giving an algorithm for *automatic* landmark selection in an abstraction/summary tree specifically (this is a genuine gap — see Gaps below).

### Minimap and overview design parameters

**Hornbæk & Frøkjær, "Reading Patterns and Usability in Visualizations of Electronic Documents," interactions XI.1, 2004** (https://interactions.acm.org/archive/view/january-february-2004/reading-patterns-and-usability-in-visualizations-of-electronic-documen — WebFetch was blocked with HTTP 403; content below is from the search-result summary only, not verified against full text) and the related controlled study **Hornbæk & Frøkjær (2001)** comparing overview+detail, fisheye, and detail-only interfaces for reading/writing tasks on electronic documents. Reported results: overview+detail produced **significantly higher-quality essays** (better synthesis/comprehension) than detail-only, but was **~20% slower** than detail-only for answering specific lookup questions. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** — a controlled study (peer-reviewed), but summarized secondhand since the primary source was inaccessible; the exact effect sizes and N should be re-verified by a worker with ACM DL access before being treated as load-bearing.

**Direct implication**: this is a real trade-off, not a solved problem — a persistent minimap/overview is good for *synthesis* tasks (which matches "understand this document") but may cost time on *lookup* tasks (which matches "find this specific fact"). Riemann should probably make the overview **dismissible/collapsible** rather than permanently forced-on, so lookup-focused sessions aren't taxed by split attention, while synthesis-focused sessions can keep it open — this is consistent with wave-1's "split attention" cognitive-load finding but adds a task-type-dependent design lever wave-1 didn't specify.

**VS Code minimap** (https://code.visualstudio.com/docs/editing/getting-started/userinterface, https://css-weekly.com/start-using-minimap-section-headers-in-vs-code) — a shipped, heavily-used reference implementation, useful for concrete parameters even without a formal study behind it: positioned at the **fixed right edge** (not overlapping content), shows a **scaled-down rendering of the whole file** (shape recognition, not readable text) plus a **density overlay** of points of interest (search matches, errors, diffs) that a plain scrollbar can't show, and supports **click-or-drag-to-jump**. Its "section headers" feature (rendering named landmarks like `//#region` comments directly in the minimap) is the most directly transferable idea: **render L0/L1 gist text (or landmark icons) directly inside the minimap**, not just a generic shape, so the overview itself carries semantic landmarks rather than being purely geometric.

**LostInZoom research project** (EU/ERC-funded, ongoing as of the sources found: https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/101003012, https://lostinzoom.github.io/home/, position paper "Please, Help Me! I Am Lost in Zoom," https://ica-proc.copernicus.org/articles/4/107/2021/ica-proc-4-107-2021.pdf). Explicitly targets desert fog in multi-scale (zoomable) maps via **"anchor theory"**: the claim that a person's spatial mental model is composed of scale-specific fragments stitched together by landmarks that remain visible/recognizable across scale changes. Their stated approach has three parts: (1) identify which existing map features function as cognitive anchors, (2) design generalization techniques that deliberately *keep those anchors visually prominent* across scales rather than shrinking everything uniformly, (3) design zoom interactions/animations that key off those anchors. Evidence strength: **MODERATE-WEAK** — this is an active/recent research program (position paper + project description), not yet a mature body of validated results, but it is the most directly on-point work I found connecting "landmark selection" to "zoom transition design," which is precisely Riemann's problem. Worth returning to as the project publishes more (see Gaps).

**Minimap size/position parameters — concrete recommendation** synthesized from VS Code + the overview+detail literature (my synthesis, flag as design proposal not finding): fixed position (not draggable — reduces decision fatigue and keeps muscle memory), width proportional to available screen real estate but capped (VS Code's default is a narrow strip, not a large panel — it's a *cue*, not a reading surface), and update rate should be **instant/synchronous** with scroll position (no debounce) since Cockburn/Karlson/Bederson's finding (from `06-spatial.md`) is that overview+detail's whole value is a stable mental map — any lag between the detail view moving and the overview updating would undermine exactly the thing it's for.

### Specific failure modes of continuous zoom and shipped fixes

- **Desert fog** (already covered in `06-spatial.md`) — this wave adds the LostInZoom anchor-theory framing above and one more concrete shipped mitigation not in wave-1's report: **Google Maps' persistent labels that fade in/out by zoom threshold rather than scaling continuously** — place names and icons appear/disappear at fixed zoom bands rather than growing/shrinking proportionally, which is a real product's compromise between "true continuous zoom" and "landmarks must stay legible" (observed via https://www.core77.com/posts/21486/google-maps-designing-the-modern-atlas-21486 and https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/javascript/controls — practitioner/product documentation, not a study).
- **Motion sickness / vection from continuous zoom**: the mechanism is visually-induced perceived self-motion (vection) conflicting with vestibular/proprioceptive input; this is well-established for VR locomotion (e.g. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.04217) and the same visual mechanism underlies discomfort reported anecdotally for 2D continuous-zoom presentation tools — the Prezi motion-sickness reports already flagged in `06-spatial.md` are the 2D-specific evidence; this wave adds the general VR mechanism as the likely underlying cause. Practitioner mitigation guidance converges on: **cap per-transition scale change** (rough figure found: limit to 10-20% size change per discrete step rather than large continuous zooms — sourced from a general UI motion-sickness guide, https://elasticsdk.holoride.com/docs/0.29/manual/Experience-Design/UI-Guidelines/Motion-Sickness-Guidelines.html — evidence strength **WEAK**, it's a game-engine SDK's house guideline, not a study, but directionally consistent with the vection literature), avoid full-viewport scaling, and prefer stepped/discrete transitions over continuous ones when large jumps are needed.
- **`prefers-reduced-motion`** (W3C Media Queries Level 5; practitioner explainer at https://webkit.org/blog/7551/responsive-design-for-motion/): a settled, shipped, cross-browser standard specifically because ~69 million Americans (cited in the WebKit post, sourced to vestibular-disorder prevalence estimates) can be made nauseated or disoriented by exactly the kind of scaling/zooming animation a zoom dial produces. WebKit's own guidance: don't strip all motion indiscriminately, but *do* replace scaling/zooming and parallax effects with simpler alternatives (e.g., crossfade) when this media query is set. Evidence strength: **STRONG as a settled, shipped standard**; the underlying prevalence figures are as-cited in a vendor blog, not independently verified by me.

## Design implications for Riemann

**For the v1 markdown zoom dial:**

1. **Transition type**: no literal text-morphing animation (none exists in the literature or in shipped products at paragraph granularity) — instead: (a) section/node headings that persist across levels use a short FLIP-style position/size animation (they have real cross-level identity), (b) prose body content crossfades in/out (~150-250ms, well under Heer & Robertson's 1s chart-transition benchmark since text has no per-glyph identity to preserve — the shorter duration is because there's nothing to track, not because faster is inherently better), (c) the whole transition set must be disabled/replaced with an instant cut under `prefers-reduced-motion`. This is a synthesis, not a lookup — no source specifies exact durations for text; 150-250ms is drawn down from Heer & Robertson's 1s chart benchmark by analogy, flagged as **untested**.
2. **Anchoring algorithm**: 06-spatial.md's space-scale-diagram framing (hold x,y/source-span fixed, sweep scale via the tree's parent/child links) is the right formal model — this wave adds that the **ancestor chain, when it leaves the viewport, must collapse into a clickable breadcrumb bar, not disappear**, per the Workflowy/Logseq convergent pattern. Concretely: render a fixed-position breadcrumb strip above the content showing L0 gist → active L1/L2 ancestors → "you are here," each segment clickable to re-center the dial at that node.
3. **Disclosure-level warning**: Nielsen's "more than two disclosure levels has low usability" finding is a direct challenge to a 4-level (L0-L3) dial. Mitigation: the *dial* can have 4 detents, but the **breadcrumb should never show more than 2-3 ancestor levels at once** (collapse deeper chains into "…"), and the dial's current-level label should always state both the level name and a one-line description ("L1 — quick summary") so users aren't relying on memorizing what "L2" means.
4. **Landmark visual signature**: give every L1 node (and any L2 node above median branching factor) a small, deterministic, content-independent visual tag (color/icon from a hash of its stable id) rendered next to its heading at every level and in the breadcrumb, so recognition doesn't require re-reading text — directly operationalizes Vinson/Sorrows & Hirtle's identifiability + permanence criteria.
5. **Minimap**: build it dismissible, not forced — default on for documents opened for the first time (synthesis mode) and rememberable-off per user preference for revisits (lookup mode), given Hornbæk & Frøkjær's task-dependent trade-off. Render L0/L1 gist snippets or landmark tags inside the minimap itself (VS Code section-header pattern), not just a geometric shape.
6. **Vection guard**: if any continuous (non-detent) scaling is ever added (e.g., ctrl-scroll per 00-plan.md), cap the animated scale change per discrete frame and never animate a full-viewport zoom in one continuous sweep for large level jumps (e.g., L0 → L3 directly) — prefer a quick crossfade-through-black-or-blur "cut" for jumps of more than one level, reserving continuous motion for adjacent-level changes only.

**For the later spatial view:**

7. **Structure the tree as first-class objects independent of layout position**, following Heptabase's card/whiteboard split — a node's identity, content, and tree relationships must not live only in its (x,y) position, so relayout, search, and multi-view display all stay possible. This also sidesteps the single biggest canvas-rot failure mode (position-as-only-record) that every abandoned-canvas source converged on.
8. **Because Riemann's tree is machine-generated and machine-maintained, it structurally avoids the "second brain graveyard" maintenance-burden failure** (nothing needs manual re-filing) — but it does NOT avoid the "false sense of comprehension from filing" risk, since users could scroll/zoom past gists without engaging. This is a genuine open risk worth flagging to the fluctuation/illusion-of-understanding workers (11, 13) rather than solving here.
9. **Treat VIKI's spatial-parser idea as inverted for Riemann**: Riemann doesn't need to *infer* structure from spatial position (the tree already has real structure) — it needs a layout algorithm that *expresses* known structure spatially, while still leaving room for a user to nudge nodes around informally later (VIKI's "tolerate ambiguity" lesson applies to any user overrides layered on top of the generated layout, not to the initial generation).
10. **Cap canvas scale honestly**: Scapple's own positioning (good for one scene, not a whole book) and the hairball evidence from `06-spatial.md` both point the same direction — the spatial view should default to a **local/ego view scoped to one document's tree**, not one canvas holding every document a user has ever fed into Riemann; cross-document spatial view (if ever built) is a distinct, harder problem that shouldn't be conflated with per-document zoom navigation.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Breadcrumb click-through rate**: how often users navigate via the breadcrumb vs. the dial — high usage validates that ancestors-as-breadcrumb (not disappearing) is doing real navigational work, per the Workflowy lesson.
- **Minimap toggle rate and correlation with task**: if users reliably turn the minimap off during rapid back-and-forth dial movements (lookup-like behavior) and on during long dwell periods (synthesis-like behavior), that's a direct behavioral confirmation of Hornbæk & Frøkjær's task-dependent trade-off, and would justify making the toggle a smarter default rather than a manual one.
- **Landmark recognition without re-reading**: track whether users navigate to a previously-visited node by clicking its visual tag before the text is legible at the current scroll/zoom position (would require eye-tracking or at least fast-click timing) — a proxy for whether the deterministic color/icon tags are actually functioning as landmarks rather than decoration.
- **Cross-level jump size distribution**: are dial movements mostly adjacent-level (L1→L2) or do users frequently jump L0→L3 directly? This should directly inform whether the "crossfade for large jumps, FLIP for adjacent" transition design (implication #1) is well-calibrated, or whether large jumps are common enough to deserve their own dedicated (not just degraded) transition.
- **Motion-discomfort proxy**: track whether users with `prefers-reduced-motion` set (a directly readable signal, zero extra friction) have measurably different session lengths or dial-usage patterns than those without — a cheap, ethically clean way to check whether the reduced-motion path is actually equally usable, not just present.

## Contradictions and caveats

- **The central gap this report surfaces — no validated text-reflow transition technique exists** — means the "transition type" recommendation above (FLIP headings + crossfade body) is a **synthesis from adjacent domains (chart animation + web engineering practice), not a cited finding**. It should be treated as a hypothesis to A/B test once v1 exists, not as settled guidance.
- **Nielsen's "≤2 disclosure levels" guidance directly conflicts with the plan's 4-level (L0-L3) dial.** This is a real tension, not resolved by this report — the breadcrumb mitigation (implication #3) is a plausible fix but unverified; it's possible 4 levels genuinely need a different navigational model (e.g., only 2 levels visible/switchable at a time, with the other 2 reachable via a secondary control) rather than one flat dial. Flag for the design-implementation phase to test directly rather than assume the mitigation works.
- **Several key sources here (Vinson 1999, Bartram et al. 1995, the full Hornbæk & Frøkjær papers, the ACM CACM spatial-hypertext piece) could not be read in full** — WebFetch failed on PDF binaries or hit paywalls (403s). Everything sourced this way is marked WEAK/secondhand above; a follow-up pass with a PDF-text-extraction-capable tool or institutional access would meaningfully strengthen the landmark-selection and overview+detail sections specifically, since those are the two most load-bearing for concrete specs.
- **The "canvas rot" and "second brain graveyard" evidence is entirely practitioner essays and product positioning, not controlled research.** It's convergent across many independent sources (which raises confidence) but none of it is peer-reviewed, and none of it isolates cause from confound (e.g., do people abandon canvases because canvases are bad, or because any PKM system gets abandoned once novelty fades, regardless of format? I found no comparative study that would distinguish these).
- **LostInZoom is an active/ongoing research program, not a finished body of results** — its "anchor theory" is a promising frame but I could not find published empirical validation of it yet (only project descriptions and a position paper); don't cite it as proven, cite it as "an active research direction that names the same problem Riemann has."
- **My proposed automatic-landmark-selection heuristic (L1 nodes + above-median-branching L2 nodes) is my own invention, not from any source** — I found no existing algorithm for automatic landmark selection in a text-abstraction tree specifically (the closest work, Vinson/Sorrows & Hirtle, is about physical/virtual spatial navigation, and LostInZoom is about cartographic generalization) — this is a genuine research gap, flagged explicitly below.

## Gaps (for a possible third wave / follow-up)

1. **No study of animated/morphing transitions for natural-language text reflow exists that I could find** — this is arguably the single most Riemann-specific unanswered question in this whole report. A follow-up could look at: subtitle/caption crossfade research (broadcast captioning has decades of practice on legible text replacement timing), reading-research on saccade disruption from text reflow (does inline content shifting harm reading the way layout shift/CLS harms web UX generally?), and any user studies from e-reader "font size change" reflow (Kindle/Apple Books reflow text on resize constantly — there may be usability data on how that's perceived, even though it's not level-of-abstraction reflow).
2. **No algorithm exists (that I found) for automatic landmark selection specifically in a generated abstraction/summary tree.** Worth a dedicated search into extractive-summarization "salience scoring" literature (TextRank, LexRank, and newer LLM-based salience/importance scoring) crossed with the wayfinding landmark criteria (permanence, distinctiveness, structural position) — this is a genuinely novel synthesis Riemann may need to do original design work on, not just borrow.
3. **Institutional/paywalled-source follow-up**: Bartram et al. 1995 (UIST), Hornbæk & Frøkjær 2001/2004 full texts, and Vinson 1999 full text, all flagged above as read only secondhand — worth a dedicated pass by a worker with ACM DL / library proxy access, since all three are directly load-bearing for the minimap and landmark specs.
4. **LLM-specific transition possibility**: since Riemann's levels are LLM-generated, an LLM could in principle be asked to generate an *explicit alignment* between L2 and L3 (which L3 sentences a given L2 gist phrase summarizes), which would supply exactly the missing "object constancy" mapping that chart-transition research assumes but text lacks. This is a genuinely novel, buildable idea (not found in any source) — worth flagging to the abstraction/build-pipeline design (01-abstraction, or direct to implementation) as a way to make FLIP-quality transitions possible for body text too, not just headings, by having `build.py` emit alignment spans alongside each gist.
5. **Comparative controlled study of infinite-canvas tools does not exist** (only practitioner essays) — if Riemann's spatial view is going to be a significant investment, a small in-house usability test (even n=3-5 with a prototype) comparing "auto-laid-out tree view" vs. "user-adjustable canvas" for the same abstraction tree would produce more decision-relevant evidence than anything currently published.
