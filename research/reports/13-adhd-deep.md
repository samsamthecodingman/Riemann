# 13 — ADHD deep dive: reading, audio, autonomy, unfinished content, thresholds, spatial, medication, strengths, transferable interventions

Researcher: claude-adhd-deep-researcher (wave 2). This fills gaps flagged by 03-adhd.md and 07-adaptive-signals.md; it does not repeat their findings (working-memory/RTV meta-analyses, bimodal reading basics, bionic reading debunk, white noise, fidgeting, RSD/Wall of Awful, learner-control meta-analyses) except where a new source adds a load-bearing detail those files didn't have.

## TL;DR

- **RSVP (word-by-word rapid serial visual presentation) is the most concrete, adult-ADHD-specific reading intervention with a real controlled study behind it**: in a 76-adult study, ADHD readers *improved* ~7% under RSVP (removing the need for saccades) while neurotypical controls *declined* ~6% under the same condition — a genuine, statistically significant crossover interaction, not just "ADHD readers do worse at baseline." This is a direct, buildable alternative reading mode for L3.
- **ADHD adults show measurably longer reading times and distinct eye-movement signatures** (more regressions, erratic saccades, reduced "pause-before-pattern" anticipation) versus controls — reading-rate assumptions in the abstraction tree's L1 pacing should not use neurotypical baselines.
- **Simultaneous audio+text ("listening while reading") is reported by ADHD adults themselves as a scaffold that *reduces* rather than increases load** — inverting the general redundancy-effect prediction from Cognitive Load Theory — via a tiny (n=2) but detailed qualitative study; natural-sounding TTS voice and word-level highlighting were specifically named as what made it work, robotic TTS was not.
- **Podcast/audio speed**: comprehension holds up to ~1.5x for lecture-style material and research on podcast-style audio specifically found no measurable comprehension cost even at 2.5x — but this is general-population data, not ADHD-specific; ADHD users' self-reported preference for 1.5–2x is attributed (plausibly, not proven) to under-stimulation/dopamine-seeking rather than a genuine comprehension benefit.
- **Choice/autonomy has real single-subject-design evidence in ADHD populations (Dunlap 1994)**: letting students choose *which* task from an equivalent menu — not whether to do it — increased on-task behavior and cut disruptive behavior, and a yoked-control condition ruled out "preference" as the sole explanation. But a 2025 qualitative study of ADHD students explicitly frames autonomy as a **double-edged sword**: the same students who lit up at having choice also lost time and orientation-energy managing that choice, and needed active teacher scaffolding to convert autonomy into benefit — this directly corroborates, from the ADHD-specific literature, what 07-adaptive-signals.md's learner-control meta-analyses found for novices generally.
- **Progress/completion indicators are a documented double-edged UX mechanism even outside ADHD**: progress bars measurably reduce completion when they reveal high remaining effort or an explicit time cost, and placing them *before* a task increases drop-off versus *after*. Layered on top of ADHD-specific findings that shame after an unfinished task increases (not decreases) subsequent avoidance, this is a real risk for any "% read" or backlog UI — but I found no study measuring this *specifically* in ADHD; treat as inference from adjacent literatures, flagged as a gap.
- **Reaction-time variability (RTV) has a direct, almost-unexplored translation into UI timeout/idle-threshold design** — I found essentially no HCI literature that has explicitly derived idle/dwell thresholds from ADHD RTV data; this remains a genuine, buildable-but-unvalidated design inference (see Gaps).
- **Spatial working memory deficits in ADHD adults are real and get worse with task difficulty and lower strategy formation** — directly relevant to Riemann's future spatial view: a poorly-structured spatial layout risks amplifying rather than compensating for an existing deficit, reinforcing 06-spatial's caution about structure.
- **Novelty-seeking in ADHD has a specific, double-edged computational mechanism** (elevated "novelty bonus" + slower reward-value updating, both partially normalized by stimulant medication) — meaning novelty as an engagement lever isn't free; it can reinforce choosing appealing-but-ineffective options. Design implication: pair any novelty affordance with a visible outcome/feedback signal, not novelty alone.
- **Medication-aware UI adaptation is confirmed, again, as a real gap**: no product or paper found that adapts an interface to time-since-dose; the pharmacokinetic literature is solid (per 03-adhd's F19) but nothing operationalizes it in software. Ethically this is genuinely fraught (see Design implications) and worth treating as a deliberate non-goal unless done with explicit, user-owned input.
- **CBT for adult ADHD (Safren) has the strongest treatment-level evidence of anything reviewed across both ADHD files**: Safren's 2010 JAMA RCT found CBT+medication produced ≥30% symptom reduction in over two-thirds of patients vs. one-third on relaxation+medication; a broader 2023–2025 meta-analytic base (17–20 RCTs) confirms benefits on ADHD symptoms, anxiety/depression, and quality of life, with effect sizes strongly dependent on control condition (waitlist g≈1.03 vs. active control g≈0.32) — meaning much of CBT's apparent benefit is generic support/structure, not CBT-specific technique. The specific mechanism that transfers to software: **externalized structure + skills training for planning/organization**, which is exactly what ADHD coaching and tools like Tiimo/Goblin.tools already do informally.

## Findings

### 1. Adult ADHD reading (not children, not dyslexia)

**F1. RSVP produces a genuine crossover interaction: it helps ADHD adult readers and hurts neurotypical adult readers on the same passages.**
A controlled study (n=76: 38 ADHD-diagnosed adults, median age 23.3; 38 neurotypical controls, mean age 19.0) had participants read 142-word passages under three conditions: normal full-page text (FULL), paced word-by-word text preserving layout (PACED), and RSVP (word-by-word, no saccades required). ADHD participants' comprehension *improved* ~6.96% under RSVP vs. saccade-requiring conditions; controls *declined* ~5.82% under the same manipulation — a "relative benefit" of ~12.78 percentage points for the ADHD group, and the group×condition interaction was statistically significant (p=0.022). No significant main effect of overall reading time on comprehension was found, i.e. it's specifically about removing eye-movement demands, not just changing pacing.
Evidence: MODERATE (single study, n=76, but a genuine controlled crossover design directly on adults, not children/dyslexia, published in Journal of the International Neuropsychological Society).
Source: [Reading without eye movements: Improving reading comprehension in young adults with ADHD — Cambridge Core / J Int Neuropsychol Soc](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-international-neuropsychological-society/article/reading-without-eye-movements-improving-reading-comprehension-in-young-adults-with-attentiondeficithyperactivity-disorder-adhd/33851CEA7C1AC6193D088D4D5551ED3C)

**F2. ADHD adults show longer overall reading times and a distinctive eye-movement signature versus matched controls, including reduced anticipatory pausing.**
Stern, Kolodny, Tsafrir, Cohen & Shalev (2024, *Journal of Attention Disorders* 28(6):1008–1016) recorded eye movements during uninterrupted text reading in ADHD vs. typically-developing (TD) adults and found significantly longer reading times for the ADHD group. A separate summary (from search aggregation, not independently opened at source) notes neurotypical adults show anticipatory pausing before predictable text patterns that ADHD adults do not show to the same degree — consistent with reduced predictive/top-down control during reading, distinct from a simple "slower reader" account.
Evidence: MODERATE (peer-reviewed, adult sample, but I read this via search-result synthesis and the Cambridge companion paper rather than the full SAGE text, which was paywalled/403).
Source: [Unique Patterns of Eye Movements Characterizing Inattentive Reading in ADHD — SAGE / J Attention Disorders](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/10870547231223728)

**F3. Text length interacts with ADHD reading difficulty, but the literature explicitly flags inconsistent findings across studies, attributed to individual differences and measurement-tool variance.**
A conference paper (ICLS 2022 workshop short paper) on "Sources of difficulties in reading comprehension in adults with ADHD" frames text length, complexity, meaningfulness/engagement, and attentional demand as jointly determining difficulty, explicitly noting that some studies find ADHD-related comprehension deficits and others find null results, and attributes this to individual heterogeneity within ADHD and to differences in how comprehension is assessed across studies. I could not fully load the PDF (timeout) so this is reported at abstract/summary level only.
Evidence: WEAK/PRELIMINARY (short workshop paper, not independently verified beyond search synthesis; explicitly flags its own field's inconsistency).
Source: [Sources of difficulties in reading comprehension in adults with ADHD — ISLS repository (ICLS 2022 short paper)](https://repository.isls.org/bitstream/1/8895/1/ICLS2022_921-924.pdf)

**F4. Mind-wandering during reading has a general (non-ADHD-specific) eye-movement signature — reduced regressions, reduced sensitivity to word length/frequency, longer/less-responsive fixations — which is a plausible passive detection channel, but the ADHD-specific eye-tracking literature has not yet been cross-validated against this general mind-wandering signature in a single study.**
A 2024/2025 meta-analysis specifically on eye-movement markers of mind-wandering during reading (general population) establishes the pattern (skipping more words, fewer fixations, reduced word-length/frequency sensitivity, fewer corrective regressions during "mindless reading" episodes). Separately, ADHD trait research finds all three ADHD symptom clusters (inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity) predict higher spontaneous mind-wandering rates, with hyperactive traits predicting wandering even on easy tasks and inattentive traits predicting it specifically on difficult tasks. These are two separate literatures I found converging in direction but not yet merged into one ADHD-specific eye-tracking mind-wandering study.
Evidence: MODERATE for each literature separately; WEAK for the specific combination (inference, not a directly-tested claim).
Sources: [Eye-movement markers of mind wandering during reading: A meta-analysis — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41107699/), [Mind wandering during attention performance: ADHD-inattention symptomatology — PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5524389/)

### 2. Audio for ADHD

**F5. Simultaneous text+audio is described by ADHD adults themselves as reducing cognitive load via a "scaffold" mechanism — this appears to contradict the general redundancy-effect prediction from Cognitive Load Theory, and 03-adhd.md's F7 already flags this tension for weaker readers generically; this finding sharpens it for ADHD specifically.**
A small phenomenological study (n=2 ADHD adults, ages 22 and 57, thematic analysis of journals + interviews) using AI-narrated TTS with word-level highlighting found participants used it selectively for "dense, complex texts... long emails" and reported the fixed narration pace prevented skimming/internal distraction ("forced me to slow down and go with this predefined pace"), that natural-sounding voice was less taxing and more engaging than robotic TTS, and that dual-channel visual+auditory presentation with synchronized highlighting "reinforced retention and sustained engagement more effectively than traditional reading alone." Participants also valued knowing reading duration in advance for planning (a time-blindness countermeasure).
Evidence: WEAK/PRELIMINARY (n=2, self-selected from ADHD online communities, phenomenological not experimental) but directly on-topic and consistent with the broader (non-ADHD-specific) bimodal reading literature in 03-adhd's F7.
Source: [ADHD Adults' Experiences in Digital Reading Environments — EdTech Archives](https://edtecharchives.org/journal/2222/20815)

**F6. Podcast-style audio comprehension tolerates speed increases surprisingly well in general populations, up to and including 2.5x in at least one study — but no ADHD-specific controlled comprehension study at speed was found.**
General findings: comprehension/memory is roughly flat from 1x–1.5x, drops noticeably above ~1.5x for lecture-style video/audio, and the "average adult can readily comprehend spoken audio at about 2x speed" (~275 wpm). One cited study presenting podcast-style audio clips at 1x/1.5x/2x/2.5x found *no* measurable test-performance impairment even at 2.5x, though the source still advises not exceeding 2x. ADHD-specific commentary attributes self-reported preference for 1.5–2x playback to under-stimulation of an "underactive dopamine system" — increasing information density per unit time to prevent attentional drift — but this is an explanatory hypothesis in secondary/practitioner sources, not something directly tested against comprehension outcomes in an ADHD sample.
Evidence: MODERATE for the general-population speed/comprehension curve; WEAK/PRELIMINARY (practitioner explanation, not tested) for the specific ADHD dopamine-driven-speed-preference claim.
Sources: [The Effect of Playback Speed and Distractions on Comprehension of Audio and Audio-Visual Materials — Educational Psychology Review (abstract/search synthesis; full text was paywalled behind Springer auth)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-024-09917-7), [Possible ADHD Behavior: Listening to Audiobooks and Podcasts Faster — ADHD Training Center](https://adhdtrainingcenter.com/possible-adhd-behavior-listening-to-audiobooks-and-podcasts-faster/)

**F7. Comorbid auditory processing difficulties are common enough in ADHD to be a real design constraint on any audio-first feature, not an edge case.**
Frequently cited clinical literature places co-occurrence of central auditory processing disorder (CAPD/APD) with ADHD at roughly 50% in both directions (about half of children with ADHD also show APD; about half of children with diagnosed APD also meet ADHD criteria), against a base APD prevalence of only 2–7% in the general population. This is predominantly pediatric literature; I did not find an adult-specific prevalence figure, but the mechanism (competing bottom-up auditory discrimination difficulty alongside top-down attentional dysregulation) has no obvious reason to fully resolve in adulthood.
Evidence: MODERATE for the comorbidity association (multiple converging clinical sources); WEAK for adult-specific base rates (extrapolated from pediatric data, flagged explicitly as a gap).
Sources: [Comorbidity of Central Auditory Processing Disorder and ADHD — ScienceDirect/JAACAP](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0890856709642878), [ADHD and Auditory Processing: Understanding the Connection — ADDA](https://add.org/adhd-and-auditory-processing/)

**F8. General dual-task/divided-attention research supports why voice-while-reading can interfere: same-modality or same-cortical-resource tasks compete, and cognitive load theory's redundancy principle predicts a comprehension cost from simultaneous audio+text, especially for syntactically complex passages — directly in tension with F5's ADHD-specific self-report.**
General (non-ADHD) dual-task literature: performing two tasks simultaneously typically produces slower, more error-prone performance than either task alone, and interference is worse when both tasks draw on the same processing modality/cortical resources (e.g., two language tasks). A specific "listening while reading" (LWR) literature review found some studies show concurrent written+spoken text interfering with comprehension, with negative effects worse for syntactically complex passages requiring more processing resources — this is the CLT redundancy-effect prediction 03-adhd.md's F7 already flagged, now with an explicit named-effect source.
Evidence: MODERATE for general dual-task interference (well-established, multiply replicated); MODERATE for the LWR redundancy-effect literature specifically, though findings there are described as genuinely mixed rather than uniformly negative.
Sources: [The Effects of LWR (Listening While Reading) and Intrinsic [Cognitive Load] — ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1457426.pdf), general dual-task synthesis from search aggregation (Oxford Academic *Cerebral Cortex*, Springer *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics*).

**Cross-reading F5 and F8**: the unresolved tension 03-adhd.md already named (bimodal audio+text helping weaker readers vs. CLT's redundancy-effect prediction) is *not* resolved by this deeper pass — if anything it's sharper, because F5's mechanism claim ("scaffold that reduces load") is the opposite causal story from F8's mechanism claim ("redundant channel competing for the same resource, worse for complex syntax"). Both are plausible and neither source directly refutes the other; this is a genuine open empirical question best resolved by Riemann's own instrumentation (see Signals) rather than by picking a side from the literature.

### 3. Autonomy and choice in ADHD

**F9. Choice-of-task (not choice-of-whether) increases on-task behavior and reduces disruptive behavior in ADHD/EBD students, with a yoked-control design ruling out simple preference as the explanation.**
Dunlap et al. (1994, *Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis*) gave 2 students (ADHD/EBD) choices from a menu of academic tasks equally relevant to their curricular goals; reversal (ABAB-style) designs showed choice conditions increased task engagement and decreased disruptive behavior versus teacher-assigned conditions. A third participant's design added a yoked-control phase (same tasks as a previous choice session, but assigned rather than chosen) — the choice condition still outperformed the yoked condition, meaning the effect isn't reducible to "students like the tasks they'd pick anyway"; the *act* of choosing carries independent value. A later systematic review of choice-making interventions (Ramsey et al., in *Review Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*) confirms choice conditions outperform no-choice conditions in ADHD, EBD, dementia, and TBI populations.
Evidence: MODERATE (small single-subject designs, but methodologically rigorous with reversal/yoked controls, replicated conceptually across populations in the later review).
Sources: [Choice making to promote adaptive behavior for students with emotional and behavioral challenges — Dunlap et al. 1994, JABA](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1901/jaba.1994.27-505), [To Choose or Not to Choose? Systematic review of choice effects — Review Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40489-018-00154-7)

**F10. Autonomy is a genuine double-edged sword for ADHD learners specifically — this is the direct ADHD-literature corroboration of 07-adaptive-signals.md's general learner-control meta-analysis finding (control helps confident/skilled learners, hurts novices navigating without guidance).**
A 2025 qualitative interview study (2 students with inattentive-type ADHD/attention difficulties + their teachers, in a Norwegian intervention combining adapted education + self-regulated learning + teacher autonomy support) found students *valued* autonomy strongly ("Finally, I get to decide for myself, then I'm happy!") and used it adaptively (one student sequenced easy tasks first to build momentum before harder ones) — but the same autonomy created real costs: one student "lost time during self-selected breaks," the other "expended energy orienting himself rather than completing work," and both struggled to make volitional choices and stay on track inside the flexible structure. The paper's own framing: autonomy support that relies on self-regulation is "a double-edged sword" for ADHD specifically, because self-regulated learning taxes exactly the executive processes ADHD impairs. The critical success factor identified was *not* removing autonomy but pairing it with active, ongoing teacher dialogue that helped students build self-awareness of what kind of autonomy they could actually use well.
Evidence: MODERATE (small qualitative sample, n=2, but rich process data with direct student quotes, published 2025 in *Frontiers in Psychology*, directly on the target population and directly on the mechanism in question).
Source: [Students with inattention and their experiences of autonomy in learning activities — PMC / Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12593520/)

**Reconciling F9/F10 with 07's learner-control meta-analyses**: the reconciliation those meta-analyses point toward ("system-led by default, transparent, easy override") gets a specific ADHD-flavored refinement here: the *type* of choice matters. Dunlap's effect is choice among a bounded, curated menu of equally-valid options (low-stakes, low-navigation-cost choice) — closer to "which document/level do you want to start with" than "navigate this space with no guardrails." The 2025 study's costs cluster specifically around *unstructured* self-pacing (managing breaks, orienting within open-ended flexibility) — closer to "figure out your own path through an undifferentiated space." This suggests Riemann's actual design (one continuous dial, bounded levels, system suggests a starting point) sits closer to the Dunlap "bounded menu" pattern than to the "wide-open self-regulation" pattern the autonomy study flags as risky — a reassuring but not directly-tested inference.

### 4. Unfinished content

**F11. Progress/completion visualizations measurably backfire under specific, well-characterized conditions — none of this research is ADHD-specific, but the mechanisms plausibly compound with ADHD-specific shame/avoidance dynamics already documented in 03-adhd.md (F16/F17, RSD and Wall of Awful).**
A synthesis (Irrational Labs, behavioral-science consultancy, citing a meta-analysis of 32 experiments and their own A/B tests) identifies four backfire mechanisms: (1) progress bars that reveal high perceived early effort reduce completion; (2) explicitly stating time-to-complete (e.g., "10 minutes") reduces completion versus not stating it; (3) long, mostly-empty progress bars demotivate versus giving early "endowed progress" or smaller milestones; (4) progress-bar placement *before* a task increases drop-off versus placement *after* (SurveyMonkey field data). None of this is peer-reviewed primary research in the strict sense (practitioner/applied-behavioral-science synthesis) but it draws on a cited academic meta-analysis for point (1).
Evidence: MODERATE for point (1) (meta-analytic backing cited); WEAK/PRELIMINARY for points (2)-(4) (single-source practitioner claims, not independently verified against primary literature here).
Source: [Knowledge Cuts Both Ways: When Progress Bars Backfire — Irrational Labs](https://irrationallabs.com/blog/knowledge-cuts-both-ways-when-progress-bars-backfire/)

**F12. Shame following an unfinished/avoided task increases — not decreases — the odds of avoiding the next one, mediated by rumination rather than acting as a direct deterrent.**
General (non-ADHD) procrastination research finds shame-proneness correlates with procrastination, and a specific mechanism study found the relationship between shame and procrastination is mediated by rumination/brooding: shame doesn't motivate completion, it triggers rumination that increases subsequent avoidance, i.e. self-criticism is not a working deterrent — it compounds the avoidance cycle. 03-adhd.md's F16/F17 already establish RSD and "Wall of Awful" as ADHD-specific shame/avoidance constructs (practitioner-level, not clinical diagnoses); this finding is the general-population mechanism study that would explain *why* those ADHD-specific patterns work the way practitioners describe, though no one has run the rumination-mediation study in an ADHD sample specifically.
Evidence: MODERATE for the general shame→rumination→avoidance mechanism (published in *Journal of Rational-Emotive & Cognitive-Behavior Therapy*); WEAK for its application to ADHD specifically (inference bridging two literatures, not directly tested together).
Source: [Does Rumination Mediate the Unique Effects of Shame and Guilt on Procrastination? — Springer/PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35847054/)

**F13. Zeigarnik/Ovsiankina "unfinished tasks stay in mind" effect is frequently invoked in ADHD lived-experience writing as a source of chronic background cognitive load from accumulated open loops — but this application is explicitly practitioner/lived-experience commentary, not a tested finding.**
The Zeigarnik effect (better memory for interrupted/incomplete tasks) and the related Ovsiankina effect (intrusive urge to resume unfinished tasks) are established general-psychology findings. ADHD-focused blogs and coaching sites (I'm Busy Being Awesome, ADHD Time) extend this to argue that ADHD's larger number of simultaneously-open tasks means a correspondingly larger number of Zeigarnik-style intrusive reminders, compounding into generalized background anxiety/overwhelm — this is a plausible, widely-repeated extrapolation in the ADHD community, explicitly lived-experience-tier, not a tested claim about ADHD populations' actual Zeigarnik-effect magnitude.
Evidence: MODERATE for the base Zeigarnik/Ovsiankina effects (classic, replicated psychology); WEAK/PRELIMINARY (lived-experience/practitioner extrapolation, clearly labelled) for the ADHD-specific "more open loops = more background load" claim.
Sources: [The Zeigarnik Effect & ADHD — I'm Busy Being Awesome (lived-experience/coaching)](https://imbusybeingawesome.com/zeigarnik-effect-adhd-unfinished-tasks/), [The psychology of unfinished tasks: Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects — Ness Labs](https://nesslabs.com/unfinished-tasks)

### 5. Engagement thresholds

**F14. RTV (reaction-time variability) is a well-established ADHD marker (already covered in depth in 03-adhd.md F2), but I found essentially no HCI literature that has explicitly translated RTV distributions into concrete idle-timeout, dwell-threshold, or auto-pause parameters for a real interface.**
Search across ScienceDirect, Neurotherapeutics, and general HCI/UX sources turned up general statements that "session timeouts and auto-logouts are particularly difficult [for ADHD users] due to time blindness" and that behavioral cues (app usage, tab switching, idle time) can in principle be fed into ML models to detect attentional state changes — but no source specified actual threshold numbers, distributions, or a validated design rule derived from RTV data. This is a confirmed, specific gap: the underlying cognitive-science finding (F2 in 03-adhd.md, g≈0.46–0.76 elevated RTV) is strong, but its translation into "how long should Riemann wait before treating a pause as idle vs. a lapse" has not been done anywhere I could find.
Evidence: N/A (gap observation, not a claim to rate).
Sources: [Reaction Time Variability in ADHD: A Review — Neurotherapeutics](https://www.neurotherapeuticsjournal.org/article/S1878-7479(23)01725-7/fulltext), [UI/UX and ADHD: Designing for Focus in a Distracted World — DesignMonks (practitioner blog, not research)](https://www.designmonks.co/blog/ui-ux-and-adhd)

### 6. Spatial navigation and ADHD

**F15. Adults with ADHD show measurable spatial working memory deficits that scale with task difficulty, plus impaired strategy formation — not just a raw capacity deficit but a *how-you-approach-the-problem* deficit.**
A controlled neuropsychological study (ScienceDirect) found adults diagnosed with ADHD were significantly impaired on spatial working memory tasks relative to controls, with the impairment increasing as task difficulty increased, and were also more impaired specifically on measures of *strategy formation* — i.e., not just holding spatial information in mind, but developing an efficient approach to searching/encoding it. Separately, real-world wayfinding commentary (secondary/practitioner sources, not independently verified as primary research here) describes ADHD adults getting disoriented even in familiar spaces, attributed to fluctuating (not fixed) working-memory/attention resources rather than a fixed trait-level spatial deficit — i.e., the same person may navigate well one day and poorly the next.
Evidence: MODERATE for the core spatial-working-memory-and-strategy-formation deficit (peer-reviewed neuropsychological study); WEAK for the "fluctuates day to day" wayfinding framing (practitioner/secondary-source synthesis, plausible but not independently sourced to a primary study here).
Sources: [Spatial working memory and strategy formation in adults diagnosed with ADHD — ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S019188690600122X), [The neural correlates of spatial attention and working memory deficits in adults with ADHD — PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6396015/)

**Design implication flag**: this is a direct, substantive caution for Riemann's planned spatial view. The baseline research already states "spatial layout can also hurt if poorly structured" — F15 sharpens *why*, specifically for this user: the deficit isn't just capacity, it's strategy formation under difficulty, meaning a spatial UI that requires the user to invent their own search/traversal strategy (rather than one with strong, consistent, pre-structured landmarks per the baseline's own recommendations) is likely to cost this specific population disproportionately more than a neurotypical user, especially as the space/document set grows (increasing "task difficulty" in the F15 sense).

### 7. Medication-aware UIs

**F16. No product or HCI paper adapting its interface to time-since-last-dose was found; the pharmacokinetic literature (already covered in 03-adhd.md F19) remains the strongest part of this picture and the software-adaptation part remains unbuilt anywhere I could locate.**
Search across pharmacology, HCI, and app-review sources confirmed the pharmacokinetic pattern (onset ~2 hours for typical stimulant dosing before a routine, 12-hour persistent effect for extended-release formulations like MAS XR with a pharmacokinetic profile comparable to twice-daily immediate-release dosing) but surfaced no app, product, or published HCI system that ingests or infers dose-timing and visibly adapts its own behavior accordingly. The closest adjacent thing found (AiCure) is a medication-adherence monitoring app, not a cognitive-adaptation app — it watches *whether* medication was taken, not how the interface should change in response to being in a different part of the pharmacokinetic curve.
Evidence: N/A (gap observation).
Sources: [Once-daily medications for the pharmacological management of ADHD in adults — PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2697545/), [Long-acting stimulants for ADHD: extended-release formulations — PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3751218/)

**F17. The cognitive-enhancement ethics literature (general, not app-specific) frames stimulant-driven cognitive variation as an area requiring explicit study of risk/benefit/moral consequence before broad design decisions are built around it — a caution that transfers directly to any medication-timing-aware feature.**
General bioethics commentary on stimulants-as-cognitive-enhancers calls for deliberate study of expected risks, benefits, and moral consequences before normalizing design or policy built around pharmacological cognitive state. Applied to Riemann: an interface that silently infers "you're probably past your dose window, here's a simplified view" risks (a) being wrong and patronizing, (b) implicitly pathologizing or surveilling a private medical fact, and (c) creating a dependency loop where the *software's* model of the user's medication state substitutes for the user's own self-knowledge — in direct tension with Riemann's own stated principle that load capacity should be user-reported, not silently inferred. The existing 03-adhd.md recommendation (F19 → design implication 11: manual, cheap "how much capacity right now" input, not inferred) is the ethically safer version of the same underlying signal, and this pass finds nothing in the literature that would justify overriding that caution.
Evidence: WEAK (general ethics commentary, not empirical; a reasoned extension rather than a tested claim) but treated here as a strong design constraint regardless, given the stakes of getting it wrong.
Source: general synthesis from search aggregation on cognitive-enhancement ethics literature (no single primary source opened; flagged accordingly).

### 8. ADHD strengths

**F18. Divergent thinking (not convergent thinking) is measurably, if modestly, elevated with ADHD symptom load, across both population-based and clinical case-control samples, with a possible inverted-U (too much symptom severity stops helping).**
A study combining a population-based sample (n=470, subsample of 215 with full creativity data, mean age 36.1) and a clinical case-control sample (n=151 adults, 79 ADHD-diagnosed) found ADHD symptoms positively associated with divergent-thinking scores (fluency, flexibility, originality; r=0.17–0.22, p<0.012) but *not* with convergent thinking, and diagnosed ADHD cases scored higher on divergent thinking than controls. Combining datasets suggested an inverted-U relationship — moderate ADHD symptom levels associate with peak divergent thinking, with the relationship flattening or reversing at higher symptom levels. Effect sizes are modest (regression models explained only 2–7% of variance) — a real but not large effect.
Evidence: MODERATE (real adult samples, both population-based and clinical, published in *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 2022, but modest effect sizes and the inverted-U claim is exploratory/combined-dataset rather than independently replicated).
Source: [Characterizing Creative Thinking and Creative Achievements in Relation to Symptoms of ADHD and ASD — Frontiers in Psychiatry](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.909202/full)

**F19. Novelty-seeking in ADHD has a specific computational signature (elevated "novelty bonus" + slower reward-learning-rate updates) that psychostimulants partially normalize — meaning novelty-as-engagement-lever is a double-edged mechanism, not a free win.**
A neurocomputational modeling study found unmedicated ADHD participants showed both a heightened initial value ("novelty bonus") assigned to unfamiliar options and slower updating of reward values from feedback, jointly producing persistent selection of novel-but-suboptimal options even after repeated poor outcomes — localized to elevated novelty signaling in the substantia nigra/ventral tegmental area. Methylphenidate selectively remediated this in ADHD participants (increased learning rate, reduced novelty signaling, better discrimination of genuinely good novel options) without equivalent effects in healthy controls, and sustained treatment correlated with durable reductions in novelty overvaluation.
Evidence: MODERATE (neuroimaging + computational modeling study, mechanistically detailed, but a single study — the specific "novelty bonus" quantification hasn't been described here as independently replicated).
Source: [A neurocomputational account of reward and novelty processing and effects of psychostimulants in ADHD — PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5917772/)

**Design implication flag from F19**: this directly qualifies "design for novelty-seeking as a strength" — the same mechanism that makes novelty an effective hook for ADHD users is the mechanism that can trap them in low-value exploration loops (e.g., compulsively opening new documents/levels rather than following through on one). The buildable version of "designing for the strength" is therefore: let novelty draw attention *in*, but close the loop with a fast, visible outcome/feedback signal (so the reward-learning-rate deficit gets help from the interface rather than being left to run unaided) — consistent with, and reinforcing, 07-adaptive-signals.md's finding that frequent low-stakes feedback loops are the single most load-bearing lever available.

### 9. Interventions with evidence that transfer to software

**F20. Safren's CBT-for-adult-ADHD protocol has the single strongest RCT-level evidence found across either ADHD-focused report in this fleet, and its active ingredient (externalized planning/organizing structure + skills training, on top of medication) is directly analogous to what a well-designed productivity/comprehension tool would need to provide.**
Safren et al.'s 2010 JAMA-published RCT found medicated adults with ADHD who added a structured CBT course (targeting organization/planning, distractibility management, adaptive thinking) had significantly better symptom control than those given relaxation training as an active control: a standardized ADHD symptom scale showed a ≥30% reduction in more than two-thirds of the CBT group versus only one-third of the relaxation group. A broader synthesis (17–20 RCTs through 2023–2025) confirms CBT improves core ADHD symptoms, anxiety, depression, emotion dysregulation, and quality of life — but effect sizes are strongly dependent on the comparison condition: waitlist control g≈1.03, treatment-as-usual g≈0.66, *active* control (matched attention/support) g≈0.32. That drop from waitlist to active-control effect size is itself an important finding: a large fraction of CBT's apparent benefit is generic structure/attention/support, not CBT's specific cognitive-restructuring technique — which is good news for a non-therapy software product, because it suggests structure and consistent scaffolding alone (without needing to replicate therapeutic technique) can carry real weight.
Evidence: STRONG for CBT's overall benefit over passive controls (multiple RCTs, meta-analytic synthesis); MODERATE for the specific magnitude attributable to CBT's unique technique versus generic structure (the active-control comparison is the more honest/conservative estimate and it's meaningfully smaller).
Sources: [The efficacy of cognitive-behavioral therapy for adults with ADHD — PMC (2025 meta-analysis)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12434339/), [A meta-analysis of the intervention effect of CBT on adult ADHD — ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032725025492), [Cognitive-behavioural therapy in medication-treated adults with ADHD — PMC (Safren-tradition RCT)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4595859/)

**F21. External scaffolding (coaching, organizational-skills training) works best with active monitoring/coaching layered on top of any tool — a "install the tool and walk away" pattern under-delivers relative to a "tool plus check-in" pattern, at least for working-memory-training analogues.**
ADHD coaching is characterized in practitioner/clinical-adjacent sources as structured external scaffolding targeting the *symptoms* of executive dysfunction (missed deadlines, disorganization, time blindness, task-initiation failure) rather than the underlying neurobiology — i.e., it doesn't try to fix working memory, it builds an external system around its limits. The clearest controlled-evidence analogue is computerized working-memory training (Cogmed): reviews find its real-world efficacy is closely tied to the amount of coaching/monitoring provided alongside the software (by clinicians or caregivers in trials), and that adult uptake specifically needs more structure/support than the pediatric trials provided, or adherence and results both suffer.
Evidence: MODERATE for the general "coaching/monitoring materially changes software-based intervention outcomes" pattern (multiple converging reviews); this is an analogy from working-memory-training literature to ADHD coaching broadly, not a direct RCT of coached-vs-uncoached use of a reading/summarization tool specifically.
Sources: [ADHD Coaching: What the Evidence Actually Shows — Dr. Ryan Sultan (practitioner synthesis of clinical literature)](https://ryansultan.com/blog/blog-adhd-coaching-evidence), [Cogmed Working Memory Training Presents Unique Implementation Challenges in Adults With ADHD — PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6121173/), [A Review of Working Memory Training in the Management of ADHD — PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8334010/)

## Design implications for Riemann

1. **Offer an RSVP-style reading mode as a genuine, evidence-backed alternative to normal text at L3** (F1) — not a gimmick, a specific intervention with a real crossover effect for exactly this population reading exactly this kind of dense material. Make it opt-in and per-session (the same study shows it can hurt neurotypical readers, and Sam's own state will vary), and let the user toggle it mid-passage without losing position — this pairs naturally with the zoom dial's existing "anchor the centre of viewport" mechanic.
2. **Don't calibrate L1's target read-time or word-count budget from neurotypical reading-speed assumptions** (F2) — ADHD adults read measurably slower with more regressions on unfamiliar/dense text; a "1–2 minute" L1 budget should be validated against Sam's own behavior (see Signals), not a population reading-speed constant.
3. **Build synced-audio (word-highlighted TTS with a natural-sounding voice) as a first-class mode, not an accessibility afterthought** (F5), but instrument it to test the CLT-redundancy-vs-ADHD-scaffold tension directly (F8) rather than assuming either literature's prediction — Riemann is well positioned to let Sam's own comprehension/dwell data settle an argument the literature hasn't resolved.
4. **Default audio playback speed control to a 1x–1.5x safe zone, expose but don't default to 2x+** (F6) — general-population data supports up to 1.5x with no real cost and even 2–2.5x podcast-style audio shows little measured comprehension cost, but there's no ADHD-specific controlled comprehension-at-speed study, so treat higher speeds as a user-chosen accelerant, not an assumed-safe default.
5. **Give the user a curated menu of starting points, not an open unstructured space, when they open a new document** (F9, F10) — e.g., "start at L0", "jump to a section", "let the dial pick" as a small bounded choice set. This is the version of autonomy with actual ADHD-specific supporting evidence (Dunlap); avoid designs that require the user to invent their own navigation strategy from scratch, which is exactly where the 2025 autonomy study found ADHD-specific cost.
6. **Never show a bare "% read" or "time remaining" progress bar as the primary framing of an in-progress document, and never place one at the top/before the content** (F11) — if a progress indicator exists at all, place it passively at the bottom/after, avoid stating a large remaining-time number up front, and consider "endowed progress" framing (e.g., "L0 done" already counts as real progress, not 0%).
7. **Make returning to unfinished content shame-neutral, explicitly** (F12, F13, and 03-adhd.md's F16/F17): no red badges, no "you have 14 unfinished items" language, no visual pile-up of abandoned documents. Given F12's rumination-mediated mechanism, any UI that triggers self-critical reflection is actively counterproductive, not neutral — the safer framing is closer to "here's where you left off" (informational) than "here's what you didn't finish" (evaluative).
8. **Treat idle/dwell/timeout thresholds as a genuinely open design parameter, not a solved one** (F14) — there is no validated number to copy from the literature. Recommend starting generous (long idle tolerance before any "are you still there" or auto-pause behavior) and letting Riemann's own logged data calibrate per-user thresholds over time, rather than importing a neurotypical default.
9. **If/when the spatial view ships, invest disproportionately in pre-built, consistent landmarks over emergent/self-organizing spatial structure** (F15) — the baseline research already recommends this, but F15 gives a mechanistic reason specific to Sam: the deficit is strategy-formation under difficulty, so a space that requires the user to develop their own search strategy will cost more as the space grows, exactly when a spatial view is supposed to be paying off.
10. **Do not build silent medication-timing inference. If a capacity signal is wanted, keep it manual and user-owned** (F16, F17) — this reinforces, with an ethics rationale, the existing 03-adhd.md recommendation; treat any temptation to infer "probably past your dose window" from behavior alone as a design smell, not a feature to build toward.
11. **Pair any novelty-driving affordance (new document type, surprising connection surfaced, "explore this" prompt) with a fast, visible outcome signal** (F19) — e.g., immediately show what following the novel path got you, so the interface helps close the reward-learning loop rather than leaving the user's slower reward-updating to work unaided. Novelty alone, without feedback, risks becoming a distraction generator rather than a genuine engagement lever.
12. **Design the "help me get unstuck" affordance (already recommended per W3C COGA in 03-adhd.md) around externalized structure, not motivation** (F20, F21) — the CBT and coaching evidence base converges on organizational scaffolding (breaking things down, making the next step visible, checking in) as the transferable mechanism, not inspiration or gamified motivation. This is closest in spirit to Goblin.tools' task-breakdown pattern (already flagged as market-validated in 03-adhd.md F15) than to a streaks/rewards system.
13. **Consider that a purely self-serve, unmonitored tool may under-deliver relative to one with any check-in/reflection loop** (F21) — even a lightweight, low-friction version (e.g., a weekly "here's what you actually read vs. what you meant to" reflection surface) is more consistent with the coaching/CBT evidence base than assuming the tool alone, unused, will sustain behavior change.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **RSVP-mode adoption and subsequent comprehension/completion rate vs. normal-text mode**, per user and per passage type — directly tests F1's transferability from the lab study to Sam's actual reading.
- **Actual per-user reading-time-per-word at each level**, compared against whatever population constant the L1 budget currently assumes — calibrates F2/F3's "don't assume neurotypical pacing" concern with real data instead of guesswork.
- **A/B or self-selected comparison of synced-audio-on vs. off, measured against dwell time, reversal count, and self-reported comprehension** — the most direct way to resolve the F5/F8 tension (scaffold vs. redundancy) for this one user rather than trusting either literature's population-level prediction.
- **Time-to-resume and re-engagement rate for documents left mid-level**, segmented by whether any progress/status indicator was shown vs. not — a natural, low-friction test of F11/F12's progress-bar-backfire and shame-avoidance predictions inside Riemann's own usage.
- **Idle-gap distribution per user, per session, per time-of-day** — the raw data needed to eventually derive a personalized idle/dwell threshold (F14), rather than importing an arbitrary constant; log gap length and whether the user resumed at the same spot or restarted, to distinguish "true idle" from "brief lapse."
- **Novel-path click-through followed by immediate abandonment vs. follow-through** — a direct behavioral proxy for F19's "novelty bonus without payoff" risk; if Sam clicks into novel/tangential content and bounces quickly and repeatedly, that's the signature the neurocomputational model predicts, and it's a cue to surface outcome feedback faster, not to suppress novelty affordances.

## Contradictions and caveats

- **The F5/F8 tension (audio-as-scaffold vs. audio-as-redundant-load) is real and unresolved in the literature reviewed here**, not just in 03-adhd.md's earlier pass — F5's ADHD-specific qualitative account and F8's general dual-task/redundancy-effect account make opposite mechanistic predictions, and I found no study that puts them head-to-head in an ADHD sample. Treat this as an empirical question for Riemann to answer with its own users, not a settled design choice.
- **F9's Dunlap evidence (choice helps) and F10's autonomy study (self-directed pacing has real costs) are not actually in tension once the *type* of choice is distinguished** — but a careless reading of "choice helps ADHD" from F9 alone, without F10's qualification, would overstate how much unstructured autonomy Riemann should offer. The reconciliation offered here (bounded-menu choice vs. open-ended self-regulation) is my inference bridging two small, non-identical studies, not something either paper states directly.
- **Small-sample and single-study risk is unusually high in this pass**: F5 (n=2), F9 (n=2-3 single-subject), F10 (n=2), F15's wayfinding "fluctuates day to day" framing (uncited secondary claim). These are flagged individually with WEAK/PRELIMINARY tags, but collectively this deep-dive leans more heavily on small qualitative/practitioner sources than 03-adhd.md's original pass did — a reflection of how thin the *adult*, *ADHD-specific* literature genuinely is on several of these sub-questions (reading, autonomy, unfinished content specifically), not a methodological shortcut taken here.
- **F18's creativity/divergent-thinking effect is real but modest (2–7% variance explained)** — worth taking seriously as a design principle (don't only design around deficits) but not worth over-indexing on; it is not evidence that ADHD confers a large or reliable creative advantage in this specific reading/comprehension context.
- **F19 complicates a naive "design for novelty-seeking as a strength" recommendation** — this is a genuine internal correction within this same report: novelty-seeking is not simply a strength to be unlocked, it's a mechanism with a documented failure mode (persistent selection of appealing-but-suboptimal novel options) that specifically improves with the very medication/reward-feedback normalization this report elsewhere argues Riemann shouldn't try to infer or substitute for. The safest synthesis is "novelty draws attention in, feedback closes the loop" rather than "maximize novelty."
- **F16/F17 (medication-aware UI) remain a confirmed gap rather than a finding** — this deep pass looked specifically for existing implementations and ethics literature and found essentially nothing built, and only general (not ADHD-app-specific) ethics commentary. This should be read as "still unexplored," not "explored and ruled out."
- **Adult-specific data is still thinner than pediatric data across several sub-questions here** (APD comorbidity rates in F7, wayfinding-fluctuation claims in F15) — consistent with 03-adhd.md's own caveat about the adult evidence base generally; don't assume pediatric magnitudes transfer directly to Sam.

## Gaps (for the next wave)

1. **No study directly tests RSVP or synced-audio against Riemann's actual UI pattern (a zoom dial across abstraction levels)** — both F1 and F5 are evidence for the *component* interventions, but nobody has tested them inside a semantic-zoom/progressive-disclosure interface specifically. This is the same "genuinely novel territory" gap 03-adhd.md flagged in its own gap #1, restated here with two additional concrete mechanisms (RSVP, synced audio) that a future study or even an internal Riemann A/B test could target directly.
2. **Idle/dwell/timeout threshold research derived from RTV data (F14) is essentially unbuilt anywhere** — this is a specific, scoped, and genuinely valuable thing for either a future HCI-literature-search worker or for Riemann's own instrumentation to tackle directly, since no existing product appears to have solved it.
3. **The redundancy-effect-vs-scaffold tension (F5/F8) deserves a dedicated, well-powered search** beyond what this pass could do — specifically searching cognitive-load-theory venues (Sweller-adjacent journals) crossed with ADHD samples specifically, not just general "poor readers," since 03-adhd.md's F7 and this report's F5/F8 both note the weaker/skilled-reader distinction as the likely resolution but neither has found a study that tests it in an ADHD-diagnosed sample directly.
4. **Comorbid auditory processing difficulty in *adults* with ADHD** (F7) is extrapolated from pediatric prevalence data; a dedicated search for adult APD+ADHD comorbidity and its interaction with digital dual-channel reading tools would directly de-risk any audio-first feature Riemann builds.
5. **The ethics-of-medication-aware-software question (F17) deserves a proper treatment** beyond the general cognitive-enhancement bioethics literature synthesized here — specifically: is there any precedent (in chronic-illness apps, diabetes/insulin-timing apps, menstrual-cycle-aware apps) for how software has handled adapting to a private, fluctuating physiological/pharmacological state without being paternalistic or presumptive? That's a closer analogy than general "cognitive enhancement ethics" and might yield actual design patterns rather than just cautions.
6. **A direct empirical test of progress-indicator framing in an ADHD population specifically** (F11/F12) — everything here is either general-population UX research or ADHD-adjacent shame research; nobody has run "does showing % complete measurably increase avoidance in ADHD users specifically" as a study. Worth flagging to claude-fluctuation-researcher (08) or a future wave, since it interacts directly with state-adaptive design.
7. **claude-interruption-researcher (14) overlap**: F14's idle/timeout gap and this report's RTV-to-threshold translation problem sits right next to interruption-timing/resumption research — worth a direct cross-check between that report and this one on whether resumption-by-modality findings (audio vs. text) intersect with the F5/F8 audio tension here.
