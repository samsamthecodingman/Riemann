# 14 — Interruption timing, resumption, calm/ambient tech, proactive voice

## TL;DR
- Interruption cost is not uniform: it is driven by *where in the task structure* the interruption lands. Coarse breakpoints (between chunks/subtasks) cost far less than fine breakpoints (mid-subtask) — resumption lag and mental workload both spike inside a chunk and drop at its boundary. This is a 20-year-old, well-replicated line (Adamczyk & Bailey 2004; Iqbal & Bailey 2007/2008) and a 2024/2025 replication adds a wrinkle: cost also depends on *where in a chunk sequence* the boundary falls, not just chunk-vs-non-chunk.
- Interruptions aren't just costly at the moment of the alert — Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008) found interrupted knowledge workers actually finish ~as fast or slightly faster (people compensate by working faster) but at measurably higher stress, frustration, and effort; "no cost" is a myth, the cost just moves from time to affect.
- Attention residue (Leroy 2009) means the danger isn't only the interruption itself — it's resuming Task B while Task A is still unfinished/open-ended, which degrades B's performance independent of the interrupting event. This argues for closing loops (marking things "parked" not just "interrupted") more than for perfect interruption timing.
- Notification *batching* (not elimination) is the sweet spot: Fitz et al. (2019) found ~3x/day batches reduced stress and raised wellbeing; hourly batching barely mattered vs. as-they-come; disabling notifications entirely raised anxiety/FOMO. Zero interruption is not the ADHD-safe default — it removes a wanted external cue.
- Resumption after interruption is a memory-retrieval problem (Altmann & Trafton's memory-for-goals model): the longer/more-demanding the interruption, the more the suspended goal's activation decays, and cues that were encoded at suspension-time (not generic "last item") are what speed re-retrieval. A cue pointing at the *last action taken* is about as effective as one pointing at the *next action to take* — both beat no cue.
- Calm technology (Weiser & Brown 1995; Amber Case's 8 principles) gives a concrete non-modal design vocabulary: information should live in the periphery by default, shift to center only when it matters, degrade gracefully, and use minimal/non-verbal signaling. This maps directly onto a "log silently" tier that's genuinely silent-but-visible, not a hidden inbox.
- Proactive assistants fail (Clippy) when they misjudge user goals, interrupt for low-value reasons, and can't be quickly dismissed or taught. They succeed (Copilot-style inline suggestion, 2025 CHI proactive-agent studies) when the proactive act is low-cost to ignore, contextually well-grounded, and confidence-gated. Trust research shows a robust asymmetry: people trust AI more as a *supportive* tool than as an *autonomous* actor — Riemann's "never takes real-world actions" principle is itself a trust lever, not just a safety one.
- Voice-specific: earcons/spearcons research favors short speech-based cues (spearcons) over abstract tones for indicating what kind of thing arrived, without users having to learn a private sound vocabulary. Conversational-repair research shows explicit acknowledgment + brief clarification beats silent failure or over-apologizing.
- ADHD angle is genuinely bidirectional: external cues are *load-bearing* for time-blind users (removing all interruption is not benign), but the same population is also the one most damaged by badly-timed interruption of hyperfocus. The resolution in the literature is control, not avoidance: user-set focus modes/quiet hours plus system-side breakpoint detection, not a single global policy.
- Text-vs-audio resumption specifically is thin in the literature (flagged as a gap by 04-modalities and confirmed here) — I could not find a study that directly compares "resume a task after an audio interruption" vs "after a text interruption." The design implication below is inferred from adjacent literature (audio is transient/serial, text is re-scannable) and should be treated as WEAK until Riemann can A/B it.

## Findings

### 1. Breakpoint location, not just presence of an interruption, drives cost
**Claim:** Interruptions delivered at coarse task boundaries (between subtask chunks) produce resumption costs close to zero/uninterrupted baseline; interruptions delivered at fine boundaries (mid-subtask) produce significantly longer resumption times and higher self-reported workload.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG (original finding replicated across two decades, multiple paradigms, including physiological measures).
**Sources:**
- Adamczyk & Bailey, "If not now, when? The effects of interruption at different moments within task execution" (CHI 2004) — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221518227_If_not_now_when_The_effects_of_interruption_at_different_moments_within_task_execution
- Iqbal & Bailey, "Understanding changes in mental workload during execution of goal-directed tasks and its application for interruption management," ACM TOCHI — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1314683.1314689
- Bailey & Konstan, "On the need for attention-aware systems," Computers in Human Behavior 2006 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S074756320500107X
- Powers & Scerbo, "Opportune moments for task interruptions: examining the cognitive mechanisms underlying interruption-timing effects," Frontiers in Psychology 2024/2025 (fetched in full) — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1465323/full and PMC copy https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11775001/

  Quantitative detail from the fetched Frontiers article: resumption cost varied significantly by *which* boundary was interrupted, not just chunk-boundary-vs-not — interruption before the 3rd of 6 subtasks cost 639ms, before the 2nd cost 485ms, before the 4th cost 515ms (p = .002–.011), and baseline reaction-time data (1378ms at subtask 4 vs 1199ms at subtask 3, p=.001, d=0.543) suggested participants had internally chunked the 6 subtasks into two triplets — i.e., the *task's own internal grouping*, not just objective task boundaries, determines where "coarse" really is. This matters for Riemann: what counts as a safe breakpoint is user/task-model-specific, not purely structural.

### 2. Interruption cost shows up as stress/effort even when time-on-task doesn't
**Claim:** Interrupted workers often complete tasks in comparable or even less time than uninterrupted ones (Mark et al. found ~7% faster in their field study), because they compensate by working faster and under higher subjective workload, stress, frustration, effort — a hidden cost invisible to a naive "did it take longer" metric.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE (one highly-cited field study, N modest, correlational in a real workplace, but frequently replicated in spirit).
**Sources:** Mark, Gudith & Klocke, "The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress," CHI 2008 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1357054.1357072 (abstract/summary confirmed via ACM DL and secondary sources; full PDF at https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf did not parse as text but is the same paper, cited via ACM DL record and corroborating summaries at https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-cost-of-interrupted-work:-more-speed-and-stress-Mark-Gudith/b8da65570a3955db52b117b9bedd8f131316501d).
**Design implication:** don't measure "did the interruption cost time" — measure or ask about stress/frustration too; a silent-log item that's technically free of measured delay can still be experienced as costly if it steals attention at the wrong moment.

### 3. Attention residue: the danger of the *unfinished* thing, not just the interrupting thing
**Claim:** People perform worse on a subsequent task when the prior task was left incomplete, especially if it was time-pressured or emotionally engaging; the effect is about unresolved cognitive activity persisting, independent of how the switch itself was triggered.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE (well-cited original organizational-behavior study, multiple internal experiments, less HCI-specific replication).
**Source:** Leroy, "Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks," Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 2009 — https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v109y2009i2p168-181.html (abstract) and secondary summary at https://magazine.washington.edu/theres-no-such-thing-as-multitasking-according-to-business-expert-sophie-leroy/
**Design implication:** Riemann should distinguish "interrupted mid-thing" from "paused at a clean stop." A resume-card for the former needs more scaffolding (recap + explicit "where you left off") than the latter.

### 4. Notification batching beats both always-on and always-off
**Claim:** Batching phone notifications several times a day (their tested design: ~3x/day) reduced stress and increased wellbeing relative to as-they-arrive delivery; hourly batching showed little benefit over the status quo; turning notifications off entirely *increased* anxiety and FOMO.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE-STRONG (preregistered-style randomized field experiment, N=237, 3-week design with baseline).
**Source:** Fitz, Kushlev et al., "Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being," Computers in Human Behavior 2019 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563219302596 ; full text PDF https://static1.squarespace.com/static/57a40c19414fb54f51f8095f/t/614a55faa7b89e25f4e48ad1/1632261627146/2019+Fitz+Batching.pdf ; secondary summary https://www.prosocialdesign.org/library/batching-notifications
**Design implication:** Riemann's "worth interrupting" tier should still exist as occasional real-time breaks-through; the "mention at next check-in" tier is functionally a batch, and should be scheduled (a check-in cadence) rather than purely triggered by opening the app, echoing the finding that *predictable* batches, not just infrequent ones, drove the wellbeing gain.

### 5. Resumption is goal-memory retrieval; cues encoded at suspension time work best
**Claim:** The memory-for-goals model treats a suspended task as a goal whose activation decays over time and during the interrupting task; resumption lag is a function of that decay and of priming from retrieval cues. Cues that were present/encoded at the moment of suspension (not generic markers) speed re-retrieval; a "last action taken" cue and a "next action to do" cue are roughly equally effective, and both beat no cue.
**Evidence strength:** STRONG for the theoretical model and decay finding (multiple converging experiments over 20 years); MODERATE for the specific "last vs next cue" equivalence (fewer, more applied studies, smaller N).
**Sources:**
- Altmann & Trafton, "Memory for goals: an activation-based model," Cognitive Science 2002 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0364021301000581
- Trafton et al., "Preparing to resume an interrupted task: effects of prospective goal encoding and retrospective rehearsal," Int. J. Human-Computer Studies 2003 (fetched via interruptions.net PDF listing) — https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Trafton-IJHCS03.pdf
- Hodgetts & Jones, "Contextual cues aid recovery from interruption," JEP:LMC — https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Hodgetts-JEP_LMC06.pdf
- Parnin & Rugaber, "Evaluating cues for resuming interrupted programming tasks," CHI 2010 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1753326.1753342 (last-action vs next-action cue equivalence, and "less structured explicit cues showed limited perceived helpfulness")
**Design implication:** the resume-card format (below) should be generated *at the moment of interruption/pause*, not reconstructed later from logs — it needs to capture what was actually salient then (which passage was centered, what the last zoom level was, what the last spoken sentence was), because that's what will prime fastest recall.

### 6. Calm technology: periphery-by-default, center-on-demand
**Claim:** Technology that lives in the user's periphery — informative without demanding attention, shifting to center only when needed, degrading gracefully on failure — reduces the "always vigilant, never resting" cost of ambient systems. Amber Case's operationalization gives 8 concrete principles.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE (foundational HCI essay + widely adopted practitioner framework; largely conceptual/design-theoretic rather than experimentally quantified, but influential and internally consistent).
**Sources:**
- Weiser & Brown, "Designing Calm Technology," 1995 (fetched) — https://people.csail.mit.edu/rudolph/Teaching/weiser.pdf
- Amber Case, *Calm Technology: Principles and Patterns for Non-Intrusive Design* (O'Reilly, 2015); principle list fetched from the Calm Tech Institute — https://www.calmtech.institute/calm-tech-principles
  The 8 principles as stated there: (1) require the smallest possible amount of attention, (2) inform and create calm (avoid unnecessary alerts/stress), (3) make use of the periphery, (4) amplify the best of technology and humanity, (5) communicate without speaking (non-verbal cues), (6) still work when it fails (graceful degradation), (7) use the minimum amount of technology needed to solve the problem, (8) respect social norms.
**Design implication:** the "log silently" tier is a calm-technology tier almost by definition — it should be a persistent peripheral artifact (a quiet log view, a small always-present count, not a modal or push), and Riemann's L0 gist habit already gives it a natural minimal representation ("this got logged" = one gist-length line).

### 7. Proactive assistants: Clippy's failure mode vs. what works now
**Claim:** Clippy failed primarily from misjudging *when* help was wanted (interrupting for low-confidence, low-value guesses) and from being hard to dismiss/teach, not from the idea of proactivity itself. Modern successful proactive systems (inline code completion, 2025 CHI-published proactive agents) work when the proactive act is cheap to ignore, contextually well-grounded, and gated on confidence — watching for signals like hesitation, repeated errors, or divergence from an expected path rather than a fixed timer.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE (retrospective analyses of Clippy are largely qualitative/historical; modern proactive-agent findings come from a small but growing set of CHI/CSCW 2023–2026 studies, some quite recent and not yet replicated).
**Sources:**
- "Intelligent Failures: Clippy Memes and the Limits of Digital Assistants" — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343408946_INTELLIGENT_FAILURES_CLIPPY_MEMES_AND_THE_LIMITS_OF_DIGITAL_ASSISTANTS
- "5 Lessons from Microsoft's Clippy" — https://medium.com/twentybn/5-lessons-from-clippys-failure-efc69297eac1
- "Need Help? Designing Proactive AI Assistants for Programming," CHI 2025 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3714002
- "Proactive Conversational Agents with Inner Thoughts," CHI 2025 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713760
- "'Having Lunch Now': Understanding How Users Engage with a Proactive Agent for Daily Planning and Self-Reflection," CHI 2026 (14-day, N=12 longitudinal) — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.24073
**Design implication:** Riemann's interruption tiers should be *confidence-gated*, not just importance-gated — a "worth interrupting" item needs both high estimated importance AND high confidence that this is actually a good moment/topic, echoing the "only interrupt when confidence is high" pattern found in the programming-assistant study above.

### 8. Trust asymmetry: supportive tools are trusted more than autonomous actors
**Claim:** Across domains, users consistently report more comfort/trust in AI systems that behave as supportive tools (surface information, suggest) than in ones that act autonomously, even when task performance is similar.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE (recent, single cross-domain study plus converging practitioner/HCI literature; not yet a mature meta-analytic base).
**Source:** "Trust in AI: supportive vs. autonomous roles across four domains," AI & Society (Springer) — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-026-03040-7 ; also a 230-participant autonomy-levels study noted in search (ScienceDirect, "The autonomy equation") — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0306457326000725
**Design implication:** this directly validates Riemann's stated principle of never taking real-world actions — that's not just a safety constraint, it is *also* the reason a "worth interrupting" voice nudge can be tolerated at all. An assistant that only ever surfaces and never acts has a much larger interruption budget than one that also executes things, because the downside of a bad interruption is capped at "wasted a few seconds," never "did something wrong."

### 9. Voice cues: spearcons beat abstract earcons for identifying what arrived
**Claim:** In auditory-menu and alert research, spearcons (time-compressed speech snippets) and plain speech produce faster, more accurate identification than abstract earcons or auditory icons, because they don't require users to learn an arbitrary sound vocabulary.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE-STRONG (systematic review + meta-analysis, plus original Walker et al. spearcon studies with consistent direction of effect).
**Sources:**
- "Auditory Icons, Earcons, Spearcons, and Speech: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Brief Audio Alerts in Human-Machine Interfaces," 2023 — https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/25742442.2023.2219201
- Walker et al., "Spearcons (Speech-Based Earcons) Improve Navigation Performance in Advanced Auditory Menus," Human Factors 2013 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720812450587
- Google's own conversation-design guidance on earcons — https://developers.google.com/assistant/conversation-design/earcons
**Design implication:** for the "worth interrupting" voice nudge, prefer a very short spoken word/phrase cue ("Riemann: —") over a synthetic chime the user has to learn, especially since the target user already carries high learning/memory load.

### 10. Conversational repair: acknowledge, clarify briefly, don't over-apologize
**Claim:** When a voice system misunderstands, explicit but brief acknowledgment + a targeted clarifying question outperforms silent failure and outperforms excessive apology/hedging, across both general voice-assistant studies and multicultural/accessibility-focused ones.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE (multiple converging qualitative/mixed-methods studies, less large-N quantitative comparison).
**Sources:**
- "My Bad! Repairing Intelligent Voice Assistant Errors Improves Interaction," CSCW/PACMHCI 2021 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3449101
- "System and User Strategies to Repair Conversational Breakdowns of Spoken Dialogue Systems: A Scoping Review," CUI 2024 — https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3640794.3665558
**Design implication:** if Riemann's voice channel misjudges a tier (interrupts when it shouldn't have, or the user says "not now"), it should say so plainly and adjust ("got it, I'll hold these") rather than defend the decision or stay silent about the miss — this is also a trust/adaptive-signal opportunity (see 07-adaptive-signals.md for the general signal-learning framing; this file adds the specific repair-utterance content).

### 11. ADHD: external cues are load-bearing, not optional — but hyperfocus interruption is a real risk
**Claim:** People with ADHD-linked time blindness rely on external cues to substitute for a weak internal sense of time; removing interruption/reminder mechanisms is not a safe "less annoying" default. At the same time, badly timed interruptions of hyperfocus or engaged states are experienced as distressing, and the ADHD/practitioner literature converges on "user control over interruptibility" (explicit focus modes, quiet hours) rather than a single population-wide policy.
**Evidence strength:** WEAK/MODERATE — mostly practitioner/clinical-adjacent sources (Stanford CTL, ADHD-coaching blogs), not RCT-grade HCI research; directionally consistent but not rigorously quantified.
**Sources:**
- Stanford CTL, "Managing Time Blindness" — https://ctl.stanford.edu/managing-time-blindness
- brain.fm, "ADHD Time Blindness: What It Is and How to Fight It" — https://www.brain.fm/blog/adhd-time-blindness-planning-tools-time-management
- Tether: A Personalized Support Assistant for Software Engineers with ADHD (arXiv, HCI-adjacent, more rigorous than the above) — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.01946
**Design implication:** this directly supports building an explicit, user-visible interruptibility control (see policy below) rather than relying purely on inferred breakpoint detection — inferred signals should set *defaults*, not override an explicit "not now" or "focus mode" the user has set.

### 12. Breakpoint detection is a solved *sensing* problem, not a solved *judgment* problem
**Claim:** Idle/activity detection (keyboard, mouse, screen-lock, dwell time) is a mature, standardized capability (e.g., the W3C/WICG Idle Detection API), so *detecting* candidate pause moments is cheap. The hard part remains distinguishing a genuine task/subtask boundary from a mere idle moment (bathroom break mid-thought vs. finished-a-section).
**Evidence strength:** STRONG for the sensing capability being mature and standardized; the judgment gap is inferred from findings #1 and #7 above (task-structure-aware breakpoints, not raw idleness, are what actually reduces resumption cost).
**Sources:**
- WICG Idle Detection API spec/README — https://github.com/wicg/idle-detection/blob/main/README.md
- Chrome for Developers, "Detect inactive users with the Idle Detection API" — https://developer.chrome.com/docs/capabilities/web-apis/idle-detection
**Design implication:** Riemann should combine cheap idle/dwell signals (available now, e.g. "scroll stopped," "dial hasn't moved in N seconds," "reading pace dropped to zero") with the abstraction tree's own structure (finished a section = L2 boundary crossed) as a proxy for task-structure breakpoints, since the tree already encodes the coarse/fine distinction the interruption literature cares about.

## Design implications for Riemann

1. **Use the abstraction tree itself as the breakpoint model.** Crossing an L2 section boundary, reaching L1, or completing a zoom-dial "settle" (dial stops moving for N seconds at a stable level) are natural coarse breakpoints — this is Riemann-specific because most interruption research had to *infer* task structure from external behavior; Riemann's structure is already explicit in the abstraction tree.
2. **Three tiers map cleanly onto three delivery channels, not three annoyance levels:**
   - *Worth interrupting* → real-time voice/visual nudge, but only fired when (a) importance is high AND (b) a coarse breakpoint has just been crossed (per finding #1) AND (c) no user-set focus mode is active (per finding #11). Use a short spearcon-style spoken cue, not an abstract chime (finding #9).
   - *Mention at next check-in* → a scheduled, predictable batch (finding #4) — not "whenever the app is reopened next," but a cadence the user sets (e.g., every 2 hours, or "next natural pause"), because *predictability* of the batch, not just infrequency, is what drove the wellbeing effect in Fitz et al.
   - *Log silently* → a calm-technology peripheral artifact (finding #6): always-visible, never modal, minimum representation is a single L0-length gist line in a persistent, low-salience log view.
3. **Resume-cards should be captured at pause-time, not reconstructed later.** When Riemann detects a pause/interruption (idle timeout, app backgrounded, explicit "pause"), snapshot: the centered passage, current zoom level, and a one-line auto-recap ("you were at L2, section 3, reading about X"). This directly operationalizes finding #5 (cues encoded at suspension beat generic markers) and gives both a "last-viewed highlight" and a "one-line recap" — the two resumption cue types the literature found roughly equally effective, so shipping both costs little and covers users who prefer either.
4. **Voice resumption needs an even shorter recap than text.** Since I could not find direct evidence comparing modality-specific resumption cost (flagged as a gap), the safe design choice given adjacent literature (audio is serial/transient, text is instantly re-scannable) is: text resume-cards can show the full snapshot at once; voice resume-cards should default to the single-sentence recap only, with an explicit "want more detail?" follow-up rather than a longer spoken dump — treat this as a hypothesis to A/B, not settled science.
5. **Give the user an explicit, first-class interruptibility control — a focus mode — that overrides inferred breakpoints.** This isn't just a settings nicety; findings #7, #8, and #11 together say that (a) confidence-gating alone isn't enough because confidence is estimated, not certain, (b) users trust non-autonomous, overridable systems more, and (c) ADHD users specifically need controllable, not just adaptive, interruption. A one-tap/one-utterance "not now" should suppress the *worth interrupting* tier for a set duration and downgrade what would've been real-time nudges into the next check-in batch — never into silent-log, since silent-log items are calibrated for low importance, not for "deferred high importance."
6. **Attention-residue-aware pausing.** When the user explicitly ends a session mid-L3-read (not just idles), Riemann should treat that differently from a clean "reached the end of a section" stop — mark it as an *open loop* and let the next check-in's recap explicitly say "you left this mid-way," rather than presenting it identically to a finished item (finding #3).
7. **Never let "log silently" become a hidden backlog.** Per calm-technology principle 7 (minimum tech to solve the problem) and principle 2 (create calm, not new stress), a silent log that quietly grows into an anxiety-inducing pile defeats its purpose — cap what's visible, or explicitly compress old silent-log items back down to nothing (they were logged for provenance, not for an eventual guilt-inducing backlog the user has to clear).

## Signals Riemann could measure
- **Dwell/settle time** at a given zoom level or passage (proxy for "engaged, mid-thought" vs. "paused/idle") — cheap, already implied by zoom-dial interaction.
- **Scroll/dial velocity dropping to zero** for N seconds as a candidate fine-vs-coarse breakpoint signal, combined with tree position (did it stop at a section boundary or mid-paragraph).
- **Explicit dismiss/defer action on a real-time nudge** ("not now") — direct negative-feedback signal to recalibrate the confidence gate in finding #7's design implication.
- **Time-to-resume after a check-in batch is shown** — a direct behavioral measure of whether the resume-card format (finding #5) is working; long time-to-resume suggests the recap isn't giving enough of a cue.
- **Rate of "log silently" items later manually promoted/opened by the user** — signals the importance model is mis-tiering things downward; feed back into thresholds.
- **Session-end type** (explicit close vs. timeout vs. backgrounded) as a proxy for open-loop vs. closed-loop state (finding #3), used to flavor the next resume-card.
- **Focus-mode usage patterns** (when/how often engaged, duration) — both a control signal and, longitudinally, a way to learn the user's own rhythms without asking them to self-report (echoes 07-adaptive-signals.md's low-friction-signal framing, applied specifically to interruptibility).

## Contradictions and caveats
- The interruption-timing literature (findings #1, #5) is almost entirely built on short, artificial lab tasks (tens of seconds to a few minutes) with clearly-defined subtask boundaries designed by the experimenter. Riemann's real use case — resuming a half-read document hours or days later — is a much longer horizon than most of this literature covers; the decay/priming mechanics of the memory-for-goals model are well-validated at the seconds-to-minutes scale but are an extrapolation, not a direct finding, at the hours-to-days scale Riemann actually needs.
- Mark et al.'s "interrupted workers go faster" finding (#2) is from 2008 knowledge-work observation, pre-dates smartphone-saturation notification culture, and is a single field study — treat the direction (hidden stress cost) as more reliable than the magnitude (~7%).
- The "text vs. audio resumption" question the brief specifically asked about is, as flagged, not directly answered by any source I found — the closest adjacent evidence (perception-of-interrupted-speech-vs-text, modality-and-comprehension studies) is about processing *degraded* signal, not about *resuming after a gap*, so treat design implication #4 as an inference requiring Riemann-specific testing, not an established result.
- The proactivity/Clippy literature (finding #7) is partly historical-qualitative reconstruction of a 1990s product, not controlled research — useful as a cautionary pattern, weak as quantified evidence.
- Calm technology (finding #6) is a design philosophy validated by decades of designer adoption and internal consistency, not by controlled experiments measuring outcomes against alternatives — treat it as MODERATE strategic guidance, not as an evidence-backed effect size.
- ADHD-specific sources here skew toward practitioner/clinical-coaching content rather than peer-reviewed HCI studies (finding #11); 03-adhd.md and 13-adhd-deep.md (if produced) are likely to have stronger primary evidence and should take precedence where they conflict with anything here.
- Single-user caveat: nearly all of the interruption-cost literature is about interrupting people *by other people or systems mid-workplace-task*; Riemann interrupting its own single user about their own content is a different social/cognitive situation (no social-obligation cost, no "someone is waiting on me" dynamic) — the raw magnitudes here likely overstate the cost for Riemann's context, even if the *direction* (breakpoints matter, batching beats always-on/always-off) should still hold.

## Gaps (for a possible wave 3)
- **Direct text-vs-audio-vs-diagram resumption study.** This was explicitly asked for and I could not find primary research on it. A targeted search on "modality of resumption cue" combined with dual-coding theory (Paivio) and multimedia learning (Mayer, already partially covered in 02-cogload-design.md) might get closer, or this may need to become a genuine Riemann experiment rather than a literature question.
- **Longitudinal habituation to a single personal assistant's interruption patterns.** Nearly everything here is first-encounter or short-longitudinal (14 days at most, in "Having Lunch Now"). How does a *single* user's tolerance for a *specific* assistant's proactivity change over months (do the tier thresholds need to loosen or tighten as trust builds)? This connects to 07-adaptive-signals.md and 08-fluctuation.md.
- **Circadian/energy-state interaction with breakpoint tolerance.** 08-fluctuation.md (not yet read at time of writing) likely covers energy/attention state directly — worth cross-checking whether low-energy states change *which* breakpoints feel acceptable, not just *how much content* to show.
- **Group/shared-resumption cues** — out of scope for Riemann v1 (single user) but worth flagging since the brief says Riemann should "work for other people later"; multi-user resumption cueing (e.g., shared documents) is a different literature (CSCW awareness/coordination) not touched here.
