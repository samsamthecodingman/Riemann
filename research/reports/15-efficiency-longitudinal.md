# 15 — Time-efficiency of learning techniques, RSVP/Spritz, concept-map construction, open learner models, and long-horizon personal modelling

## TL;DR
- Time-matched comparisons of retrieval practice vs. rereading (Roediger & Karpicke 2006) show retrieval wins decisively at equal *study time*: after equated exposure, retrieval-practice groups hit 61% recall a week later vs. 40% for rereading — this is one of the few learning-technique comparisons that actually controls for time, not just "technique used," which is rare in this literature and worth flagging as the exception rather than the norm.
- Constructing a concept map beats merely studying one, and the size of that gap is now precisely quantified: Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope's 2018 meta-analysis (142 effect sizes, n=11,814) found g=0.72 for construction vs. g=0.43 for studying, against an overall concept-map effect of g=0.58 — construction is roughly 65% larger than studying, both against non-map comparison conditions like text/outlines.
- RSVP/Spritz-style flashing-word reading reliably degrades *literal* comprehension once regressions are removed, with effects appearing above ~250–350 wpm; the inferential-comprehension picture is genuinely mixed across studies (some find inferential comprehension unaffected, others find it degrades with speed too) — this is a live contradiction in the literature, not settled.
- A qualitative BCU/Coventry study of Spritz with real undergraduates (Adefila, Graham & Patel 2020, n=9) found it "acceptable for certain skim reading and scanning" but explicitly **not suitable** for most students' actual academic study needs — practitioner-level evidence that RSVP is a plausible L0-gist-only tool, never an L2/L3 substitute.
- Almost none of the classic technique literature (self-explanation, elaborative interrogation, summarization, concept mapping) reports learning gain *per minute of study time* — Dunlosky et al.'s utility ratings are gain-per-technique, not gain-per-minute, and the one study that explicitly measured elaborative-learning time-on-task (32 min elaborative vs. 28 min plain reading) found only a small time premium, but this is a single, thin data point, not a literature.
- Duolingo's Birdbrain (IRT-based proficiency estimation on every exercise, ~1B exercises/day) and Khan Academy's five-tier mastery ladder (Not started → Attempted → Familiar → Proficient → Mastered) are the two most mature consumer open-learner-models; both surface a single scalar/ordinal "how much do you know" rather than a rich knowledge graph, and Khan Academy actually *killed* its one attempt at a graph-like knowledge map (the 2012 "starry night" map) in 2013 in favor of the simpler dashboard — a real, documented failure case for spatial/graph learner models in a shipped consumer product.
- Anki's FSRS surfaces per-item memory state (difficulty, stability, retrievability) and a "true retention vs. target retention" dashboard number; this is the most granular, quantitatively-grounded open learner model in mainstream use, but it requires an explicit review-queue interaction model (flashcards) that doesn't map cleanly onto free-form reading.
- Li et al.'s (2010) five-stage personal-informatics model (preparation → collection → integration → reflection → action) is the standard HCI framework for any system asking a user to track something about themselves over time, and its core finding — barriers *cascade*: a failure at collection guarantees failure at reflection and action — is a direct warning for Riemann's longitudinal "what Sam knows" model: if capture (which documents were actually read, to what level) isn't reliable and low-friction, no downstream personalization will work regardless of how good the modelling is.
- Kalyuga's rapid verification method (present partial/plausible solution steps, ask the learner to rapidly judge correct/incorrect) diagnoses prior knowledge in a fraction of the time of full problem-solving (times reduced ~3.8x) while correlating r=.66 with full test scores — a genuinely fast, cheap prior-knowledge probe Riemann could borrow, though it was validated on well-defined procedural/STEM domains, not open-ended reading material.
- Knowledge-component inference from free text (rather than from structured quiz responses) is an active but immature research area — 2025 work explores LLMs extracting knowledge components from multimedia questions and dialogue, but this is not a solved, off-the-shelf capability; expect to need a custom, conservative first pass if Riemann tries to infer "what Sam knows" from the content of his own documents/notes rather than from explicit quiz-like interactions.

## Findings

1. **Claim:** Time-matched retrieval practice beats time-matched rereading on delayed recall — a rare case where "gain per minute" is directly measurable because the experiment equalized study time between conditions.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG (foundational, widely replicated; this specific paper is one of the most-cited in the testing-effect literature).
   **Sources:** Roediger & Karpicke (2006), "The Power of Testing Memory," *Perspectives on Psychological Science* — https://psychnet.wustl.edu/memory/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Roediger-Karpicke-2006_PPS.pdf (opened, confirmed 61% vs 40% one-week recall figures); Karpicke & Roediger (2008) follow-up on repeated retrieval vs. repeated restudy at equal total time — https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2012/2012_Karpicke_CDPS.pdf (opened).

2. **Claim:** Constructing a concept map produces a substantially larger learning benefit than studying a pre-made one; both beat non-map comparison conditions (text, outlines, or no intervention), and the overall concept-map effect itself is moderate-to-large.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG (large-scale meta-analysis, 142 independent effect sizes, n=11,814, published in *Educational Psychology Review* 2018).
   **Exact numbers:** overall g=0.58 (p<.001); construction g=0.72 (p<.001); studying g=0.43 (p<.001) — construction's advantage over studying is roughly +0.29 g, i.e., about two-thirds larger as a standardized effect.
   **Sources:** Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope (2018), "Studying and Constructing Concept Maps: A Meta-Analysis," *Educational Psychology Review* — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9 (landing page confirmed via search; full text sits behind Springer's IDP login wall, so I read the abstract/summary via search-result aggregation, not the full PDF — treat the moderator-level detail, e.g. map complexity or domain breakdowns, as unverified and the top-line g-values as MODERATE-confidence pending a full-text read); corroborating record: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1179084. I could not obtain the full PDF text directly (Springer login-walled it, ERIC's own PDF link 404'd) — flagging this explicitly rather than inventing moderator detail I didn't verify.

3. **Claim:** RSVP reading (Spritz-style single-word flashing at a fixed point, no saccades/regressions) impairs literal comprehension at speeds above roughly 250–350 wpm; findings on whether inferential comprehension is spared or also degraded are inconsistent across studies.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (multiple peer-reviewed studies, but directly conflicting results on the inferential-comprehension question — this is a genuine unresolved split, not a synthesis error on my part).
   **Sources:** Benedetto et al., "Rapid serial visual presentation in reading: The case of Spritz," *Computers in Human Behavior* 2015 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563214007663 (abstract read; full PDF at https://www.tsw.it/wp-content/uploads/Rapid-serial-visual-presentation-in-reading-The-case-of-Spritz-1.pdf fetched but returned as unparsed binary — could not verify exact numbers from the raw PDF, treat literal/inferential split as reported in secondary summaries, MODERATE confidence) — this study reportedly found literal comprehension impaired but inferential comprehension unaffected by RSVP. Contrast: a separate paper titled "Rapid Serial Visual Presentation: degradation of inferential reading comprehension as a function of speed" (ResearchGate, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327269635) reports the opposite — inferential comprehension *does* degrade with RSVP speed. I read only abstracts/search snippets for both, not full texts; the disagreement between these two titles is itself the finding — don't treat RSVP's effect on inferential comprehension as settled.

4. **Claim:** Loss of regressions (re-fixations/re-reads, which normal silent reading uses roughly 10-15% of fixations for) is the mechanistic reason RSVP/Spritz formats hurt comprehension, more than raw presentation speed per se; when regressions were experimentally permitted within an RSVP-like design, comprehension repair occurred but reading slowed, and readers regressed to the start of the sentence more often than short two-word jumps.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (converges with the well-established eye-movement literature that regressions serve comprehension-repair functions, cited generally in Rayner et al. 2016 covered by the 05-speed-to-understanding report; the RSVP-specific regression-permitting study is a narrower, less-replicated design).
   **Sources:** search-aggregated from multiple RSVP papers, notably a mental-workload study finding that RSVP's comprehension cost traces to "difficulties in encoding" rather than attentional demand — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225191569_Assessing_the_Impact_of_Rapid_Serial_Visual_Presentation_RSVP_A_Reading_Technique (abstract-level read, not full text).

5. **Claim:** In a real (if small) study of undergraduates using a Spritz-style app for their own academic reading, users found RSVP acceptable for skimming/scanning but explicitly not suitable for their actual study needs.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (single small qualitative study, n=9 participants who opted into trials out of 55 enrolled — not generalizable on its own, but valuable as real-user, real-task evidence rather than a lab comprehension test).
   **Source:** Adefila, Graham & Patel (2020), "Fast and Slow: Using Spritz for Academic Study?," *Technology, Knowledge and Learning* — https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1272487 (abstract read via search); publisher record https://pureportal.bcu.ac.uk/en/publications/fast-and-slow-using-spritz-for-academic-study/.

6. **Claim:** Time-on-task data for elaborative techniques (self-explanation, elaborative interrogation) versus plain rereading is very thin in the literature — most self-explanation studies don't report time-on-task at all, and the one figure found (elaborative learning: 32 min vs. plain reading: 28 min) is a single small data point, not a systematic "cost" literature.
   **Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY — this is a genuine gap in the field, not something I failed to find; Dunlosky et al. and the 2021 Frontiers meta-analysis (both already covered in 05-speed-to-understanding) rate *utility*, not *efficiency-per-minute*.
   **Sources:** Bisra et al. (2018), "Inducing Self-Explanation: A Meta-Analysis" (PDF fetched but returned unparsed binary content — could not extract exact g-values or time data directly; treat citation as located-but-unverified) — https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf; secondary summary noting the "32 vs 28 minutes" figure and the general absence of time-on-task reporting, surfaced via aggregated search of Dunlosky-adjacent commentary — this specific number should be treated as WEAK, single-study level, not independently verified from a primary source in this pass.

7. **Claim:** Duolingo's Birdbrain model is a deep-learning item-response system that estimates learner proficiency and exercise difficulty jointly and updates after every interaction, at massive scale (~1B exercises/day), selecting a "Goldilocks difficulty" zone; the visible skill tree gives users a coarse map of what topics exist and roughly where they stand, but the fine-grained proficiency estimate itself is not directly exposed to the user as a number — it's used internally to pick exercises.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (Duolingo's own blog and IEEE Spectrum coverage are credible but are vendor/press accounts, not independently audited; no public accuracy/validation numbers found).
   **Sources:** Duolingo engineering blog, "Learning How to Help You Learn: Introducing Birdbrain" — https://blog.duolingo.com/learning-how-to-help-you-learn-introducing-birdbrain (read via search aggregation); IEEE Spectrum, "How Duolingo's AI Learns What You Need to Learn" — https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo.

8. **Claim:** Khan Academy's current open learner model is a simple five-level ordinal mastery ladder per skill (Not started / Attempted / Familiar / Proficient / Mastered) plus an aggregate course-mastery percentage; Khan Academy previously shipped a genuinely spatial/graph-like "Knowledge Map" (a starry-night visualization with color-coded stars for proficiency/recommended/review) but discontinued it in August 2013 in favor of the simpler linear dashboard.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (Khan Academy's own support documentation confirms the current system directly; the discontinued Knowledge Map's existence and 2013 retirement date come from a fan wiki, which is a weaker source for the *reason* it was retired, though the fact of retirement is corroborated by the current absence of the feature).
   **Sources:** Khan Academy Help Center, "How do Khan Academy's Mastery levels work?" — https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/5548760867853--How-do-Khan-Academy-s-Mastery-levels-work; "What are Course and Unit Mastery?" — https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/115002552631-What-are-Course-and-Unit-Mastery; Khan Academy Wiki (Fandom), "Knowledge Map" — https://khanacademy.fandom.com/wiki/Knowledge_Map (fan wiki, treat retirement-reason claims as unverified, retirement-date claim as MODERATE).

9. **Claim:** Anki's FSRS (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler) exposes a per-card memory model (Difficulty, Stability, Retrievability) and an aggregate "true retention" statistic the user can compare against a chosen target retention; this is a more quantitatively transparent open learner model than Duolingo's or Khan Academy's, because it's built entirely around explicit recall events (flashcard answers) rather than inferred engagement.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (well-documented by the FSRS open-source project itself and community blogs, but this is a self-reported, self-improving algorithm without independent third-party validation of its accuracy beyond the project's own published fit-quality claims).
   **Sources:** Expertium's Blog, "Understanding retention in FSRS" — https://expertium.github.io/Retention.html; "A technical explanation of FSRS" — https://expertium.github.io/Algorithm.html; open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki tutorial — https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md.

10. **Claim:** Readwise and RemNote both use spaced-repetition review of highlights/notes as their primary "what you retain" surface (not what you *understand* at a conceptual level) — Readwise emphasizes user feedback per-highlight (a "Mastery" rating that reweights which highlights resurface) and reports engaged users maintaining daily-review streaks approaching 1,000 days; RemNote shows an exponentially-growing interval visualization plus a 7-day practice-history bar chart. Neither tool exposes a structured knowledge-graph "what I know" model to the user beyond the review queue itself and simple streak/interval visuals.
    **Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY as product-design evidence — these are vendor blog posts and help-center pages, not independent research or controlled studies of user outcomes; the "nearly 1,000 days straight" claim is an anecdote, not a usage-distribution statistic.
    **Sources:** Readwise blog, "Using Spaced Repetition and Active Recall with Books to Hack Your Brain" — https://blog.readwise.io/hack-your-brain-with-spaced-repetition-and-active-recall/; RemNote Help Center, "Understanding Spaced Repetition" — https://help.remnote.com/en/articles/9337171-understanding-spaced-repetition.

11. **Claim:** Li, Dey & Forlizzi's (2010) stage-based model of personal informatics systems — preparation, collection, integration, reflection, action — identifies that barriers at an earlier stage cascade forward and block later stages, that the process is iterative rather than linear, and that systems need a deliberate balance of automation vs. user control at each stage.
    **Evidence strength:** STRONG as a foundational, heavily-cited (1,132+ citations) HCI framework from CHI 2010, though it is a qualitative/interview-derived model (Quantified Self community interviews), not an experimentally validated causal theory.
    **Sources:** Li, Dey & Forlizzi (2010), "A Stage-Based Model of Personal Informatics Systems," *CHI 2010* — https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1753326.1753409 (abstract/summary read via search aggregation, not full ACM-paywalled text); open PDF listing — https://ianli.owlstown.net/publications/17-a-stage-based-model-of-personal-informatics-systems.

12. **Claim:** Personal knowledge graph tools (Obsidian, Roam, Notion-style "second brain" apps) are a mature product category built on the Zettelkasten note-linking method, but the category's own framing acknowledges it as primarily a *capture and connection* tool ("visualize personal learning as a network"), not a system that models *degree of understanding* or *confidence* per node — the graph shows what's connected, not what's known well vs. shakily.
    **Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY as research evidence — these are product/blog descriptions of a category, not empirical studies of whether PKG tools actually improve or accurately represent a user's knowledge state over time. I found no controlled longitudinal study measuring whether a personal knowledge graph correctly tracks "what a user knows" against ground truth.
    **Sources:** Dan McCreary, "Personal Knowledge Graphs" — https://medium.com/data-science/personal-knowledge-graphs-9a23a0b099af; Volodymyr Pavlyshyn, "Personal Knowledge Graphs in Obsidian" — https://volodymyrpavlyshyn.medium.com/personal-knowledge-graphs-in-obsidian-528a0f4584b9.

13. **Claim:** Knowledge-component (KC) inference — identifying the specific skill/fact units a learner does or doesn't know — is traditionally done from structured assessment performance, and inferring KCs directly from unstructured free text (a learner's own writing, or arbitrary reading material) is an active, unresolved 2025-era research direction, including LLM-based extraction from multimedia questions and from open dialogue.
    **Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY (very recent, mostly unreplicated arXiv/EDM-conference work, similar maturity level to the LLM-diagram-generation gap already flagged in 04-modalities).
    **Sources:** "Using Large Multimodal Models to Extract Knowledge Components for Knowledge Tracing from Multimedia Question Information," EDM 2025 — https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2025/proceedings/2025.EDM.long-papers.170/index.html; "A Survey of Current Machine Learning Approaches to Student Free-Text Evaluation for Intelligent Tutoring," *IJAIED* — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40593-022-00323-0 (abstracts read via search aggregation, not full text).

14. **Claim:** Kalyuga's rapid verification method — presenting partial/plausible next-steps in a procedure and asking learners to rapidly judge whether each is correct — diagnoses a learner's prior-knowledge/expertise level in a fraction of the time full problem-solving would take, and correlates well with traditional complete-solution test scores.
    **Evidence strength:** MODERATE-STRONG (multiple validation studies by the same research group over ~15 years, consistent methodology, but concentrated in well-structured procedural/STEM domains like algebra and electrical troubleshooting, not open-ended reading comprehension).
    **Exact numbers:** test time reduced by a factor of ~3.8x (some reports say "up to 5x"), correlation with traditional test scores r=.66.
    **Sources:** Kalyuga, "Rapid Assessment of Learners' Knowledge Structures" and related papers — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222424396_Rapid_assessment_of_learners'_knowledge_structures; "Rapid Dynamic Assessment of Expertise to Improve the Efficiency of Adaptive E-Learning," *ETR&D* — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225365494 (abstracts read via search aggregation).

## Design implications for Riemann

- **Don't budget "learning per minute" from the general technique literature — it barely exists.** Outside retrieval practice vs. rereading (finding 1), the field reports *effectiveness*, not *effectiveness per minute spent*. Riemann's own budget-allocation model (proposed in 05-speed-to-understanding) will have to be calibrated empirically from Riemann's own usage logs (dwell time × outcome signal), not imported wholesale from the literature — treat any literature-derived "minutes per technique" number as a rough prior to be overwritten by real data quickly.
- **If Riemann ever offers a "build your own L2 map" mode (letting the user drag/rename/connect section nodes), expect roughly a 0.3-g bump over just reading the auto-generated tree**, per the Schroeder et al. split (construction g=0.72 vs. studying g=0.43). This strengthens the 04-modalities recommendation to make tree nodes lightly editable — this is now a quantified, not just plausible, case for that feature, though it should stay opt-in (construction takes real time/effort the user may not have).
- **Do not ship an RSVP/Spritz flashing-word mode as a general reading substitute; if built at all, scope it tightly to L0 (one-line gist) and literal-fact skimming, never to comprehension-critical or inference-heavy material**, and say so explicitly in the UI. The evidence is split on whether inferential comprehension survives RSVP, but converges that literal comprehension degrades above ~250–350 wpm and that real students (finding 5) don't consider it usable for actual study — this is a stronger, more specific caution than the general "skimming trades comprehension for speed" framing already in 05-speed-to-understanding.
- **Model prior knowledge with a fast, rapid-verification-style probe rather than a full pretest**, per Kalyuga (finding 14): e.g., before expanding a document, show 2-3 plausible-sounding one-line claims from the material (some true, some subtly wrong) and ask the user to mark right/wrong. This is cheap (seconds), gives a real signal correlated with deeper knowledge tests, and — unlike the prequestion technique from 05-speed-to-understanding — is explicitly designed as a *diagnostic*, not just a priming device, so it can gate how much scaffolding Riemann shows (tying directly to the expertise-reversal implication already flagged in 05-speed-to-understanding).
- **Treat "what Sam knows" as an ordinal per-topic ladder (à la Khan Academy: not-started/familiar/proficient) rather than a rich graph, as the default surfaced UI**, even if the underlying model is richer. Khan Academy's own abandonment of its spatial knowledge map in favor of a simpler dashboard (finding 8) is a real, shipped-product data point that a graph-shaped learner model may not survive contact with actual daily use, even though the *tree-as-graph* view is separately well-supported for the abstraction tree itself (04-modalities finding 8) — the difference is structural navigation (good, evidenced) vs. a "map of my competence" overlay (attempted once at scale, dropped).
- **Follow Li et al.'s stage model explicitly when designing the longitudinal "what Sam knows across hundreds of documents" system**: build reliable, low-friction *collection* first (which documents were opened, to what level, for how long — this is nearly free, Riemann already has this data from the zoom dial) before investing in *integration* (merging signals across documents into a topic model) or *reflection* (surfacing it back to Sam) — per finding 11, a broken collection stage guarantees the reflection/action stages will be built on sand, so this is a sequencing argument, not just a nice-to-have ordering.
- **If Riemann eventually infers "topics Sam already understands" from the content of his own notes/documents (not from quiz answers), treat this as a genuinely unsolved capability (finding 13), not an engineering detail** — start with the cheapest possible proxy (has Sam read this topic before, at what level, how recently — a Kalyuga-style rapid check when uncertain) rather than attempting free-text knowledge-component extraction as a v1 feature.
- **A per-topic "memory state" akin to FSRS's Stability/Retrievability (finding 9) is a plausible longer-term model for Riemann's spaced-return feature** (already flagged as opt-in in 05-speed-to-understanding) — but note FSRS's model depends on explicit, frequent recall *events* (flashcard answers) to fit its parameters; Riemann's implicit signals (dwell, re-open, zoom pattern) are much noisier proxies for "did you actually recall this," so don't expect FSRS-grade precision without adding some explicit recall checkpoints.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Time-to-outcome per technique, logged per user**, to build Riemann's own empirical "gain per minute" table rather than relying on the literature's thin data (finding 6) — e.g., log dwell time at L1 before a teach-back attempt, dwell time when a rapid-verification probe is shown vs. skipped, and correlate with later re-open/return behavior as an outcome proxy.
- **Rapid-verification probe responses** (right/wrong, latency) as both a diagnostic (per finding 14) and a free efficiency signal — this is a stronger, more specific version of the "prequestion response" signal already listed in 05-speed-to-understanding, because it's explicitly calibrated as a knowledge-level gate, not just an attention primer.
- **Per-topic "last seen at what level, how long ago" as the minimum viable open learner model** — mirrors Khan Academy's ordinal ladder (finding 8) and is cheap to compute directly from existing zoom-dial logs, with no NLP/knowledge-graph inference required.
- **Node-construction events if editable tree nodes ship** (rename, connect, reorder) as a proxy for the "constructing > studying" boost (finding 2) — directly testable within Riemann's own data: does editing a node correlate with better later recall/return, closing the loop on whether Schroeder et al.'s effect transfers to Riemann's specific tree structure.
- **Collection-stage completeness** (per Li et al., finding 11) as a meta-signal: what fraction of opened documents have *any* level-of-engagement data at all — if this is low, any downstream "what Sam knows" model is unreliable regardless of its sophistication, so this should be a visible internal health metric, not just a UX feature.

## Contradictions and caveats

- **RSVP/inferential-comprehension findings genuinely disagree across studies** (finding 3) — I did not resolve this, and Riemann should not either; the safe design choice is to restrict RSVP to literal-fact/gist use only, sidestepping the open question rather than betting on either side of it.
- **The Schroeder et al. construct-vs-study numbers are reported at MODERATE confidence** because the full paywalled text wasn't read — the top-line g-values (0.72 vs 0.43 vs 0.58) came through consistently across the search-aggregated abstract and the ERIC record, so they're probably right, but any moderator-level claim (e.g., "construction works better for novices than experts," which I did not find explicitly) should not be assumed without a full-text read.
- **The self-explanation time-cost data point (finding 6) is essentially anecdotal** — a single "32 vs 28 minutes" figure from secondary commentary, not a verified primary-source number (the Bisra et al. PDF I attempted to fetch returned as unparsed binary). Do not treat this as a load-bearing number; it's included only to show how thin this specific literature is.
- **Consumer open-learner-model evidence (Duolingo, Khan Academy, Anki, Readwise, RemNote) is almost entirely vendor/product documentation, not independent research** — none of it is peer-reviewed or independently audited for accuracy. Treat "what works" here as product-design precedent (what shipped, what got removed) rather than as validated efficacy evidence — this is a different, weaker evidence class than the cognitive-science findings elsewhere in this fleet's reports, and I've flagged each finding's strength accordingly rather than inflating vendor claims to STRONG.
- **Kalyuga's rapid-verification validation (finding 14) is in well-structured procedural/STEM domains** (algebra, electrical fault diagnosis) where "correct next step" has an unambiguous ground truth — Riemann's content (email, notes, general documents) is mostly not like this, so a rapid-verification probe for Riemann would need "true/false claim about the document" items rather than "correct/incorrect solution step" items, which is a different (untested) instantiation of the same idea, not a directly transferred, validated technique.
- **None of the long-horizon personal-modelling literature (Li et al., PKG tools) directly measures accuracy of a "what do I know" model against ground truth** — it's process/design guidance (how to structure the system) or product description (what exists), not evidence that any current system correctly tracks a user's actual knowledge state over months. This is a genuine open problem, not a solved-elsewhere thing Riemann can just copy.

## Gaps

- **A full-text read of Schroeder et al. 2018** (behind Springer's login wall) to extract moderator breakdowns (map complexity, novice vs. expert, domain) — worth a follow-up if the fleet has institutional access or can find an author-hosted preprint; the top-line numbers are solid but the "when does construction help most" question remains open.
- **A primary-source read of the Bisra et al. self-explanation meta-analysis** and any comparable meta-analysis for elaborative interrogation with actual time-on-task data extracted — the "gain per minute" table the brief asked for is only half-built here (retrieval practice and concept-map construction have real numbers; self-explanation, elaborative interrogation, and plain summarization do not) — a dedicated pass specifically hunting for time-cost-normalized comparisons (searching terms like "efficiency" or "cost-effectiveness" alongside technique names, rather than "effect size" alone) could close this gap.
- **Failure/retirement case studies of open learner models beyond Khan Academy's Knowledge Map** — if consumer ed-tech has tried and dropped other spatial/graph-based "what you know" visualizations, a dedicated audit of ed-tech product-history retrospectives (Hacker News threads, ed-tech blogs, teardown posts) would sharpen the "graph learner models don't survive contact with real users" hypothesis this report only found one data point for.
- **ADHD-specific interaction with rapid-verification/prior-knowledge probes** — does a fast true/false probe at the start of a document read as friction or as a helpful shortcut to someone with fluctuating attention? This sits at the intersection of this report and 03-adhd/13-adhd-deep — worth a targeted question to whichever of those workers is still active, or a follow-up search on "testing effect ADHD attention."
- **Knowledge-component inference from free text specifically for adult, informal, cross-document reading** (not classroom quiz responses) — the 2025 KC-extraction papers found here are about structured multimedia questions or tutoring dialogue, not about inferring "does this person already understand topic X" from the fact that they've read three prior documents mentioning it. This is arguably the single most novel and highest-value piece of Riemann's long-horizon modelling ambition, and it's the least-covered by existing research — likely needs original design work, informed by but not copied from, the KC-tracing literature.
