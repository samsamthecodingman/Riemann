# 16 — Generation formats: how an LLM should generate each presentation format well

## TL;DR
- NotebookLM-style two-voice conversational audio is popular and Google now ships four formats (Deep Dive, Brief, Critique, Debate) plus an "Interactive Mode" that lets the listener barge in and ask a question mid-episode — but I could not find a controlled study directly comparing two-voice audio vs monologue vs text on comprehension; the "it helps learning" claim in circulation is largely anecdotal/product-marketing, not measured.
- LLM-generated podcasts are measurably unfaithful to source material even from the best models: GPT-4o scored only 3.89/5.0 on a faithfulness scale, with academic-domain podcasts the worst (3.38/5.0). A turn-level "catch-and-repair" grounding pass recovered ~5–13% relative faithfulness — this is a solved-enough-to-use, not solved, problem.
- Discourse-aware summarization (explicit discourse-schema prompting, e.g. RST-style relations, or a "global structure awareness" pass) reliably beats plain LLM summarization on coherence/structure in the papers that measure it, but the underlying evidence I could verify was thin on hard numbers — treat as MODERATE, not proven at scale.
- LLM length control is unreliable in the literature: GPT-4-class models violate explicit word-count constraints roughly half the time in some benchmarks, and small changes to the target number (600 vs 610 words) flip pass/fail — this directly threatens Riemann's L1 "1–2 minute read" contract and needs a post-hoc check, not just a prompt instruction.
- Auto-generated concept maps are measurably worse than human/expert ones on relational accuracy: automatically created maps show 50–80% concept-relevance vs 65–90% for semi-automated extraction; in a clinical-reasoning domain, GPT-generated concept maps had precision as low as 16–50% and recall 46–74%. Crucially, one study found connection *quality* (not count) predicted diagnostic accuracy — sparse-but-correct beats dense-but-wrong.
- Chain-of-Density prompting (iteratively re-writing a summary to fold in missing salient entities without growing length) is a well-evidenced technique (Adams et al. 2023) for controlling information density independent of length, and is a good candidate for Riemann's L0/L1 generation step.
- LLM-generated analogies and "explain like I'm five" simplification measurably help engagement but carry a specific failure mode: simplification silently drops word-sense/polysemy information and can flatten multi-valid answers into a false-single-answer, and personalized explanations can hallucinate claims tailored to (but not grounded in) what the user said.
- LLM-as-judge is the standard cheap evaluator for summary quality/faithfulness but has known reliability problems (self-preference, length bias, self-inconsistency); a citation-faithfulness judge correlated only ~0.63 (Pearson) with human ratings in one podcast-faithfulness study — usable as a monitoring signal, not a ground truth.
- ISO 24495-1:2023 gives a real, citable structure for what "plain language" means (findable, understandable, usable, relevant) — useful as a checklist for Riemann's L0/L1 prompts, distinct from pure Flesch-Kincaid score targeting.

## Findings

### 1. Two-voice conversational audio (NotebookLM Audio Overviews) — popularity is real, comprehension evidence is not
**Claim:** NotebookLM's Audio Overview turns source material into a two-host conversational podcast (with filler words, interruptions, "m-hm"/"right" backchanneling) and, as of the September 2025 update, offers four formats — Deep Dive, Brief, Critique, Debate — across 80+ languages, plus a December 2024 "Interactive Mode" where the user can literally interrupt the hosts by voice and ask a question, which the hosts answer live before resuming. Early versions of Interactive Mode had hosts that acted "annoyed" at being interrupted, which Google explicitly had to fix.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE for the product description (directly observable/documented); WEAK/PRELIMINARY for any comprehension/engagement/trust benefit — I found no controlled study isolating two-voice-conversational vs monologue vs text for comprehension outcomes. What exists is qualitative/anecdotal ("makes complex material genuinely easier to absorb") from product-adjacent writeups, not measured trials.
**Sources:** [xda-developers overview](https://www.xda-developers.com/notebooklm-audio-overview/); [Jake Miller on Interactive Mode](https://jakemiller.net/notebooklms-interactive-podcast-feature-revolutionizing-learning-through-conversation/); [TechCrunch on hosts trained not to act annoyed](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/14/googles-notebooklm-had-to-teach-its-ai-podcast-hosts-not-to-act-annoyed-at-humans); [sider.ai 2025 upgrade summary](https://sider.ai/blog/ai-tools/what-is-google-notebooklm-audio) — all opened, all secondary/product-writeup sources, not peer-reviewed.

### 2. PaperWave: research-paper-to-podcast via LLM scripting (CHI 2025)
**Claim:** PaperWave (Choi et al., CHI EA 2025) scripts research papers into conversational two-host podcasts, and found that presenting a podcast **outline at the start** measurably improves engagement and comprehension, and that converting text to audio format broadens who engages with academic papers (lowers the barrier to entry) while changing what gets comprehended vs. reading.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE — this is a real HCI venue paper (CHI Extended Abstracts), but I was only able to read search-engine-summarized excerpts, not the full paper text (the PDF fetch returned binary/compressed content I couldn't parse, and the secondary summary site was unreachable from this sandbox). Treat specific numeric claims as unverified until the primary text is read directly.
**Sources:** [arXiv abstract/PDF page (2410.15023)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.15023) — attempted, PDF binary not extractable in this session; [ACM DL record](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706599.3706664) — listed, not opened.
**Implication for Riemann:** the "outline-first" finding directly supports prepending an L0 gist / table-of-contents before any generated audio or long-form output — consistent with the abstraction-tree architecture already planned (L0 before L1).

### 3. LLM-generated podcasts are measurably unfaithful — even the best models
**Claim:** In a study on faithfulness of document-grounded podcast generation, the strongest model tested (GPT-4o) achieved only 3.89/5.0 on a faithfulness scale; faithfulness was worst in academic-domain source material (3.38/5.0) and best in policy domains (4.05/5.0), meaning even top models "frequently generate ungrounded content" in long-form multi-speaker conversation generation. A proposed fix, **catch-n-repair**, uses a fine-tuned turn-level classifier (LLaMA-3.1-8B, F1 79.9%/67.3% for faithful/unfaithful turns) to flag ungrounded turns and regenerate them with explicit source-grounding instructions, recovering ~5% relative faithfulness in-domain and up to 12.8% relative out-of-domain, without hurting coverage (≤0.2 point drop).
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE — single arXiv paper (2607.21961, "On Improving Faithfulness of Podcasts from Documents"), not yet peer-reviewed as far as I could confirm, but with a concrete methodology and numbers, and an LLM-judge-vs-human correlation reported (0.63 Pearson) rather than asserted.
**Source:** [arXiv 2607.21961](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21961) — opened and read.
**Implication for Riemann:** if audio/conversational generation is ever added, do **not** trust a single LLM pass to stay grounded in the source, especially for the kind of technical/academic content Sam's coursework involves (this study's *worst* faithfulness bucket). A cheap turn/sentence-level "does this claim trace to a source span?" check — mirroring Riemann's existing `source_span` field on tree nodes — is directly applicable and cheap to implement because the abstraction tree already carries span provenance.

### 4. Discourse-aware / structure-guided summarization outperforms plain prompting on coherence — but evidence is thin on hard numbers
**Claim:** Several 2025–2026 papers argue that explicitly modeling discourse structure (RST-style relations, or "global structure awareness") when generating a summary produces better coherence and logical flow than a flat LLM summarization prompt. "GloSA-sum" reportedly matches GPT-4's average quality score (4.30/5) while exceeding it specifically on coherence (4.4 vs 4.3). DiscoSum assigns RST-based discourse labels per sentence (elaboration, attribution, explanation, etc.) and uses that schema to drive which content goes where in the summary.
**Evidence strength:** WEAK-to-MODERATE — I could not retrieve genuinely detailed, numeric results for DiscoSum (the fetch returned a generic-sounding summary without hard figures, a sign the underlying claims may not be well-supported or the source I read was thin); the GloSA-sum number (4.30 vs 4.30, 4.4 vs 4.3 coherence) came from a search snippet, not a directly-read primary source. Treat the *direction* of the finding (discourse-explicit prompting > flat prompting, for coherence specifically) as plausible but not strongly verified.
**Sources:** [DiscoSum arXiv 2506.06930](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06930) — fetched, but summary was thin; [Text summarization via global structure awareness, arXiv 2602.09821](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09821) — listed, not opened; [StrucSum arXiv 2505.22950](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22950) — listed, not opened (uses "Neighbor-Aware Prompting," "Centrality-Aware Prompting," and "Centrality-Guided Masking" for long-document extractive summarization with LLMs).
**Implication for Riemann:** the plan's build strategy (leaf → L2 gist → L1 → L0) already *is* a structure-aware pipeline — it summarizes with awareness of section boundaries rather than flattening the whole document into one prompt. This finding is a mild confirmation that Riemann's architecture choice (bottom-up, structure-respecting) is directionally correct versus a naive "summarize the whole thing in one shot" approach, even though I can't cite hard numbers proving the size of the benefit.

### 5. Signposting ("first / second / therefore") as an explicit prompting target
**Claim:** None of the papers I found study signposting/advance-organiser cues in LLM output as a named, isolated variable (distinct from "discourse structure" broadly). This appears to be a **gap** — structure-aware summarization research targets RST relations and coherence scores, not the reader-facing verbal scaffolding (signal words, meta-discourse markers) studied in classic reading-comprehension literature (e.g. work on "structure strategy" instruction, which sits outside NLP/ML venues and which I did not have time to search directly in this pass).
**Evidence strength:** N/A (absence of evidence, not evidence of absence) — flagged as a gap below rather than a finding.

### 6. Readability/length control: plain-language standards exist and are usable; LLM compliance is unreliable
**Claim A (standard):** ISO 24495-1:2023 defines plain language via four pillars — the text must be **findable**, **understandable**, **usable**, and **relevant** to its target audience — and recommends common words, short sentences, and avoiding unnecessary technical terms. It's a real, citable international standard (25 countries, 19 languages, evidence-based) as opposed to a single readability formula.
**Claim B (LLM reliability):** LLMs can be prompted toward a target reading level (e.g., "reply at a Flesch-Kincaid grade 1 level") and can be taught via examples to hit a target simplification level, but **explicit length constraints are unreliable**: one study found GPT-4-class models violate simple max-word-count instructions on the order of 50% of the time, and that changing the target from "at most 600" to "at most 610" words can flip a substantial number of test cases from pass to fail — i.e., the failure isn't just "roughly close," it's brittle to the specific number requested. Root causes cited: tokenizer operates at sub-word level (models don't reliably "count" words), and training data underrepresents strict length-constrained examples. Proposed mitigations: explicit length-tracking/budget-allocation prompting ("length awareness"), iterative feedback loops, and instruction-tuning specialized for length control.
**Evidence strength:** Claim A — STRONG as a description of the standard's existence and content (official ISO summary + secondary sources agree). Claim B — MODERATE: multiple independent 2024–2026 arXiv papers converge on "length control is unreliable," which is a fairly robust pattern even though no single meta-analysis exists.
**Sources:** [ISO 24495-1:2023 official page](https://www.iso.org/standard/78907.html); [Following Length Constraints in Instructions, arXiv 2406.17744](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.17744) — opened; [Can LLMs Track Their Output Length, arXiv 2601.01768](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.01768v1) — listed; [Plan-and-Write: Structure-Guided Length Control, arXiv 2511.01807](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.01807v1) — listed, proposes staged/structured generation as a fix.
**Implication for Riemann — this is the single most actionable finding for the plan.** The plan targets L1 at "~230 words/min × 1–2 min, scaled by source length." Given the ~50% violation rate found in the literature, **do not trust the LLM's own length compliance.** Riemann's `build.py` already validates JSON structure with pydantic and retries once on failure — extend that same validator to check word count against the target band and retry (or truncate/expand programmatically) rather than assuming the prompt instruction was followed. This is a cheap, deterministic check (`len(text.split())`) that closes a known, well-documented gap.

### 7. Auto-generated concept maps are measurably worse than human ones, and the deficit isn't in node count — it's in edge quality
**Claim:** Automatically created concept maps show concept-relevance factors of 50–80%, vs 65–90% for semi-automated (human-in-the-loop) extraction. In a clinical-reasoning education context (virtual patients, medical students), GPT-generated concept maps had precision as low as 16–50% and recall 46–74% against a gold standard. Separately, in a study of 40 student-made concept maps (20 from students who diagnosed correctly, 20 who erred), there was **no significant difference in the number of concepts or connections** between groups — the diagnostic-accuracy signal came entirely from **connection quality**: maps from correct-diagnosis students scored significantly higher on a relational-validity rubric (mean 12.13 vs 9.09, p=0.03, inter-rater κ=0.85). Notably, 82% of students in that study made maps with *no* connections at all despite instructions, i.e., people default to bare node lists unless pushed.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE — the clinical concept-map precision/recall figures come from a PubMed-indexed study I could not fully open (blocked by a bot-check page) so I'm relying on the abstract-level figures surfaced in search; the connection-quality/diagnostic-accuracy study (PMC10594037) I read in full and the numbers above are directly from that source.
**Sources:** [PMC10594037, opened and read in full](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10594037/); [PubMed 40380644, GPT vs human concept maps — abstract-level only, blocked by bot-check](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40380644/); [Automated Approaches for Concept Map Assessment: A Systematic Review](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-03870-8_34) — listed, not opened.
**Implication for Riemann:** directly reinforces finding #11 already logged in `04-modalities.md` (don't trust one-shot LLM graph extraction). The added nuance here: if Riemann ever renders the abstraction tree as a node-link diagram, the **edges are the risk, not the nodes** — the tree's parent/child edges are structurally guaranteed by the chunker (safe), but any additional *cross-links* an LLM might propose between non-adjacent nodes should get the same skepticism as a from-scratch concept map, and specifically should be validated for relational correctness, not just presence/absence.

### 8. Chain-of-Density prompting is a validated technique for content-density control independent of length
**Claim:** Chain-of-Density (Adams et al., 2023) has GPT-4 generate an initial "entity-sparse" summary, then iteratively rewrite it to incorporate 1–3 additional missing salient entities per step **without increasing length** (compressing/fusing existing content to make room). A human preference study on 100 CNN/DailyMail articles found people prefer denser CoD summaries over vanilla-prompt summaries, approaching the density of human-written reference summaries. CoD summaries were also more abstractive (less copy-paste, more fusion) and had less lead bias (less "just summarize the first paragraph") than vanilla prompting.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE — single paper, but well-known, methodologically concrete (500 human-annotated + 5,000 unannotated summaries released), from a credible group (Columbia/Salesforce-adjacent authors), on arXiv since 2023 (mature enough that its claims have circulated and not been overturned in what I found).
**Source:** [arXiv 2309.04269](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.04269v1) — search-summarized in detail, not directly fetched as full text this session but corroborated across multiple independent summaries (HuggingFace paper page, GitHub notes, learnprompting.org).
**Implication for Riemann:** CoD maps almost exactly onto the plan's L0/L1 generation step. Rather than a single "summarize this in ≤25 words" prompt for L0, run a 2–3 step density pass: draft → identify 1-2 missing salient entities → rewrite at the same length. This should produce denser, more information-rich L0/L1 text without violating the length band (addresses finding #6's length-control risk *and* improves content quality in the same step) — worth prototyping against the plain single-shot prompt currently implied by `build.py`.

### 9. LLM-generated analogies and "explain like I'm X" help engagement but have a specific, checkable failure mode
**Claim:** LLM-generated analogies for educational content are cheap to produce and can genuinely help (cited use in CS1 education contexts), but weak/wrong analogies mislead and confuse. Separately, simplification (ELI5-style) has a measured failure mode: it tends to flatten polysemous/multi-sense words into a single implied meaning, silently dropping the "this word has more than one valid sense here" signal, and because users tend to assume LLM output is both correct *and* complete, this specific failure is more dangerous than an obviously-wrong answer. Personalized explanations carry a related but distinct risk: "personalization-induced hallucination," where the model aligns explanations with what it infers about the user rather than objective correctness, and can fabricate individual-level claims (e.g., inferred background/preferences) that were never actually stated.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE for the polysemy-flattening finding (single arXiv paper with a clear, testable claim); WEAK/PRELIMINARY for "analogies help learning" (asserted, not measured, in what I found) and MODERATE for the personalization-hallucination risk (multiple 2025–2026 papers converge on the pattern, though "personalization" in those papers usually means longer-term user-modeling, not a one-off "explain like I'm a beginner" request — so transfer to Riemann's use case is partial, not direct).
**Sources:** [Simplifications are Absolutists, arXiv 2507.11981](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.11981v1); [When Personalization Misleads, arXiv 2601.11000](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11000); [A Comprehensive Analysis of Student-Generated Analogies with LLMs in CS1, arXiv 2403.09409](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.09409v1) — all listed via search, read at summary level, not full-text fetched.
**Implication for Riemann:** if a future "explain like I'm a [level]" or personalized-analogy feature is added, treat any analogy or simplified explanation as a candidate for a lightweight faithfulness check against the source span (same pattern as finding #3), and be specifically wary of over-simplification silently erasing "this term means two different things depending on context" — a real risk for engineering coursework where terms are often overloaded (e.g., "state" in control theory vs. software).

### 10. LLM-as-judge is the practical evaluation tool but has known, specific reliability failure modes
**Claim:** LLM-as-judge is the dominant cheap-evaluation paradigm for summary/format quality (faithfulness, coherence, citation-support) because human evaluation doesn't scale, but has documented failure modes: self-preference (judge favors output from its own model family), sensitivity to spurious surface features like length, self-inconsistency across repeated judgments, and general difficulty evaluating attribution/citation support specifically. One concrete calibration point: in the podcast-faithfulness study (finding #3), the LLM judge correlated with human ratings at only 0.63 Pearson — usable as a directional signal, not a precise measurement.
**Evidence strength:** MODERATE — the specific failure-mode list is corroborated across several 2025–2026 survey/analysis papers (not just one), though I did not open any single paper in full; the 0.63 correlation figure is from a directly-read primary source (finding #3).
**Sources:** [Neither Valid nor Reliable? arXiv 2508.18076](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.18076); [Reliability without Validity, arXiv 2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19544v1); [From Generation to Judgment, arXiv 2411.16594](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.16594) — all listed, read at search-summary level.
**Implication for Riemann:** for the cheap per-format eval recipes below, LLM-as-judge is the right *tool*, but Riemann should never let an LLM judge silently gate what gets shown to the user (e.g., auto-hiding a summary it judges "unfaithful") without a deterministic backstop (word-count check, span-citation check) — consistent with the length-control finding above: prefer cheap deterministic checks over LLM-judged ones wherever a deterministic check exists.

## Design implications for Riemann

1. **Extend `build.py`'s existing "validate + retry once" pattern to length, not just JSON schema.** After generating L0/L1 text, check `word_count` against the target band programmatically; if it's outside the band, do one targeted retry with the delta stated explicitly ("you produced 340 words, target is 200–230, shorten by removing detail, not deleting sentences wholesale"). This directly addresses the ~50% length-violation rate found in the literature (#6) and is cheap since the validation infrastructure already exists in the plan.
2. **Use a Chain-of-Density-style 2-pass generation for L0/L1**, not a single-shot summarize prompt: pass 1 drafts, pass 2 asks "what's one important entity/fact missing, rewrite at the same length to include it." This is directly evidenced (#8) and composes naturally with the retry-on-length-violation loop in (1) — both are "generate, check, one bounded repair pass" patterns, which matches the plan's existing philosophy.
3. **Prepend an outline/gist before any future audio or long-form generation** (PaperWave's outline-first finding, #2) — Riemann already does this by construction (L0 is the gist, and the plan's anchoring logic keeps L0 visible as you zoom), so no architecture change needed, just a note that this ordering choice is evidence-aligned if audio generation is added later.
4. **If/when audio or a "podcast mode" for L1 is built, do not trust one LLM pass for faithfulness.** Add a lightweight grounding check: for each generated claim/turn, verify it traces to a `source_span` already present in the tree (Riemann's Node model already carries this field) — this is essentially a free version of the "catch" half of catch-n-repair (#3), because the span-tracking infrastructure already exists for a different reason (anchoring).
5. **Treat any cross-links added on top of the tree (as opposed to the tree's own parent/child edges) as unverified concept-map edges**, per finding #7 — validate them the same way MYTH-check finding #11 in `04-modalities.md` already recommends (schema check + a repair pass), and specifically weight edge/relation correctness over node count in any future auto-grading of diagram quality.
6. **Adopt ISO 24495-1's four pillars (findable, understandable, usable, relevant) as an explicit prompt checklist for L0/L1 generation**, not just a target Flesch-Kincaid number — "findable" maps to good headings/signposting in L2, "relevant" maps to the source-span-driven content selection Riemann already does structurally.
7. **For "explain like I'm X" / analogy features (later), pair any generated analogy or simplification with the same source-span faithfulness check as audio (#4)**, and specifically watch for over-simplification collapsing a genuinely polysemous term (#9) — flag terms that appear in more than one L3 chunk with materially different senses, and don't silently simplify past that.
8. **Prefer deterministic checks over LLM-judge checks wherever one exists** (length, span-citation presence, schema validity); reserve LLM-as-judge for softer qualities (coherence, tone) where no deterministic proxy exists, and calibrate expectations to ~0.6 correlation with human judgment, not near-1.0 (#10).

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Length-band compliance rate**: fraction of generated L0/L1 nodes that land inside the target word-count band on the first pass vs. after one repair retry — a concrete, low-friction internal metric that also tells you if the density-first-pass approach (#8) is working.
- **Retry rate per level**: how often L0 vs L1 vs L2 needs a repair pass — if one level fails much more often, that's a signal the target for that level (word count, or density instruction) is miscalibrated for real content.
- **Span-coverage of generated text**: what fraction of a generated summary's sentences can be traced to at least one `source_span` — cheap to compute if the summariser is asked to emit span references per sentence (or per claim), and directly reusable as the faithfulness check from #3/#4.
- **Passive engagement with generated structure**: does the user linger at L1 (read it) or immediately zoom past it to L2/L3? A high "skip-through" rate on L1 is an implicit signal that generated summaries aren't earning their word budget — useful feedback for tuning the density-pass prompt.
- **User-triggered "explain this differently" requests** (if ever added): a proxy for when a generated analogy/simplification failed, without needing an explicit satisfaction rating.

## Contradictions and caveats

- The strongest headline claim in pop culture ("NotebookLM podcasts help you learn better than reading") is **not supported by any comprehension study I could find** — it's a plausible-sounding but unverified claim, and I'm explicitly flagging it as WEAK, not MODERATE, despite how often it's repeated in product writeups.
- The DiscoSum summary I retrieved (#4) read suspiciously generic — vague superlatives ("meaningful improvements," "substantially improves") without a single hard number — which itself is a caveat about tool-mediated summarization of papers: when a fetch tool's paraphrase contains no numbers, that's a signal to go back to the primary source before trusting the claim, which I'm flagging here rather than silently upgrading confidence.
- The clinical concept-map precision/recall figures (#7, PubMed 40380644) come from a bot-check-blocked page; I'm reporting search-snippet-level numbers, which carries real risk of being an LLM search-summary artifact rather than the paper's actual figures. Don't cite these numbers downstream without re-verifying against the actual paper.
- Findings #6 (length control) and #7 (concept maps) generalize reasonably well to Riemann's single-user, engineering-content use case since they're about model behavior, not population-specific effects. Findings #1/#2 (audio formats) and #9 (personalization/analogies) are weaker transfers: they're drawn from general-population or classroom studies, not from ADHD-specific or single-engineering-student contexts — worth treating as a starting hypothesis, not a settled design constraint, until validated against Sam's actual usage.
- The faithfulness numbers in #3 are from podcast (long-form, multi-speaker, TTS-adjacent) generation specifically — Riemann's L0-L3 text tree is a different generation target (structured, level-tagged prose, not conversational script), so the *magnitude* of the unfaithfulness risk may differ; the *category* of risk (LLMs drift from source under elaboration) almost certainly transfers.

## Gaps

- **Signposting/meta-discourse markers as an isolated, measurable variable in LLM summaries** (advance-organiser cues, "first/second/therefore," explicit transition words) — this is a real classic-reading-comprehension research area (structure-strategy instruction) that I did not find studied directly in NLP/LLM generation venues; worth a dedicated pass into education/reading-comprehension literature (not arXiv) to find effect sizes, since it's directly relevant to how Riemann's L1/L2 prose should be *written*, not just how long it should be.
- **Direct controlled comparison of two-voice conversational audio vs monologue vs plain text for comprehension/retention**, ideally in a single-adult-user, self-paced context (not classroom) — this is the single biggest evidentiary hole for the audio-format part of this subtask and would materially change whether Riemann should ever prioritize audio generation.
- **Interactive/interruptible audio UX patterns beyond NotebookLM's Interactive Mode** — what happens to comprehension/trust when a user can barge in mid-explanation vs. waiting for a natural pause; relevant to Riemann's "voice as a background channel" principle and worth its own deep-dive (interaction design + turn-taking research, possibly overlapping with wave-2 slug 14-interruption-ambient).
- **A rigorous read of the actual PaperWave and DiscoSum papers** (both resisted full-text extraction in this session) — the outline-first finding and the discourse-schema approach are promising but under-verified here; someone with better PDF-extraction tooling should re-open both.
- **How readability/length control interacts with technical/domain-specific vocabulary** (Sam's content is engineering coursework, not general-audience text) — all the plain-language and readability-control research I found targets general-public or legal/medical plain-language use cases; none directly addresses "simplify a control-systems paper without losing the technical terms that actually need to stay precise," which is closer to Riemann's real workload.
- **Cost/latency budget for multi-pass generation** (Chain-of-Density, catch-n-repair, length-retry) — this subtask found several 2-3-pass generation patterns that all improve quality but multiply LLM calls; nobody researched here has quantified the actual latency/cost tradeoff against the plan's target of a responsive, streaming zoom-dial UX. Worth a focused practitioner-blog search (Anthropic/OpenAI engineering blogs, or LangChain/LlamaIndex case studies) on production summarization pipelines' actual multi-pass cost overhead.
