# 17 — Beyond ADHD: generalising Riemann's zoom dial across populations

Researcher: claude-inclusion-researcher. Question: Riemann is built for Sam (ADHD). What changes per group, what stays universal, and what does the zoom dial need for accessibility specifically (keyboard, ARIA, reduced motion)?

Read first (per brief): 00-brief.md, 00-roster.md, 03-adhd.md, 02-cogload-design.md. This report treats those as given and does not re-derive CLT, W3C COGA's general existence, or ADHD-specific findings already covered there — it extends into the ten named groups plus zoom-dial a11y mechanics.

## TL;DR

- **W3C COGA's own scope already spans most of these groups** (ADHD, dyslexia, autism, memory impairment, anxiety, age-related decline) under one framework — the group-specific research below mostly sharpens COGA's generic patterns rather than replacing them; this is evidence *for* Riemann building one adjustable substrate, not ten different UIs.
- **Dyslexia-specific fonts (OpenDyslexic/Dyslexie) are a MYTH** for comprehension gain (already flagged in 02-cogload-design.md) but **increased letter/word/line spacing has real, repeated positive effects**, including a same-day, no-training effect on reading speed and error rate — this is a cheap, high-leverage, purely-visual setting distinct from font family.
- **Monotropism** (Murray, Lesser & Lawson 2005) reframes autistic attention as concentrated on few interests at once rather than deficient — the direct design cost is **task-switching/interruption**, described in the primary source as "truly, if briefly, catastrophic" when unanticipated. This is a much harder constraint on interruption/notification design than ADHD's fluctuating-capacity model, and the two should not be conflated.
- **Easy Read (intellectual disability) has a weak, contested evidence base** — a 2026 systematic review found Easy Read materials often have actual reading ages (13-14) far above their 8-year-old target, and no study showed statistically significant comprehension or behaviour-change gains attributable to Easy Read formatting itself. Treat "just make it Easy Read" as an unproven simplification heuristic, not a solved recipe — this directly echoes 02-cogload-design.md's caution about unverified "bullet points reduce load" claims.
- **ME/CFS / long COVID pacing uses a fundamentally different resource model than ADHD**: the "energy envelope" / spoon-theory framing treats cognitive effort as a *depletable, slowly-replenishing* resource where overuse causes delayed, multi-day crashes (post-exertional symptom exacerbation), not a moment-to-moment attention lapse. A capacity dial built around ADHD's spiky, same-session fluctuation model will misread this population's needs — they need a *budget that persists and rolls over across sessions*, not just a per-session slider.
- **Reading under acute stress (clinicians, crisis) and reading under chronic fatigue (ABI, long COVID) both degrade the same resources** (working memory, sustained attention) that ADHD degrades, but the recovery dynamics differ completely: stress is fast-onset/fast-offset, ABI fatigue is fast-onset/slow-offset with "recovery not complete despite rest," and depression/anxiety concentration deficits are chronic/baseline-shifted. The zoom dial's L0-first, low-commitment design serves all three, but auto-adaptation logic (if any) cannot use one decay curve for all of them.
- **Low vision and screen-reader users need the zoom dial to be a *sequential, announced state*, not a spatial/visual gesture** — a slider you drag with a mouse is unusable non-visually; the dial must be operable via discrete keyboard steps with each level change announced through an ARIA live region, and "the passage stays anchored" needs a non-visual equivalent (focus stays on the corresponding text node, not just visually stable pixels).
- **Non-native speakers (L2) benefit from text simplification for comprehension, but a 2025 study found AI-simplified and human-simplified text were *not* easier to comprehend than the original once readers' topic familiarity was controlled** — a caution directly relevant to Riemann's LLM-generated L0/L1 tiers, since the compression itself may not be doing the comprehension work people assume.
- **Universal-design principles (curb-cut effect) recur across nearly every group**: plain sentences, consistent structure/navigation, chunking with white space, low working-memory demand, user-controlled pacing, and an always-available "get unstuck" path. These should be Riemann's defaults for everyone. **Personalisation dimensions** (spacing/font, motion, audio pairing, session-vs-multi-day pacing, literalness of language, interruption tolerance) should be explicit settings, not inferred, because several of them (noise, motion, literal-vs-figurative) have effects that *reverse* between individuals or groups.

## Findings — by group

### Dyslexia (and comorbid ADHD+dyslexia)

**F1. Comorbidity between ADHD and dyslexia is large and bidirectional (25-40%), but the interventions barely overlap.** 30-50% of children with ADHD have a co-occurring reading disorder; 20-40% of children with dyslexia have ADHD, with shared genetic loading. Reading-fluency intervention studies found effectiveness for children with ADHD+dyslexia was **not reduced** relative to dyslexia-only — i.e. structured-literacy-style intervention still works with comorbid ADHD, but ADHD's own supports (executive scaffolding, medication) don't substitute for it.
Evidence: MODERATE (comorbidity rates from multiple secondary sources/reviews; the specific "intervention not weakened by comorbidity" claim is from one short report, not yet a meta-analysis).
Sources: [ADDitude — Dyslexia/ADHD comorbidity](https://www.additudemag.com/dyslexia-evaluation-adhd-comorbidity-overlap/), [PubMed — reading/spelling intervention in co-occurring ADHD+dyslexia](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41920745/), [PMC — shared neural correlates meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6873566/)

**F2. Dyslexia-specific fonts show no reliable benefit (confirmed, cross-referencing 02-cogload-design.md F: "Dyslexia-specific fonts...no reliable benefit"); increased letter/word/line spacing does, and with an immediate, no-training effect size worth noting explicitly.**
The clearest primary result: a controlled study found simple letter-spacing manipulation improved reading speed >20% and halved errors in dyslexic children, same-day, attributed to reduced visual crowding — not a font-family effect at all. A follow-up study (inter-letter spacing, inter-word spacing, and "dyslexia-friendly" fonts tested together) again found spacing, not font style, drove the readability gain. Very tight or very wide line spacing both hurt; ~1.5x line spacing is a reasonable default. Shorter line length independently helps struggling readers by reducing visual-tracking/working-memory demand during the return sweep.
Evidence: MODERATE-STRONG for letter/word spacing (a specific, replicated, non-training effect); already MYTH-tagged for font family (see 02-cogload-design.md).
Sources: [Extra-large letter spacing improves reading in dyslexia — ResearchGate/original study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225184694_Extra-large_letter_spacing_improves_reading_in_dyslexia), [Inter-letter spacing, inter-word spacing, and font with dyslexia-friendly features — Annals of Dyslexia, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11881-020-00194-x), [Shorter Lines Facilitate Reading in Those Who Struggle — PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3734020/)

### Autistic readers

**F3. Monotropism (Murray, Lesser & Lawson, 2005) is a theory developed by autistic researchers themselves, reframing autism as an attention-distribution difference (concentrated/monotropic vs. distributed/polytropic) rather than a deficit — with a specific, strongly-worded claim about interruption cost.**
The primary source states plainly that task-switching is exceptionally demanding for monotropic individuals, and that "every unanticipated change is abrupt and is truly, if briefly, catastrophic." This is qualitatively different from ADHD's "attention wanders unpredictably" — here the problem is not sustaining attention but being *forcibly redirected* from a state of deep, well-functioning focus.
Evidence: MODERATE (an influential, widely-cited theoretical/phenomenological framework, originated and championed by autistic scholars — treat as strong lived-experience evidence per the brief's sourcing norms, not as an RCT-backed causal model).
Sources: [Attention, monotropism and the diagnostic criteria for autism — Murray, Lesser & Lawson, 2005, full text](https://monotropism.org/murray-lesser-lawson/), [Sage Journals abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362361305051398), [National Autistic Society explainer](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/professional-practice/what-is-monotropism)

**F4. Autistic readers commonly report literal-language processing, strong preference for predictable/consistent interface structure, and heightened sensitivity to unexpected sensory events (motion, sound, flicker).**
Practitioner/UX-accessibility sources converge on: avoid idiom/metaphor in interface copy and generated summaries (a literal reading of "the meeting fell through" or figurative L0 gists could genuinely mislead); keep navigation and layout identical session-to-session; avoid flashing, autoplay, and unexpected sound; offer non-text alternatives (structure/visuals) alongside prose.
Evidence: WEAK-MODERATE (design-practice consensus and accessibility-industry sources, not controlled comprehension studies specific to autism; treat as directionally credible practitioner knowledge, consistent with COGA's disability-general guidance).
Sources: [Scope for business — Designing for people on the autism spectrum](https://business.scope.org.uk/designing-for-people-on-the-autism-spectrum/), [Accessibility.com — Sensory-Friendly Design](https://www.accessibility.com/blog/sensory-friendly-design-creating-digital-spaces-that-support-autistic-users/)

### Anxiety and depression

**F5. Anxiety and depression both measurably degrade the exact resources Riemann's abstraction tree is designed to economize — working memory, sustained attention, processing speed — via distinct but related mechanisms (worry vs. rumination).**
Depression is associated with executive dysfunction, impaired learning/memory, reduced attention/concentration, and lower processing speed. Anxiety self-report surveys found the large majority of respondents report reading-comprehension/concentration trouble. Rumination specifically is identified as a mechanism that "prolongs depressive mood and impairs executive functioning including working memory and attentional control, even in healthy individuals" — i.e. it's not just a symptom riding along with depression, it actively causes the cognitive impairment, including experimentally in non-depressed people.
Evidence: MODERATE (multiple converging secondary/clinical sources and at least one specific claim — rumination impairing working memory in healthy individuals — traceable to controlled experimental work on rumination-induction paradigms, though I did not open the primary RCT).
Sources: [Psych Central — Cognitive Symptoms of Depression](https://psychcentral.com/depression/the-cognitive-symptoms-of-depression), [AnxietyCentre — Reading Comprehension Problems](https://www.anxietycentre.com/anxiety-disorders/symptoms/reading-comprehension-problems/), [ScienceDirect — rumination and working memory in depressive/anxious tendencies](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691825014945)

**F6. Design implication distinct from ADHD: anxiety/depression's core problem is often *motivation and dread of starting*, similar to ADHD's Wall-of-Awful (03-adhd.md F17) but with a different emotional texture — low energy/hopelessness rather than shame-avoidance — meaning the "make L0 payoff near-instant" lever (03-adhd.md implication #2) likely transfers, but gamification-style engagement hooks (already flagged as weak for ADHD, F13) are even less appropriate here, since low mood is associated with reduced reward sensitivity, not just reduced initiation.**
Evidence: WEAK/PRELIMINARY (this is a reasoned extension from F5 plus 03-adhd.md, not a directly-sourced claim — flagged as inference, not finding).

### Long COVID, ME/CFS, chronic fatigue

**F7. "Energy envelope" pacing is a resource-management model fundamentally different in time-scale from any of the attention/capacity models above: overshooting the envelope causes delayed, multi-day crashes (post-exertional symptom exacerbation / PESE), not an in-session lapse.**
Pacing — staying within one's perceived energy envelope — is the only management strategy NICE's 2021 ME/CFS guideline recommends (explicitly *not* CBT or graded exercise therapy). It's WHO-endorsed for long COVID rehabilitation specifically because it reduces PESE episodes. "Spoon theory" is the popular lay vocabulary for the same finite, must-be-budgeted-across-a-day resource. Cognitive effort specifically is costly here because the brain uses ~20% of body energy on ~2% of body mass — for this population that ratio is "a profound vulnerability," not a metaphor.
Evidence: STRONG for pacing as the recommended clinical management strategy (NICE guideline, WHO endorsement); MODERATE for the specific brain-energy-vulnerability framing (stated by a patient-advocacy/clinical resource, plausible physiologically but not a controlled study in itself).
Sources: [Long COVID Physio — Pacing](https://longcovid.physio/pacing), [PMC — Pacing and energy management with digital tools in ME/CFS and Long COVID](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13405098/), [RTHM — Cognitive Pacing for Brain Fog](https://www.rthm.com/resources/blogs/cognitive-pacing-brain-fog), [Patient-Led Research Collaborative — Clinician's Pacing Guide](https://patientresearchcovid19.com/clinicians-pacing-and-management-guide-for-me-cfs-and-long-covid/)

**F8. Design implication distinct from ADHD: this population needs a capacity signal that (a) persists and is *cumulative across a day or week*, not reset per-session, and (b) treats "I have capacity right now" as potentially misleading — because PESE is delayed, feeling fine while reading is not proof that reading was within budget; the crash can arrive hours or a day later. A dial that only asks "how much can you take on right now" is actively unsuited to this group without an added retrospective/predictive layer (e.g. "how did yesterday's reading load feel today").**
Evidence: WEAK/PRELIMINARY (reasoned extension; the PESE-delay mechanism itself is well-evidenced in F7's sources, but no source directly proposes this specific UI pattern).

### Acquired brain injury and concussion

**F9. Post-ABI cognitive/mental fatigue is qualitatively distinct from ordinary tiredness: it appears more quickly, during non-demanding tasks, and "recovery is not complete despite rest" — a chronic, incompletely-resolving pattern.**
This is close in shape to the ME/CFS/long-COVID pattern (F7) but with a different etiology (injury vs. post-viral/unclear), and importantly it *also* directly damages the reading substrate itself (comprehension, concentration, word-finding, plus documented "acquired reading impairment" as its own clinical category), not just the energy available to deploy toward reading.
Evidence: MODERATE (peer-reviewed review articles on post-ABI fatigue and on acquired reading impairment specifically, though effect sizes/study counts weren't extracted in this pass).
Sources: [PMC — Mental fatigue and impaired cognitive function after ABI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6085903/), [Taylor & Francis — Acquired reading impairment following brain injury](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23279095.2023.2165923), [PubMed — Fatigue after ABI and socio-professional reintegration](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32374533/)

**F10. Standard clinical guidance for restoring reading after ABI is graded exposure (daily practice on manageable material, broadening to harder material over time) — this is structurally identical to Riemann's zoom-dial concept of re-entering the same content at higher levels of detail once the initial gist is comfortable, suggesting the tree itself (not just capacity signalling) is a plausible rehabilitation-adjacent tool for this group.**
Evidence: WEAK/PRELIMINARY as applied to Riemann specifically (this is an analogy from clinical practice guidance, not evidence that a zoom-dial tool produces this outcome).
Source: [Psychology Today — Reading Behavior After Brain Injury](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/concussion-is-brain-injury/202402/reading-behavior-after-brain-injury)

### Low vision and screen-reader users

**F11. Screen magnification and screen reading are functionally different assistive technologies with different failure modes for a "dial" metaphor.** Magnifier users still use a mouse/pointer and see a portion of an enlarged, potentially reflowed layout; a small viewport at high magnification makes navigating between related content (exactly what a zoom-level change does) difficult, and browser zoom in particular tends to *break* layouts compared to dedicated tools like ZoomText. Screen-reader users navigate via keyboard/semantic structure entirely non-visually — a visual analog-slider "dial" has no meaning to them at all unless it is exposed as a proper form control.
Evidence: MODERATE (multiple converging assistive-technology vendor and accessibility-practice sources; not academic literature, but domain-authoritative for AT behaviour).
Sources: [Vispero — How Screen Readers & Magnifiers Support Accessibility](https://vispero.com/resources/how-screen-readers-and-magnifiers-support-accessibility-compliance/), [BeMyEyes — Making Websites Accessible for Blind/Low-Vision Users](https://www.bemyeyes.com/business/blog/how-to-make-your-website-accessible-for-blind-and-low-vision-users/)

**F12. "Semantic zoom" (changing level of detail rather than visual scale) is an established HCI concept, and recent accessible-data-visualization research explicitly frames screen-reader exploration as needing the same structure/navigation/description dimensions that semantic zoom needs visually — i.e. the zoom dial's *concept* transfers non-visually if implemented as a navigable structure (headings/regions), not as pixels.**
Evidence: MODERATE (peer-reviewed HCI research on accessible data viz, a closely analogous but not identical problem to text-abstraction zoom).
Sources: [Rich Screen Reader Experiences for Accessible Data Visualization — Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Rich-Screen-Reader-Experiences-for-Accessible-Data-Zong-Lee/ac47d785610ab432b59375f4b1c2d97379050132), [A Review of Overview+Detail, Zooming, and Focus+Context Interfaces](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220566544_A_Review_of_OverviewDetail_Zooming_and_FocusContext_Interfaces)

### Older adults

**F13. Reading comprehension is preserved reasonably well into the "third age" (60-79) but declines significantly in the "fourth age" (80+); reading habit strength buffers decline in both bands.** Age-related changes also include weakened visual processing and vocabulary/language-comprehension retrieval, raising baseline readability requirements, and reduced short-term/working memory capacity makes complex interfaces specifically risky (information overload). Page margins and font size both have measurable effects on digital reading efficiency for older adults specifically (not just "make text bigger" — layout matters too).
Evidence: MODERATE (peer-reviewed studies, though single-study for several specific claims like page-margin effects).
Sources: [PMC — Reading Comprehension in Older Adults: Age, Education, Reading Habits](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11765946/), [PMC — Age-Friendly Web UI Design](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11689724/), [Influence of Text Interface Factors on Digital Reading in Older Adults](https://jps.ecnu.edu.cn/EN/10.16719/j.cnki.1671-6981.20230604)

### Non-native speakers (L2/ESL)

**F14. Text simplification measurably improves L2 comprehension speed and accuracy in controlled studies, and there's a well-established vocabulary-coverage threshold (95-98% known words needed for fluent comprehension) that's directly operationalizable.** But a 2025 study is an important caveat: comparing original, human-simplified, and AI-simplified passages, **neither simplified version was significantly easier to comprehend than the original once the reader's topic familiarity was statistically controlled** — suggesting some of the apparent "simplification helps" effect in less-controlled studies may really be a familiarity/vocabulary-coverage effect, not a pure syntactic-simplicity effect. This is directly relevant to Riemann's LLM-generated tiers, which are exactly this kind of simplification.
Evidence: MODERATE for the general text-simplification benefit (older, converging literature); MODERATE for the 2025 null/confound finding (single recent study, but well-controlled and directly on-topic for AI-generated simplification specifically).
Sources: [Comprehensibility of AI-generated and human simplified texts for L2 learners — ScholarSpace 2025](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/e03eae07-5d53-4936-9cb3-8c3f9c748921), [To simplify or not? — Rets 2021, JCAL](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jcal.12517), [Text Readability Assessment for Second Language Learners](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/333865922_Text_Readability_Assessment_for_Second_Language_Learners)

### Reading under stress (clinicians, crisis)

**F15. High-stress/time-pressured reading narrows attention and shifts processing toward heuristics, and this is the explicit rationale behind formal emergency-communication standards (the US National Incident Management System mandates plain language and common terminology specifically because jargon fails under stress).** Practitioner guidance for clinicians converges on: shorter sentences, one-or-two-step instructions at a time, and closed-loop confirmation ("teach-back" — ask the listener to restate the plan) — this last one has no direct analog in Riemann's read-only design (it never takes actions or asks the user to restate), but it does suggest a design pattern: a brief, explicit "does this match what you needed?" checkpoint at L0 could serve a similar confirmatory function without becoming an action.
Evidence: MODERATE (converging practitioner/standards sources — NIMS is an authoritative mandated standard; the cognitive-narrowing-under-stress claim is well-established stress-cognition literature, though I did not open a specific meta-analysis in this pass).
Sources: [Text it Easy — Plain language in crisis](https://text-it-easy.eu/blog/plain-language-in-crisis-communicating-clearly-during-emergencies/), [ACP Decisions — How to Communicate More Clearly with Patients](https://www.acpdecisions.org/how-to-communicate-more-clearly-with-your-patients-members/), [Readable — Reading in a crisis: readability of risk communication](https://readable.com/blog/reading-crisis-readability-risk-communication/)

**F16. This group is the strongest evidence that Riemann's L0-first design is not ADHD-specific at all — "reading under stress" is a universal, situational (not trait-based) state that any user, including Sam on a bad day, can be in. It argues for capacity/state being genuinely orthogonal to diagnosis, exactly as the brief's own principle states ("load capacity and emotional state are two separate, fluctuating signals") — this group is the clearest illustration of why that separation matters, since a clinician in a code situation has zero relevant "disability" but needs the exact same L0-first, minimal-extraneous-load behavior as anyone else at low capacity.**
Evidence: N/A (design-logic observation, not an empirical claim).

### Intellectual disability (Easy Read)

**F17. Easy Read's evidence base is weaker and more contested than its widespread policy adoption suggests.** A 2026 systematic review found real-world "Easy Read" materials frequently have actual measured reading ages of 13-14 years — far above the ~8-year target the guidance itself claims to hit — and found no study demonstrating statistically significant comprehension or behaviour-change gains attributable to Easy Read formatting specifically (as opposed to, e.g., simpler content generally). A companion review found support-person mediation, despite being "highly valued" by users, showed no significant RCT-measured improvement in comprehension, retention, or knowledge application. Common Easy Read guideline elements (10-16 word sentences, active voice, ≥14pt sans-serif, bullet points, defined hard words, images) are widely prescribed but largely untested individually.
Evidence: MODERATE-STRONG *for the claim that the evidence is weak* (this is itself a systematic review's conclusion, i.e. well-evidenced skepticism); WEAK/PRELIMINARY for any individual Easy Read guideline component's actual effectiveness.
Sources: [Easy Read Health Information: Systematic Review of the Evidence — PMC 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12893875/), [The Evidence for Easy-Read for People With Intellectual Disabilities — Wiley 2016 systematic review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jppi.12201), [Easy read — Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easy_read)

### The general substrate: W3C COGA

**F18. W3C COGA's own disability scope already spans ADHD, dyslexia, autism, memory impairment, anxiety, and age-related cognitive decline under a single set of ~8 objectives — meaning Riemann's ADHD-first design and this report's group-specific findings are, structurally, refinements within one accessibility framework, not evidence for ten separate UIs.** The objectives directly relevant to personalisation: "Help users focus" (minimize distraction, reorientation cues after disruption — directly serves autism's interruption cost, F3, and ADHD's attention lapses), "Ensure processes do not rely on memory" (directly serves ABI, ME/CFS brain fog, older adults), and "Support adaptation and personalization" (explicitly calls for adjustable white space/density and simplified-content variants matched to user preference — i.e. COGA itself, from a standards body, recommends exactly the kind of user-set dial Riemann is building).
Evidence: MODERATE-STRONG (as design-guidance consensus, consistent with how 03-adhd.md F11 already rated it).
Source: [W3C COGA — Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities](https://w3c.github.io/coga/content-usable/)

## Findings — universal design and zoom-dial accessibility mechanics

**F19. The "curb-cut effect" is a real, named phenomenon (originating from literal wheelchair curb cuts later used by strollers, luggage, bikes) with well-documented digital analogs — captioning, screen-reader-driven structure, and simplified/plain-language content all get used far beyond their original target population.** This is the strongest single argument for Riemann defaulting to accessible, low-load design for everyone rather than gating it behind a disability toggle.
Evidence: MODERATE (the effect and its digital analogs are well-documented across many practitioner/accessibility sources; it's a well-established concept, though "curb-cut effect" itself is more a named pattern than a quantified empirical result).
Sources: [Wikipedia — Curb cut effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curb_cut_effect), [BOIA — The Digital Curb Cut Effect](https://www.boia.org/blog/the-digital-curb-cut-effect-the-universal-benefits-of-web-accessibility)

**F20. WCAG 2.3.3 (Animation from Interactions, AAA) and the `prefers-reduced-motion` CSS media feature together give a concrete, already-standardized mechanism for the zoom dial's "anchored passage" transition to be safe for vestibular-disorder users without losing its object-constancy value for everyone else.** The harm model is specifically large-scale movement across the visual field (parallax, big pans/zooms causing dizziness/nausea); color/opacity/blur changes are explicitly *not* motion in this sense, so a crossfade-plus-instant-position-match (already recommended in 02-cogload-design.md for reduced-motion users) is both accessible and implementable as a drop-in CSS media-query branch, not a parallel feature.
Evidence: STRONG (WCAG is a normative standard; `prefers-reduced-motion` is a shipped, well-supported OS/browser-level API).
Sources: [web.dev — Animation and motion](https://web.dev/learn/accessibility/motion), [AAArdvark — WCAG 2.3.3 plain English](https://aaardvarkaccessibility.com/wcag-plain-english/2-3-3-animation-from-interactions/), [OpenReplay — Using prefers-reduced-motion](https://blog.openreplay.com/prefers-reduced-motion-accessible-animation/)

**F21. WCAG 4.1.3 (Status Messages, AA) requires level changes to be programmatically announceable, and ARIA live-region best practice gives specific, non-obvious implementation constraints the zoom dial must satisfy:** use `aria-live="polite"` for routine level changes (not `assertive`, which interrupts whatever the screen reader is currently reading — inappropriate for a user-initiated, non-urgent change); use `aria-atomic="true"` so the whole announcement ("Now showing: section summary, level 2 of 4") is read, not just the changed fragment; the live region must exist empty in the initial DOM and be populated via script (not injected fresh each time), and should be given ~2 seconds after insertion before content is pushed into it if created dynamically, for reliable AT pickup. This is a well-known gotcha space — a naive implementation (toast-style injected div) commonly fails silently in real screen readers despite looking correct in code review.
Evidence: STRONG (WCAG 4.1.3 is normative AA; the specific implementation gotchas are converging, detailed practitioner/standards guidance).
Sources: [accessibility.build — WCAG 4.1.3 Status Messages](https://accessibility.build/wcag/4-1-3), [UXPin — ARIA Live Regions for Dynamic Content](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/aria-live-regions-for-dynamic-content/), [Centre for Excellence in Universal Design — Use ARIA to announce updates](https://universaldesign.ie/communications-digital/web-and-mobile-accessibility/web-accessibility-techniques/developers-introduction-and-index/use-aria-appropriately/use-aria-to-announce-updates-and-messaging)

**F22. A slider-style "continuous dial" is not natively screen-reader-operable unless implemented as (or alongside) a proper `role="slider"` with `aria-valuemin`/`aria-valuemax`/`aria-valuenow`/`aria-valuetext` and full keyboard support (Arrow keys for fine steps, Home/End for the extremes, Page Up/Down for level jumps) — `aria-valuetext` specifically should carry the human-readable label ("Level 1 of 4: summary"), not just the raw numeric position, since numeric position alone is meaningless for an abstraction tree.** Combined with F12 (semantic-zoom-as-navigable-structure), the practical implication is that the dial needs two synchronized representations: a continuous visual slider for sighted/motor users, and a discrete, headed/landmarked document structure (L0/L1/L2/L3 as actual navigable regions) for screen-reader users, kept in sync rather than one faked as the other.
Evidence: MODERATE-STRONG (WAI-ARIA APG slider pattern is a normative authoring practice; the specific "two synchronized representations" recommendation is a reasoned synthesis of F11/F12/F21, not a single cited source).
Source: (WAI-ARIA slider pattern is standard practice; not independently re-verified via a fresh fetch in this pass — flag as a good target for the next wave to confirm against the current APG spec.)

## Design implications for Riemann

1. **Treat capacity as multi-timescale, not single-timescale.** ADHD's capacity signal (03-adhd.md) is legitimately per-session/spiky. ME/CFS and long COVID (F7-F8) need a signal that persists and is informed by *yesterday's* load, because the cost of overshoot is delayed. If Riemann ever generalises the capacity dial beyond Sam, it needs at minimum a "how did recent reading feel, in retrospect" input, not just "how do you feel right now" — these are different questions and conflating them will misserve the fatigue-condition groups specifically.
2. **Separate "spacing/typography" personalization from "font family."** F2 gives a concrete, evidence-backed lever (letter/word/line spacing, ~1.5x line height, shorter line length) that's cheap to implement as a user-controlled setting and helps dyslexia, ADHD (already covered), and probably low vision — but resist adding "dyslexia font" as a feature; it's specifically debunked (02-cogload-design.md, F2 here).
3. **Never auto-simplify literal language away without an opt-out, and audit L0 gists for idiom/metaphor.** F4 means a figurative one-liner ("the project hit a wall") is a worse failure mode for autistic readers than it is inconvenient for anyone else — this is a concrete QA check the abstraction-generation prompt/pipeline should include, not just a nice-to-have.
4. **Interruption design must distinguish ADHD's "attention drifts and needs re-anchoring" from autism's "forced task-switch is acutely costly" (F3).** Any future notification/interruption feature (per 00-brief's "voice as background channel") should default to *not* interrupting an active zoom-dial session at all, and if it must, should warn before switching context rather than switching abruptly — this is a stricter constraint than anything derived from the ADHD-only research.
5. **Don't assume LLM-simplified text is inherently easier — measure it.** F14's 2025 null result on AI-simplified text (once familiarity is controlled) is a direct challenge to an implicit assumption in Riemann's whole premise (L0/L1 = "simpler" = "easier"). This argues for treating comprehension gains from the abstraction tree as an empirical question Riemann itself should be able to test (see Signals below), not a given.
6. **Build the zoom dial's screen-reader path as a first-class, separately-designed interaction from day one, not a retrofit.** Per F11/F12/F22: implement `role="slider"` with full keyboard operation and `aria-valuetext` carrying level labels, and additionally expose each abstraction level as a landmark/heading-navigable region so screen-reader users can jump levels via heading/landmark navigation (a redundant, native AT interaction pattern) even without touching the slider at all.
7. **Wire level-change announcements as `aria-live="polite"` + `aria-atomic="true"`, using a persistent empty live region populated via script — not a freshly-injected toast** (F21). This is a well-known failure point; get it right once rather than debugging silent AT failures later.
8. **Gate all zoom-transition motion behind `prefers-reduced-motion`, with a crossfade + instant position-match fallback** (F20) — this was already recommended in 02-cogload-design.md for general reduced-motion users; this report adds the specific vestibular-disorder rationale and the WCAG 2.3.3 citation.
9. **For crisis/stress-reading and older-adult use, lean on the same L0-first, plain-language, chunked defaults already planned for ADHD** (F15-F16, F13) — these groups don't need distinct UI, they need Riemann's existing low-extraneous-load defaults to actually hold up under pressure or reduced processing speed, which argues for testing the *default* (not an ADHD-specific mode) under time pressure and with older-adult readers specifically.
10. **Do not adopt "Easy Read" as a checkbox feature without treating it as an open design problem** (F17) — the evidence for its component guidelines (sentence length, bullet points, images) is weak and inconsistently supported, mirroring 02-cogload-design.md's caution about unverified "bullet points reduce load" claims. If Riemann ever targets intellectual-disability users, this needs original usability testing with that population, not guideline-following alone.
11. **Universal defaults (curb-cut, F19): plain sentences, consistent structure, chunking with white space, low memory demand, user-paced advancement, always-visible "get unstuck"/help affordance** — apply to every level, every user, by default. This list is nearly identical to 03-adhd.md's W3C COGA-derived implication #8, which is the point: it's not an ADHD-specific list, it's the universal substrate ADHD research happened to surface first.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Level-change reversal pattern by *duration* of session, not just count** — ME/CFS/long-COVID users may show a within-session decline (increasing reversals/slowing as the session goes on) distinct from ADHD's more erratic, non-monotonic pattern (03-adhd.md) — the shape of the curve, not just the count, could eventually distinguish fatigue-driven from attention-driven struggle without ever asking the user to self-classify.
- **Next-day engagement drop after a long/deep-level session** — a proxy for PESE-style delayed crash (F8): if a user consistently reads less the day after a long L3 session, that's an actionable pacing signal specific to the fatigue-condition group, distinct from anything ADHD research would predict.
- **Comprehension/recall spot-checks comparing L0/L1-only engagement vs. L3 engagement, opt-in** — directly tests F14's open question (does the abstraction actually help, or just feel faster) — low-friction if framed as an optional occasional "quick check" rather than a quiz burden.
- **Idiom/figurative-language flags on generated L0/L1 text** — an automatable QA signal (not a user-behavior signal) addressing F4; could be a simple heuristic or LLM-judge pass over generated gists before they're shown.
- **Screen-reader vs. pointer input detection** (already inferable from standard web APIs) to decide whether to foreground the landmark/heading navigation path or the visual slider path — low-friction, already-available signal.

## Contradictions and caveats

- **Bimodal audio+text (03-adhd.md F7) and L2/non-native reading may interact differently than for ADHD.** The ADHD report found bimodal presentation helps weaker/less-skilled readers generally — L2 learners are, in one sense, "less-skilled" in the target language, so the same mechanism might transfer; but I found no source in this pass testing bimodal presentation specifically for L2 comprehension. Flag as untested transfer, not a confirmed win.
- **"Simplification helps" is not as settled as the fleet's baseline assumptions might suggest.** F14's 2025 null result (AI/human-simplified text not easier once familiarity is controlled) sits in real tension with F7's general text-simplification literature and with Riemann's entire premise that abstraction levels aid comprehension. This is the single most important caveat in this report: it should be treated as a testable hypothesis for Riemann itself, not an established fact even for the general (non-ADHD) population.
- **Easy Read (F17) and dyslexia fonts (F2) are both cases of "widely believed, weakly evidenced" — but for opposite reasons.** Dyslexia fonts have been specifically tested and found ineffective (a real negative result). Easy Read hasn't been tested rigorously enough to say either way (an absence of evidence, not evidence of absence) — don't collapse these into the same confidence level even though both end up "don't build this as a confident feature."
- **Monotropism (F3) is a theory *by and for* autistic people and should be weighted accordingly per the brief's instruction to label lived-experience sources — but it is not a peer-reviewed clinical-trial construct, and its strong claims about interruption ("truly catastrophic") are a phenomenological description, not a measured effect size.** Useful and important, but cite it as what it is.
- **Group boundaries are not clean.** Real users are commonly at intersections this report treats separately (ADHD+dyslexia, F1; ME/CFS with concurrent depression from illness burden; older adults with early cognitive decline vs. healthy aging, F13's third/fourth-age split). None of the adaptations above should be built as if a user picks exactly one identity from a list.
- **Almost everything here is secondary-source or single-study for the specific Riemann-relevant claim**, even where the surrounding science is well-established (e.g., W3C COGA is authoritative as guidance-consensus but is not itself an RCT). Treat this report as a map of where to look next and what to test in Riemann itself, not a final specification.

## Gaps (for the next wave)

1. **No source found tests a multi-level abstraction/zoom-dial tool directly with any of these ten populations** — same gap 03-adhd.md flagged for ADHD specifically (Gap 1 there), but broader: this appears to be genuinely unstudied territory across the board, which is either a risk (no evidence base to lean on) or an opportunity (Riemann could be a first mover generating real data). Worth a targeted ASSETS/CHI search specifically for "cognitive accessibility" + "progressive disclosure" + "reading tool" intersections.
2. **WAI-ARIA APG's current slider pattern spec was not independently re-verified in this pass (F22)** — a dedicated fetch of the current W3C ARIA Authoring Practices Guide slider pattern page, plus a check for any existing "level of detail" or "reading level" ARIA pattern precedent, would firm up the zoom-dial-as-control-type recommendation.
3. **Interruption/notification design for monotropic (autistic) attention vs. ADHD attention** is a sharp, underexplored tension for Riemann's future voice/ambient channel (00-brief's "voice as a background channel") — worth a dedicated cross-check with claude-interruption-researcher's 14-interruption-ambient report, since this report suggests the two populations may need opposite interruption defaults (ADHD arguably benefits from more frequent light re-engagement cues per delay-aversion; monotropic autism needs the opposite).
4. **Bimodal audio+text for L2/ESL comprehension specifically** — flagged as an untested transfer above; a dedicated search crossing "listening while reading" with "second language acquisition" (distinct from the ADHD-focused search already done in 03-adhd.md) would sharpen whether Riemann's planned audio channel should behave differently for non-native speakers.
5. **PESE/energy-envelope-aware UI patterns in any existing product** (analogous to 03-adhd.md's Gap 6 on medication-timing-aware UI) — nothing found in this pass on any tool that adapts to multi-day fatigue budgets; worth checking chronic-illness/disability-tech venues (e.g. ASSETS, or chronic-illness patient-advocacy technology reviews) specifically, since this looks like as much of a genuine product gap as the medication-timing one.
