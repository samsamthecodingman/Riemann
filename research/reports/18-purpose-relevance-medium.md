# 18 — Purpose, relevance, and medium: tailoring content to why Sam is reading

## TL;DR
- Relevance instructions reliably change *what* gets encoded and remembered, not just how carefully text is read: readers given an explicit purpose recall more purpose-relevant material without reading slower (McCrudden & Schraw's goal-focusing model). This is the strongest lever in this file for Riemann's abstraction levels — L0/L1 content should be conditionable on a stated or inferred reading goal.
- The self-reference effect (Symons & Johnson 1997 meta-analysis, 129 studies, d≈0.45 relative to semantic encoding) is real and moderately-sized, but it is an encoding-strategy effect from lab word-list studies — a weak base for assuming "this connects to your ECE4078 lab" will boost memory for arbitrary reading material the way it boosts memory for self-relevant words.
- Utility-value / self-generated relevance ("explain why this matters to you") boosts interest and performance, especially for low-expectancy learners (Hulleman & Harackiewicz 2009; Harackiewicz et al. 2016) — but only when the user generates the connection themselves. When an authority figure or system *tells* a low-confidence person why something matters, it can backfire and reduce interest (Canning & Harackiewicz 2015, "unintended consequences" line of work). This is a direct, concrete risk for any auto-generated "this matters for X" framing.
- Screen inferiority for expository text is a real, replicated, and apparently *growing* effect (Delgado et al. 2018 meta-analysis, g≈-0.21 to -0.27 for expository text, n>170k across 54 studies; Clinton 2019 meta-analysis, independently converging). It is absent for narrative text and is worsened, not fixed, by time pressure (Sun et al., PMC7463273) — undercutting any assumption that "just give people more time" mitigates it.
- The mechanism behind screen inferiority under time pressure looks like an attention-control failure, not a perceptual one: screen readers fail to suppress mind-wandering when demands increase, where paper readers do. This is compatible with, but not proof of, the "shallowing hypothesis" (Carr).
- Scrolling vs. paging: paging (fixed, spatially stable pages) produces better integrated comprehension and more strategic backtracking than continuous scrolling, especially for readers with lower working-memory capacity — directly relevant to whether Riemann's zoom dial should feel like discrete "pages" or a continuous scroll.
- E-ink vs. LCD: evidence is mixed/inconsistent on visual fatigue, and largely irrelevant to comprehension — this is not where Riemann's design effort should go.
- Advance organizers / previews reliably help comprehension (~1980 meta-analysis of 135 studies still the base evidence), which supports Riemann's L0→L1→L2 zoom order (gist before detail) but the *inverted-pyramid/BLUF-first* framing is a journalism/abstract-writing convention, not itself an RCT-tested reading-comprehension claim (see 01-abstraction.md, which already flags this — I did not find new experimental evidence beyond what's already logged there).
- Brief affect-regulation before a cognitive task has real support for anxiety specifically (Ramirez & Beilock 2011, expressive writing before an exam, Science) but the mindfulness/working-memory literature is genuinely mixed (positive Mrazek et al. 2013 result alongside null replications) — a one-line grounding phrase is plausible only as a very low-cost nudge, not a validated intervention.
- Gap flagged for next wave: nobody has tested purpose-conditioned *abstraction-level* selection specifically (i.e., does stating "I'm deciding" vs. "I'm studying for an exam" change which zoom level a reader should land on, not just which sentences get recalled) — this is the load-bearing open question for Riemann's design and isn't answered by any single existing literature.

## Findings

### 1. Reading purpose and goals

**McCrudden & Schraw's goal-focusing model.** Their 2007 *Educational Psychology Review* theoretical synthesis and follow-up empirical work describe four types of relevance manipulations — targeted segments, elaborative interrogation, perspective instructions, and purpose statements — and a four-stage goal-focusing model of how externally-provided relevance instructions narrow a reader's processing goals, which then guide attention allocation and memory. Their empirical finding, replicated in the mixed-methods follow-up (McCrudden, Schraw & Hartley, 2010; McCrudden 2009 in *Journal of Experimental Education*): readers given relevance instructions recalled more task-relevant text than non-relevant text, and read relevant segments **no slower** than readers without instructions — i.e., relevance instructions change *selectivity of encoding*, not just time-on-task. Evidence strength: **MODERATE-STRONG** (a coherent multi-study program from one lab, with a theoretical model, replicated across several designs, though not an independent meta-analysis). Sources: [Relevance and Goal-Focusing in Text Processing (Semantic Scholar)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Relevance-and-Goal-Focusing-in-Text-Processing-McCrudden-Schraw/0abb7cd71e1d4e0ac12b10705f948f68317a3061), [Exploring how relevance instructions affect personal reading intentions (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0361476X09000447), [The Effects of Relevance Instructions and Verbal Ability on Text Processing (Taylor & Francis)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220970903224529).

**Should Riemann ask or infer purpose?** The literature doesn't directly test "ask vs. infer," but it does establish that the instruction needs to be specific enough to narrow a goal (a vague "read carefully" doesn't produce the same selectivity as a stated purpose). For Riemann this argues for a **small, closed set of purpose primitives** (e.g., "deciding," "studying," "replying," "browsing") rather than open-ended free text, since McCrudden's manipulations were themselves closed instruction types (targeted segment, perspective, purpose statement), not open self-report. Given Riemann's zero-input principle, **inference from context** (calendar entry "ECE4078 exam Friday" + document opened Thursday night → studying-for-exam) is more consistent with the "useful with zero input" constraint than asking; asking should be a low-friction optional override, not a gate.

**Purpose-conditioned / personalised summarisation with LLMs.** This is an active but young area, not yet a mature evaluation literature. PersonalSum (2024) is a human-annotated dataset for personalised *news* summarisation using explicit reader profiles + past interaction history, and found that incorporating personalisation signals into prompts generally improves alignment with what a specific reader wants — but the iCOPERNICUS evaluation framework (companion paper) found only 2 of 17 tested LLMs actually achieved genuine in-context personalisation rather than superficial style changes, i.e., most models fake it. A separate line (intent-aware educational summarisation) builds explicit reader profiles (age, background knowledge, reading goal) and generates goal-tailored versions of the same source text. Evidence strength: **WEAK/PRELIMINARY** — small number of papers, mostly 2024-2026, no independent replication, and the negative iCOPERNICUS finding is a real caution: naive "personalise this" prompting is not reliably faithful to the stated goal. Sources: [PersonalSum (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03905), [PersonalSum (OpenReview)](https://openreview.net/forum?id=ETZk7lqyaF), [In-Context Personalization Learning / iCOPERNICUS (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.00149v1), [Intent-aware personalized summarization for educational texts (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44443-025-00333-7).

### 2. Self-reference and personal relevance

**Self-reference effect.** Symons & Johnson (1997, *Psychological Bulletin*, 129 studies) found a robust memory advantage for self-referentially encoded material over semantically encoded material, average effect size d ≈ 0.45 (smaller, but still positive, vs. other-referent encoding). Their moderator analysis attributes the effect primarily to the self being a rich, well-organized construct that promotes both elaboration and organization during encoding — not a "magic" self-tag. Evidence strength: **STRONG** as a memory-encoding phenomenon, but the base studies are almost entirely word-list/trait-adjective judgment tasks in lab settings, not "does linking a document to your ongoing project help you understand or remember *that document*." Transfer to Riemann's use case is **plausible but untested**. Sources: [Symons & Johnson 1997 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9136641/), [full text (UConn repository)](https://digitalcommons.lib.uconn.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1008&context=chip_docs).

**Utility-value interventions.** Hulleman & Harackiewicz (2009, *Science*) and the expanded program (Harackiewicz et al. 2016, "Closing Achievement Gaps with a Utility-Value Intervention") show that having students **write, in their own words**, how course material connects to their own life produces higher interest and better grades than a control (summarizing the material), with the largest gains for students who started with **low expectations of success**. Mechanism: self-generation appears to matter — the student constructs the relevance link rather than receiving it. Evidence strength: **STRONG** (multiple field RCTs in real classrooms, replicated across STEM domains). Sources: [Enhancing Interest and Performance With a Utility Value Intervention (ERIC)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ910428), [Closing Achievement Gaps with a Utility-Value Intervention (PDF)](https://sfbuild.sfsu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/Closing%20Achievement%20Gaps%20with%20a%20Utility-Value%20Intervention%20-%20Harackiewicz%C2%A0et%20al.,%202016.pdf), [The Benefits of Combining Value for the Self and Others (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6879189/).

**Where forced/fake relevance fails.** Canning & Harackiewicz's "unintended consequences of framing a utility-value intervention" work (and related Wisconsin/Motivate Lab findings) found that **directly telling** a student why material is useful can *backfire* for those already struggling or low in confidence — those students report *lower* interest and utility after being told, compared to a no-message control, apparently because being told "this matters" can read as a signal that they're expected to already see it, or invites unfavorable self-comparison. This is the opposite direction from the self-generation studies above, and the two together triangulate a clear design rule: **system-authored relevance statements are the riskier form; user-generated or user-confirmed ones are the safer, validated form.** Evidence strength: **MODERATE** (fewer replications than the core utility-value literature, but converges with the self-generation-vs-told distinction that runs through the whole program). Sources: [I Could Be Changing the World Right Now (Motivate Lab)](https://motivatelab.org/publications-2/2018/1/8/7htdy8ai7y5kvqjl6r2xvlys15d647), [Unintended consequences of framing a utility-value intervention (ERIC, PDF — not machine-readable via fetch, citation only)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED622933.pdf).

**Design risk for Riemann specifically:** an auto-generated "this matters for your ECE4078 lab because…" line is structurally the *told* condition, not the *self-generated* condition, and Sam is a plausible target for the backfire pattern if the system's guess feels wrong, forced, or patronizing (a known ADHD-adjacent sensitivity — unsolicited "helpful" framing can read as judgment). The utility-value literature's safest version of this feature is **prompting self-generation** ("why does this matter to you?" as an optional one-tap chip that logs Sam's own answer) rather than **asserting** a connection on his behalf.

### 3. Medium

**Screen inferiority (expository text).** Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman & Salmerón (2018, *Educational Research Review*, meta-analysis of 54 studies, >170,000 participants, 2000-2017): overall screen disadvantage g ≈ -0.21, concentrated in expository/informational text (g ≈ -0.27) and **absent for narrative text** (g ≈ 0.01). Notably the screen-paper gap has *increased* in more recent studies rather than shrinking as digital natives grow up with screens. Clinton (2019, *Journal of Research in Reading*, independent systematic review/meta-analysis) converges: print advantage for both literal and inferential comprehension, limited to expository texts, and print readers show **better-calibrated metacomprehension** (screen readers are more likely to be overconfident about how well they understood). Evidence strength: **STRONG** (two independent, well-cited meta-analyses converging on the same moderator pattern). Sources: [Delgado et al. 2018 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324993558_Comparison_of_reading_performance_on_screen_and_on_paper_A_meta-analysis), [TUM summary of Delgado et al.](https://www.edtech.tum.de/dont-throw-away-your-printed-books-why-reading-performance-is-better-on-paper-than-on-screens/), [Clinton 2019 (Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9817.12269), [EdWeek summary of Clinton](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/screen-reading-worse-for-comprehension-leads-to-overconfidence-new-meta-analysis-concludes/2019/04).

Note for the fleet: a newer meta-analysis (Kong et al. / Tandfonline 2022, "No Negative Effects of Reading on Screen on Comprehension of Narrative Texts Compared to Print") specifically confirms the narrative-text null result — consistent with, not contradicting, Delgado. Riemann's content is presumably mostly expository (articles, docs, notes), so this is the relevant regime, not the safer narrative-text case.

**Time pressure does not fix it — it makes it worse.** Sun, Shen & Chen (PMC7463273, "The inattentive on-screen reading," reading medium × time pressure design, n=140): under time pressure, screen readers' comprehension dropped *further* relative to print, and mind-wandering probes showed print readers successfully suppressed task-unrelated thought under pressure while screen readers did not. This directly undercuts the intuitive fix "just give a time budget/countdown to force focus" — for screen reading specifically, added pressure seems to recruit *more* attentional lapses, not fewer. Evidence strength: **MODERATE** (single well-designed study, not yet independently replicated at scale). Source: [PMC7463273](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7463273/).

**What does plausibly mitigate screen inferiority** (weaker evidence, worth flagging rather than trusting): metacomprehension calibration training (teaching readers that they tend to overestimate screen comprehension, prompting them to self-test) is suggested but not strongly RCT-validated in what I read; text type (narrative vs. expository) is the strongest known moderator; and several studies suggest device/format familiarity and reading purpose (skimming-intent vs. deep-reading-intent) interact with the effect, though I did not find a clean meta-analytic estimate for this interaction.

**Scrolling vs. paging.** Paging through digital text produces more coherent recall/summaries and more strategic backtracking than continuous scrolling (early foundational work by Piolat and by Chaparro/Baker replicated the pattern across designs); the mechanism proposed is that scrolled text lacks a stable spatial "place" for content, forcing readers to hold content in memory rather than using spatial/positional memory the way paper readers do — and this cost is worse for readers **lower in working-memory capacity** (a "To Scroll or Not to Scroll" finding). Evidence strength: **MODERATE** (consistent direction across several older and newer studies, no large meta-analysis found). Sources: [The Impact of Paging vs. Scrolling on Reading Online Text Passages (Semantic Scholar)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Impact-of-Paging-vs.-Scrolling-on-Reading-Text-Chaparro-Baker/535a87a6697b6c83730acd3ddd0867a4c15e92a0), [To Scroll or Not to Scroll (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/41668120_To_Scroll_or_Not_to_Scroll_Scrolling_Working_Memory_Capacity_and_Comprehending_Complex_Texts), [Is Scrolling Disrupting While Reading? (ISLS repository, PDF)](https://repository.isls.org/bitstream/1/497/1/18.pdf).

**Mobile vs. desktop.** Older work (Univ. of Alberta ~2010) found comprehension impairment on small mobile screens, attributed to higher working-memory load from seeing less text per view. More recent studies (post-2020, with modern high-resolution phones) find smaller or null differences in raw comprehension but still find behavioral differences: readers on mobile screens engage in more "skim reading" and spend less time per unit text, especially for dense/complex material, with a 2023 CHI paper characterizing distinct "deep" vs. "skim" reading profiles by device. Evidence strength: **MODERATE, and shifting** (older strong effect, newer studies suggest device hardware improvements have narrowed the raw-comprehension gap, but behavioral/skimming differences persist). Sources: [Characteristics of Deep and Skim Reading on Smartphones vs. Desktop (CHI 2023, ACM DL)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544548.3581174), [Analysis of Skim Reading on Desktop versus Mobile Screen](https://doi.org/10.3390/app11167398).

**E-ink vs. LCD.** Mixed and largely inconclusive for comprehension. One PLOS One study (2013) found e-ink lower in visual fatigue than LCD by blink-rate and subjective scales; other studies found no significant difference in fatigue, reading speed, or oculomotor behavior between e-ink and LCD, and comprehension differences are not well established either way. Evidence strength: **WEAK/MIXED** — treat any "e-ink is better for reading" claim as unresolved, not settled. Source: [E-Readers and Visual Fatigue (PLOS One)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0083676), [Reading on LCD vs e-Ink displays (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22762257/).

**Shallowing hypothesis.** Traceable to Nicholas Carr (*The Shallows*, not an empirical researcher — a science journalist/essayist), the hypothesis that habitual screen reading trains a generalized "skim and scan" mode that then impairs performance even on tasks requiring sustained deep reading. It is a plausible *interpretive frame* for the time-pressure/mind-wandering finding above (§ time pressure), but it is a hypothesis under active investigation, not itself a settled empirical result — flag as **WEAK/PRELIMINARY, pop-science-adjacent origin, but with some genuine supporting empirical threads** (the PMC7463273 mind-wandering data is the closest thing to direct empirical support found in this pass). I did not find a source connecting this specifically to Annie Murphy Paul; that appears to be a misattribution in the subtask brief — the concept is Carr's. Source: [Theories of Digital Reading (Springer chapter, discusses shallowing hypothesis)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-75121-9_1).

### 4. Pre-reading orientation

**Advance organizers / previews.** The classic evidence base here is old (Ausubel's original theoretical work, and a 1980 meta-analysis of 135 studies establishing a facilitative effect of advance organizers on learning and retention) but the direction is consistent across decades of follow-up: previewing structure before reading detail improves comprehension and recall, with the effect moderated by reader ability (good readers benefit from both comparative and expository organizers; poorer readers benefit more narrowly). This directly supports Riemann's fixed zoom order (L0 gist → L1 summary → L2 section gists → L3 full text) as a *default direction of travel*, independent of purpose. Evidence strength: **STRONG as a long-replicated effect**, though the base meta-analysis is 45 years old and mostly classroom-based, not digital-reading-specific. Source: [Effects Of Advance Organizers On Learning And Retention (UCF thesis review of literature)](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=4114&context=etd).

**"Read the conclusion first" — is there reading-research evidence, or is it only journalism practice?** As already noted in 01-abstraction.md, the inverted pyramid / BLUF convention is a **practitioner convention**, not an RCT-tested reading-comprehension finding. I did not find controlled reading-comprehension studies (as opposed to journalism-craft or military-writing guidance) that directly test "conclusion-first ordering improves comprehension or retention when reading time is cut short" as a lab manipulation. The closest genuine empirical support is indirect: (a) the advance-organizer literature (orientation before detail helps), and (b) the goal-focusing literature (a stated purpose/goal — which a conclusion-first line effectively supplies — improves selective encoding). Neither is a direct test of conclusion-placement per se. Flag: **the BLUF-first design choice is well-motivated by adjacent literatures but is not itself directly evidenced** — this remains an open empirical gap, not a debunked claim.

### 5. Emotional grounding before information

**Expressive writing before a stressful cognitive task.** Ramirez & Beilock (2011, *Science*) — two studies (lab arithmetic-under-pressure, and a real high-school classroom final exam) found that students who spent 10 minutes writing about their thoughts and feelings about the upcoming test just before taking it scored higher than controls, with the largest gains for habitually test-anxious students. Proposed mechanism: the writing offloads worry that would otherwise consume working-memory capacity during the test itself (worry directly competes with working memory, per the broader choking-under-pressure literature this builds on). Evidence strength: **STRONG for this specific paradigm** (published in *Science*, two converging studies including a real classroom RCT), but scope is narrow: **pre-exam anxiety**, not general "brief affect regulation before reading." Sources: [Ramirez & Beilock 2011, Science (DOI/PDF)](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc56677a8a9d82b40ce3085_RamirezBeilock2011.pdf), [Science.org abstract](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1199427).

**Brief mindfulness induction and working memory.** Mrazek et al. (2013, *Psychological Science*) found a 2-week mindfulness training course improved GRE reading-comprehension scores and working-memory capacity vs. a nutrition-education control — but this is a **2-week course**, not a "one-line grounding phrase." More directly relevant to Riemann's scale, single-session brief mindfulness studies show **genuinely mixed results**: some find reduced mind-wandering and improved comprehension/working-memory from a single ~15-minute session; at least one well-controlled study found **no** working-memory benefit from a single 15-minute breathing-focus session vs. a mind-wandering control condition. Evidence strength: **MODERATE for multi-session training, WEAK/MIXED for single-session inductions**, and there is essentially **no evidence** in what I found for anything as brief as a one-line phrase — that specific claim would be extrapolating two steps past the actual literature. Sources: [Mindfulness meditation improves cognition (ScienceDirect, Mrazek et al. 2013 summary)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1053810010000681), [Brief Mindfulness Breathing Exercises and Working Memory Capacity (PMC7911752, null-ish result)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7911752/), [Psychological Science press release on Mrazek et al.](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/brief-mindfulness-training-may-boost-test-scores-working-memory.html).

**Plausibility judgment for a one-line grounding phrase specifically:** there is no direct evidence either way. The Ramirez & Beilock mechanism (worry competes with working memory) is theoretically compatible with a very brief acknowledgment-of-state serving a similar (much smaller) function, but the dose in the actual studies (10 minutes of writing, or 15 min of guided breathing) is 1-2 orders of magnitude larger than a one-line phrase. Treat a one-line grounding phrase as **an untested, low-cost, plausible-but-unproven UX nicety** — reasonable to ship as an option, not to claim as evidence-based.

## Design implications for Riemann

1. **Condition L0/L1 content on a small, closed purpose taxonomy, inferred by default.** Given the goal-focusing model's requirement for a specific (not vague) purpose signal, and Riemann's zero-input principle, infer purpose from context (calendar proximity to an exam/deadline, document source, recent task list) into 3-5 closed categories (studying, deciding, replying, browsing/curiosity, reference-lookup) rather than free-text "why are you reading this." Surface the inferred purpose quietly (e.g., a small chip: "studying mode") so Sam can correct it in one tap — correction becomes the low-friction ask, not an upfront question.
2. **Never auto-assert personal relevance; prompt for self-generated relevance instead.** Given the utility-value self-generation vs. told asymmetry, do not synthesize lines like "this matters for your ECE4078 lab because…" as system output. Instead, offer an optional one-tap prompt ("why does this matter to you?") whose answer, if given, both benefits Sam (utility-value effect) and becomes ground truth Riemann can reuse later (safe, since it's Sam's own words) — a much lower-risk design than model-generated relevance framing, and it also sidesteps hallucinated/incorrect connections.
3. **Design the zoom dial as discrete, spatially stable "pages" per level, not continuous scroll**, per the paging > scrolling comprehension finding — especially valuable for Sam given ADHD-linked working-memory variability, since the scrolling cost is worst for lower-WM readers.
4. **Treat screen-inferiority and time pressure together as a joint risk for Riemann's core use case.** Since Riemann is explicitly for expository content read under real time/attention constraints (the exact regime where screen inferiority is largest and where time pressure makes it *worse*, not better), the zoom-dial's whole value proposition (reach chosen understanding in least time) has to fight this headwind by other means — e.g., leaning harder on structure (L0/L1 orientation, paging) rather than assuming "give people a countdown" will help; it may actively hurt.
5. **Default zoom order (gist → detail) is well supported** by the advance-organizer literature independent of purpose-conditioning — keep this as the always-on scaffold, with purpose-conditioning as a layer on top that reweights *which sections* get surfaced at L1/L2, not a replacement for the coarse-to-fine order itself.
6. **Grounding phrase: ship as an optional, skippable, near-zero-cost affordance, not a claimed intervention.** If included, keep it consistent with the *actual* evidenced dose-response — a single short line is closer to "friendly UI copy" than to the 10-minute-writing or 15-minute-breathing doses that show real effects; don't oversell it internally as evidence-based.

## Signals Riemann could measure

- **Inferred-purpose acceptance/correction rate**: how often Sam corrects the inferred purpose chip vs. leaves it — a cheap signal of inference accuracy and a training signal for the inference model.
- **Self-generated relevance opt-in rate and reuse**: whether Sam ever taps "why does this matter to you," what he writes, and whether that free-text later predicts faster path to his chosen zoom level on related documents.
- **Level-of-arrival by inferred purpose**: does "studying" purpose correlate with dwelling at L2/L3, "deciding" with stopping at L1? This would validate or falsify goal-focusing transfer to Riemann's specific levels.
- **Paging vs. scroll interaction telemetry**: backtracking frequency, re-visits to earlier sections, and comprehension-adjacent proxies (time-to-next-action) segmented by whether the dial is used in a paged vs. continuous-scroll interaction style, if both are ever offered.
- **Time-pressure context (proximity to a calendar deadline) × completion behavior**: does Riemann usage under known time pressure show the same "attention doesn't adapt" signature (rapid abandonment, shallow dwell) the screen literature predicts — this would tell Riemann whether to *actively compensate* (e.g., auto-suggest a lower zoom level) rather than assume the user will self-regulate.
- **Grounding-phrase engagement**: does Sam ever interact with/dismiss it quickly, and does its presence/absence correlate with anything measurable in session length or bounce — treat as an experiment, not an assumption.

## Contradictions and caveats

- The goal-focusing model's own base studies use **externally imposed, experimenter-authored** relevance instructions and still show benefit — this is in tension with the utility-value literature's finding that externally-authored relevance framing can backfire. The likely resolution is that goal-focusing instructions narrow *attention/encoding scope* (a cold, task-structural manipulation) while utility-value framing makes a claim about *personal meaning* (a motivational, ego-relevant claim) — these are different mechanisms and Riemann should not conflate "tell the reader what to look for" (supported, low-risk) with "tell the reader why they should care" (higher-risk, self-generation preferred).
- Self-reference effect studies are almost entirely trait-adjective/word-list paradigms; there is a real transfer gap to "does linking a whole document to an ongoing project improve comprehension of that document," which this pass did not find direct evidence for either way.
- Screen inferiority meta-analyses are dominated by studies using traditional web pages/PDFs on early-2000s-to-2010s hardware and typical browsing/scrolling UIs — it is not clear how much of the effect is "screens are inherently worse" vs. "typical digital reading UIs (ads, notifications, poor typography, scrolling) are worse," which is a confound Riemann, as a purpose-built minimal reading surface, might partially escape. Treat the effect size as an upper bound on the risk, not a fixed tax Riemann must pay.
- The time-pressure/mind-wandering study (PMC7463273) is a single study (n=140); promising and mechanistically clean, but not yet independently replicated at meta-analytic scale — weight it as suggestive, not conclusive, especially before hard-wiring any product decision (like disabling countdowns) around it.
- Mobile-vs-desktop findings are genuinely time-sensitive: pre-2015 studies (small, low-res screens) and post-2020 studies (large, high-DPI phones) disagree, and I could not find a recent meta-analysis reconciling them — treat the "comprehension gap has narrowed" conclusion as provisional.
- The BLUF-first ordering is well-supported *indirectly* (advance organizers, goal-focusing) but I found no direct RCT of conclusion-first vs. conclusion-last ordering on reading comprehension outcomes — this remains an assumption imported from journalism practice, as 01-abstraction.md already flags; I did not find new evidence to either strengthen or weaken that flag.
- "Shallowing hypothesis" traces to a journalist (Nicholas Carr), not to Annie Murphy Paul as the subtask brief suggested — likely a misattribution upstream; flagging explicitly since the brief asked to cite only sources actually opened, and no source connecting Paul to this term was found.
- One-line grounding phrases have **no direct empirical test** in the literature reviewed; the design implication above is explicitly labeled speculative to avoid the fleet overstating this as evidence-based in the final Riemann spec.

## Gaps (for a possible next wave)

- **Direct test of purpose-conditioned abstraction *level* selection**, not just sentence-level recall: does telling/inferring "I'm deciding" vs. "I'm studying" change which zoom level (L0 vs L3) a reader should be steered toward, and does steering them there (vs. leaving free navigation) help or feel controlling? This is the single most load-bearing open question for Riemann's adaptivity layer and nothing in the reading-purpose literature tests it directly — it would need a novel study or at minimum a closely-analogous HCI paper (IUI/UMAP venues) on adaptive-summarization-length-by-inferred-intent.
- **ADHD-specific relevance/motivation research**: the utility-value and self-reference literatures are from general/typical-development populations; ADHD-specific work on interest-based motivation (e.g., work building on Russell Barkley's "interest-based nervous system" framing, or ADHD-coaching practitioner literature on task initiation via personal relevance) would sharpen whether the self-generation-preferred design rule holds, or whether ADHD readers respond differently to system-offered relevance framing (this may be a job for whichever worker owns the ADHD-specific research file, 03-adhd.md — worth a direct ping since it overlaps).
- **Confidence/calibration interventions for screen reading**: since screen readers are reliably overconfident about their own comprehension (Clinton 2019), and Riemann explicitly wants to help users choose the *right* zoom level for their goal, research on real-time comprehension self-assessment prompts (e.g., "quick check: could you explain this back?") as a mitigation for screen-reading overconfidence would be directly actionable and wasn't covered here.
- **Query-focused / aspect-based summarisation UX**, beyond the metrics-focused NLP papers found here — specifically HCI evaluations of how users perceive and trust purpose-tailored summaries vs. generic ones (trust/skepticism effects), which overlaps with 11-illusion-trust.md and might be better owned there or in a joint follow-up.
