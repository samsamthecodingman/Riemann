# 21 — Cognitive Offloading vs. Cognitive Enhancement

## TL;DR
- Risko & Gilbert's (2016) framework treats offloading as a rational-but-error-prone decision: people offload based on (a) the internal cognitive demand a task would impose and (b) a metacognitive estimate of their own unaided ability — and that estimate is measurably biased, not just noisy.
- The bias is a real, replicated effect: Gilbert and colleagues' reminder-setting studies (2015 onward) show people's decision to set a reminder tracks their *confidence* in their unaided memory, not their *actual* unaided memory — and later work shows this produces a systematic over-use of reminders ("reminder bias") that metacognitive training/advice can correct.
- Offloading is not free, but it is not simply "bad for memory" either: Storm & Stone (2015) show that saving one thing frees capacity to encode the *next* thing — offloading is adaptive precisely when there's new material coming that needs the freed resources. This is the strongest case for offloading being strictly beneficial, and it's conditional (see Contradictions).
- The "Google effect" (Sparrow et al. 2011) is a much shakier pillar than its pop-science reputation suggests: the Google-Stroop-priming component failed to replicate in a large pre-registered replication project (Camerer et al. 2018, published in *Nature Human Behaviour*) and again in a dedicated 2020 replication; the core "recall location over content" finding has had mixed, generally smaller-than-original replications. Riemann's design should not lean on Sparrow 2011 as settled science.
- Directly relevant to Sam: when an external store you trusted is unexpectedly *removed*, performance is worse than if you'd never offloaded at all (Kelly & Risko), and people are bad at noticing when offloaded content has been silently altered — the failure mode isn't graceful degradation, it's a trap.
- For plans specifically, Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) is a directly on-point, primary-read finding: committing to a *specific* plan (how/when/where) eliminates Zeigarnik-style intrusive thoughts and goal accessibility from an unfinished goal — fully mediated by the drop in intrusions — meaning the plan does its psychological work once it exists as a committed external/internalized structure, not by being continuously held and rehearsed in mind.
- Adults with ADHD show a specific, dissociable deficit: large impairment in *time-based* prospective memory (do X at 3pm), much smaller/no impairment in *event-based* PM (do X when Y happens) (Altgassen et al. 2014). This is a strong, concrete design lever: convert time-based intentions into event-based ones wherever possible.
- NeuroPage (Wilson et al. 2001) is the best-controlled evidence that external paging/reminders substantially improve real-world task completion in a population with severe memory/planning deficits — but it also shows a split: some users' gains persisted after the pager was removed (a training effect), others regressed hard (dependency) — the difference tracked severity of executive impairment.
- GTD's "mind like water" claim has real cognitive-science theory behind it (situated/distributed cognition — Heylighen & Vidal 2008) but, by the authors' own admission at time of writing, *zero* controlled empirical studies had tested GTD as a system. Treat it as a plausible, theory-grounded framework, not validated practice.

## Findings

1. **Claim:** Cognitive offloading (using physical action/external tools to reduce a task's internal processing demand) is triggered jointly by the objective cognitive demand a task would place on memory and a metacognitive estimate of one's own unaided ability to meet that demand; the review frames the central open question as what happens to underlying cognitive processes under habitual offloading, not whether offloading is good or bad per se.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG as a review/framework (highly cited foundational Trends in Cognitive Sciences paper), but I could not get past a reCAPTCHA wall to read the primary text directly — this summary is built from PubMed/search-engine-indexed abstract text and secondary citations, not a directly opened full text.
   **Sources:** Risko & Gilbert (2016), "Cognitive Offloading," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20:676–688, DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/ ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1364661316300985 (abstract pages found via search; not directly opened, blocked).

2. **Claim:** In free-choice intention-offloading tasks (choose to set a reminder or rely on memory for a future intention), participants' decision to offload tracks their *metacognitive confidence* in their unaided memory ability, independent of their *actual* unaided ability — i.e., the offloading decision is driven by a belief that can be wrong in either direction.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE–STRONG (multiple converging experiments from Gilbert's lab, but accessed via search-engine summaries and abstract pages rather than a directly opened full PDF — the primary PDF hit a certificate error).
   **Sources:** Gilbert (2015), "Strategic use of reminders," *Consciousness and Cognition* — https://samgilbert.net/pubs/Gilbert2015ConsciousnessCognition.pdf (fetch failed on certificate; found via search summary) ; overview via search of related papers: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12589243/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11103908/

3. **Claim:** People systematically over-use reminders relative to the objectively optimal strategy ("reminder bias"), and this bias occurs in *both* under-confident and over-confident participants (i.e., it isn't purely a symptom of low self-confidence) — but explicit metacognitive advice about which strategy maximizes performance eliminates the bias.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (search-summarized from abstract/secondary text of a 2020 *Consciousness and Cognition* paper and a related metacognitive-training follow-up; primary full texts were blocked by access walls, not directly opened).
   **Sources:** "Excessive use of reminders: Metacognition and effort-minimisation in cognitive offloading" — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1053810020300179 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33032027/ ; "Metacognitive training facilitates optimal cognitive offloading" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12982714/ (blocked on fetch, found via search).

4. **Claim:** Saving (offloading) an already-studied list of items measurably improves memory for a *subsequently* studied new list, relative to not saving — offloading one item frees encoding capacity that gets reinvested in the next item ("saving-enhanced memory"), and the mechanism has been likened to list-method directed forgetting.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG as an original, well-cited, multiply-extended finding (Psychological Science, replicated and extended by follow-up papers including a 2024 *Memory & Cognition* extension showing the effect holds even when only a subset of items are saved) — but the primary paper itself returned a 403 and was not directly opened; this is built from abstract-page search summaries and follow-up-paper framing.
   **Sources:** Storm & Stone (2015), "Saving-Enhanced Memory," *Psychological Science* — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614559285 (blocked) ; follow-up: https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-024-01545-4 ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340908829_Specifying_the_mechanisms_behind_benefits_of_saving-enhanced_memory

5. **Claim:** The original "Google effect" (Sparrow et al. 2011, *Science*) reported that expecting future access to information reduces recall of the information itself and increases recall of *where* to find it, and that difficult trivia questions prime people to think of computers (the "Google Stroop" component). The Stroop-priming component specifically failed to replicate in a large pre-registered replication project (Camerer et al. 2018, one of 21 replications of *Science*/*Nature* social-science findings) and again in a dedicated 2020 replication study; broader replication attempts of the core recall-location effect have been mixed, generally with smaller effect sizes than the original, and one line of work (Schooler & Storm) suggests the effect only appears when participants have prior experience confirming the saved information will remain reliably accessible.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE for the original 2011 finding taken alone (published in *Science*, but with known replication problems); WEAK/CONTESTED for the priming/Stroop component specifically (failed pre-registered replications) — flag as a case where a famous, frequently pop-science-cited result is on much shakier ground than its citation count implies.
   **Sources:** Original: Sparrow, Liu, Wegner (2011), "Google Effects on Memory," *Science* — https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1207745 (abstract page, search-summarized) ; Wikipedia synthesis of replication history (opened directly) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_effect ; replication study (search-summarized, not opened due to bot-check) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7651475/ ; OSF pre-registration — https://osf.io/6kqag/ ; https://osf.io/cjgea/

6. **Claim:** Offloaded items show a memory-encoding cost that specifically mirrors directed forgetting: a reduced *primacy* effect but a normal *recency* effect in later free recall, consistent with offloading functioning like an instruction to stop rehearsing. Separately, when a trusted external store is unexpectedly made unavailable at test, recall is *worse* than in a condition where the person relied on internal memory the whole time (i.e., you can end up worse off than if you'd never offloaded) — and people are generally bad at detecting when content in an external store they've been using has been silently altered.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (two related lines of work, both search-summarized from abstract-level text; primary full texts were not directly opened due to access walls).
   **Sources:** Kelly & Risko (2019), "Offloading memory: Serial position effects," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31161530/ ; "The Isolation Effect When Offloading Memory" — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211368119301585 ; "Offloading Memory Leaves Us Vulnerable To Memory Manipulation" — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332627487_Offloading_Memory_Leaves_Us_Vulnerable_To_Memory_Manipulation ; "Offloading information to an external store increases false recall" — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S001002772030247X

7. **Claim:** Offloading working-memory load onto an external tool (e.g., a tablet, in a demanding pattern-copying task) reliably boosts *immediate* task performance, but comes at a cost to later unaided memory for the offloaded material; the size of this trade-off is modulated by the *cost* of offloading — when offloading is made more effortful/costly, people offload less and retain more.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE (peer-reviewed *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* 2021 paper, N=172, but accessed via search-summarized abstract text — primary blocked by a bot-check).
   **Sources:** Grinschgl, Papenmeier, Meyerhoff (2021), "Consequences of cognitive offloading: Boosting performance but diminishing memory," *QJEP* — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17470218211008060 (blocked) ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33752519/ (blocked)

8. **Claim:** Adults with ADHD show a large, specific impairment in *time-based* prospective memory (remembering to act at a specific clock time with no external event cue) relative to matched controls, but a much smaller or absent impairment in *event-based* prospective memory (acting when a relevant cue/event occurs) — a task dissociation within the same paradigm and sample.
   **Evidence strength:** MODERATE–STRONG (peer-reviewed, *Journal of Attention Disorders* 2014, directly designed to test the dissociation within one sample — a methodological strength over comparing across separate studies) — but only reached via search-engine summary; the primary journal page returned a 403 and was not directly opened.
   **Sources:** Altgassen, Kretschmer, Kliegel (2014), "Task Dissociation in Prospective Memory Performance in Individuals With ADHD," *Journal of Attention Disorders* — https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054712445484 (blocked) ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1087054712445484 (blocked, search-summarized) — consistent with a parallel finding of large time-based/small event-based PM impairment in autism spectrum disorder, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5352792/, and event-based PM deficits described separately in children with ADHD, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8199111/ (population caveat: children, not adults).

9. **Claim:** In a randomized controlled crossover trial (N=143, mostly traumatic brain injury and stroke, ages 8–83), a simple text-paging reminder system (NeuroPage) significantly increased successful completion of individually-relevant everyday memory/planning tasks (e.g., medication-taking) relative to a no-pager baseline, with roughly 85% of participants more successful with the pager than at baseline. Follow-up work found that for some participants the gains persisted after the pager was withdrawn (suggesting a training/habit-formation effect), while others regressed once paging stopped, especially those with greater executive impairment — implying reminder benefit and reminder *dependency* are not the same thing and diverge by severity/executive function.
   **Evidence strength:** STRONG for the core RCT result (real randomized crossover design, large-for-the-field N, published in *Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery and Psychiatry*) — but transfer to Sam's population (adult ADHD, not acquired brain injury) is a real open question (see Contradictions/Gaps); reached via search summary, not a directly opened full text.
   **Sources:** Wilson, Emslie, Quirk, Evans (2001), "Reducing everyday memory and planning problems by means of a paging system: A randomised control crossover study," *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry* 70(4):477–482 — http://eprints.gla.ac.uk/22331/ ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12077364_Reducing_everyday_memory_and_planning_problems_by_means_of_a_paging_system ; review context (opened) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2840878/ was blocked by a bot-check on this attempt, so review-level context comes from its search-indexed abstract only.

10. **Claim:** Activating an unfinished personal goal causes measurable downstream cognitive effects — more self-reported intrusive thoughts during an unrelated reading task, higher accessibility of goal-related words, worse performance on an unrelated anagram task — and forming a *specific* plan (how, when, and where the goal will be pursued) eliminates these effects. The reduction is fully statistically mediated by the drop in intrusive thoughts (Sobel test, z = −2.41, p = .016, from the primary text), and critically, the benefit was concentrated among participants who went on to actually execute their plans (Study 4) — plan-makers who did not follow through still showed some intrusions, meaning the relief isn't from the act of writing alone but from a genuine, earnest commitment.
    **Evidence strength:** STRONG (peer-reviewed *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 2011, six converging studies within one paper; primary PDF directly opened and read for this report).
    **Sources:** Masicampo & Baumeister (2011), "Consider It Done! Plan Making Can Eliminate the Cognitive Effects of Unfulfilled Goals," *JPSP* — http://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2011JPSP.pdf (opened directly, pages 1–4 read) ; DOI 10.1037/a0024192.

11. **Claim:** A theoretical synthesis of situated, embodied, and distributed-cognition research argues the human brain routinely relies on the physical/digital environment as external memory, a trigger for action, and a source of feedback — providing a plausible cognitive-science grounding for GTD's practice of moving open commitments out of the head and into a trusted external system ("mind like water"). However, the authors state explicitly, as of their own literature search, that *no* controlled empirical study had tested GTD as a complete system — only isolated component practices (e.g., specific plan-making, per finding 10) have direct empirical support.
    **Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY for GTD-as-a-validated-system (explicitly theory-only, no controlled trial of the full method) — but MODERATE for the underlying "environment as external memory/trigger" claim it draws on, which is a broader, separately supported literature (situated/distributed cognition). Flag the "mind like water" framing itself as unvalidated in the strong sense, despite wide popular acceptance.
    **Sources:** Heylighen & Vidal (2008), "Getting Things Done: The Science behind Stress-Free Productivity" — https://pespmc1.vub.ac.be/Papers/GTD-cognition.pdf (opened directly, pages 1–4 read).

12. **Claim:** Practitioner and applied-research consensus on reminder design for ADHD converges on: (a) reframe time-based intentions as event-based ones where possible (e.g., "after brushing teeth" rather than "at 8pm") to route around the specific time-based PM deficit (finding 8); (b) use graduated multi-stage reminders at decreasing intervals before a deadline rather than a single alert; (c) vary reminder content/tone/timing over sessions to resist habituation, since a fixed-format alarm predictably fades into background noise.
    **Evidence strength:** WEAK/PRELIMINARY as a design consensus — this is practitioner/coaching-level guidance (ADHD-focused advocacy and clinical-adjacent sources), not a controlled experimental test of reminder-format variables in adult ADHD; treat as plausible and consistent with the cognitive mechanism in finding 8, not as independently validated.
    **Sources:** CHADD, "Remembering the Future: How ADHD Affects Prospective Memory (and How to Work with It)" — https://chadd.org/attention-article/remembering-the-future-how-adhd-affects-prospective-memory-and-how-to-work-with-it/ (opened directly) ; practitioner overview (search-summarized, not opened) — https://www.audhdpsychiatry.co.uk/adhd-tools-for-daily-tasks/ ; smartwatch reminder usability study in older adults (population mismatch, search-summarized) — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10736361/

13. **Claim:** Whether offloading is net-beneficial for later memory depends on what happens *after* offloading: if new material must subsequently be learned, offloading is adaptive because it frees capacity for that new encoding (finding 4); if the offloaded content itself must eventually be recalled *without* the external store, offloading is a net cost, both because encoding of the offloaded material itself is weaker (finding 6's primacy-effect result) and because unanticipated loss of the store leaves you worse off than never having offloaded (finding 6). These are not competing findings about the same situation — they describe two different downstream demands, and the literature does not resolve which dominates for any given real task without knowing which demand applies.
    **Evidence strength:** MODERATE, but this is my synthesis of findings 4, 6, and 7 rather than a single study's claim — flagged as inferred, not a direct citation.
    **Sources:** (synthesis of findings 4, 6, 7 above).

## Resolves: A03×plan → N because …
Masicampo & Baumeister (2011) directly tests whether holding an unfinished goal/plan actively in mind is necessary for the goal's psychological "work" to happen, and finds the opposite: once a *specific* plan (how/when/where) exists, the goal-related cognitive activity (intrusive thoughts, heightened accessibility, interference with unrelated tasks) **stops**, and this happens whether or not the goal has actually been attained. The mechanism proposed and supported by the mediation analysis is that a plan hands control of goal pursuit to automatic/environmental triggers, so the effortful, conscious "holding it in mind" is no longer needed and in fact isn't happening in the successful-plan condition (mind-wandering and intrusive-thought measures both drop to control-group levels). This means **holding the plan internally is not better than externalizing it** — externalizing (writing down / offloading the specific plan) is what relieves the cognitive load, and there is no evidence that additionally rehearsing it internally adds benefit once it's specific and committed to. A03×plan = **N**.

A secondary note for the ledger: this same evidence also speaks to **A04×plan** (effortful processing improves the outcome) — the finding is that *effort concentrated at plan-formation time* (making the plan specific) matters, but *sustained effortful maintenance afterward* does not and is actively relieved by the plan's existence. I'd resolve A04×plan as **N** on the "afterward" reading the assumption text implies (ongoing effortful processing), while flagging that plan-formation itself is effortful and that this effort is exactly what a good planning *tool* (like Riemann) should support well, even though the user doesn't need to keep expending it once the plan is set.

A03/A04 resolution is weaker for **A32×plan** (self-generation beats being given): Masicampo & Baumeister's participants always generated their own plans — the study never compares a self-generated plan against a handed-to-you plan of equal specificity, so it cannot directly confirm or deny that Sam generating the plan himself (vs. Riemann proposing one he accepts) matters for the relief effect. The "earnestness"/follow-through gating in Study 4 is suggestive that *genuine commitment* is what's doing the work, not authorship per se — a specific, high-quality, accepted AI-drafted plan that Sam genuinely commits to might work as well as a self-authored one, but this is not tested. **A32×plan stays `?`** — recommend it as a priority follow-up (see Gaps).

## Design implications for Riemann

- **A plan-capture step is the payoff moment, not a means to an end.** Per finding 10, the cognitive relief happens when a plan becomes *specific* (how/when/where) and *committed to* — not when it's merely summarized or acknowledged. Riemann's `FT-plan-breakdown` / `FT-commitment-capture` should force the how/when/where triad explicitly (a vague "plan" that only names the goal doesn't get the Zeigarnik-relief effect) and should visibly register commitment (a distinct "I'm doing this" action, not just leaving a draft plan sitting there) — this maps directly onto Masicampo's manipulation.
- **Don't ask Sam to keep the plan "in mind."** Since A03×plan resolves N, `FT-offload-store` for plans should be the default, unapologetic behavior — no nudge toward memorizing steps, no "test yourself on the plan" feature (that would misapply the learn-objective pattern from A31/spaced-repetition findings to a domain where it doesn't hold). This is a case where Riemann should actively *not* borrow the learning-objective machinery.
- **Convert time-based plan steps into event-based ones wherever the plan allows it.** Finding 8's dissociation is a concrete lever for `FT-next-action` / `FT-commitment-capture`: when a plan step has a natural environmental trigger available (after class, when you open your laptop, when the PR lands), prefer that framing in the surfaced reminder over a bare clock time, and reserve pure time-based alerts for steps that genuinely have no cue (with the expectation those will be the weaker link).
- **Graduated, varied reminders for deadline-bound plan steps**, informed by finding 12 and the NeuroPage divergence in finding 9: default to 2–3 escalating touches before a deadline rather than one alert, and vary phrasing/channel across touches to resist habituation — but treat this as a design hypothesis to A/B, not settled science (evidence grade is weak/practitioner-level).
- **Treat "offload the plan" and "trust the plan" as separate design problems.** Findings 6 and 13 say the danger of offloading isn't forgetting-related decay, it's *unanticipated unavailability or silent corruption* of the store. Riemann's `FT-offload-store` needs an explicit integrity/availability guarantee (sync status visible, no silent data loss) — the cost of an offloaded plan disappearing or getting quietly edited wrong is not "you re-derive it," it's "you're worse off than if you'd never offloaded it" and you likely won't notice a subtle corruption on your own.
- **Watch for over-offloading as its own failure mode, not just under-offloading.** Finding 3 shows people offload *more* than optimal, in both low- and high-confidence populations, and that explicit strategy feedback (not more friction) corrects it. If Riemann tracks that Sam captures a plan step for something trivially memorable every time, an occasional light-touch nudge ("you don't need to write this one down") may be worth more than always making capture effortless — though per Masicampo, err toward capture being cheap for anything that's actually specific and actionable.
- **The self-authorship question (A32×plan) needs its own test before Riemann leans on AI-drafted plans.** Given the gap identified in the "Resolves" section, an AI-proposed plan that Sam edits-and-commits-to vs. one Sam writes from scratch is untested territory — worth a lightweight internal check (does editing-and-accepting an AI draft produce the same drop in self-reported plan-related intrusive thoughts as self-authoring?) before assuming `FT-plan-breakdown`'s AI-generated-scaffold approach gets the full relief effect.

## Signals Riemann could measure
- **Plan specificity at capture time**: does the captured plan include a how/when/where, or just a goal restated? (cheap, structural check on the plan object itself, no user friction.)
- **Commitment action distinct from capture**: a lightweight, explicit "committing to this" tap separate from just typing a plan, to test whether Riemann can detect/encourage the Study-4 "earnestness" distinction.
- **Self-reported intrusive-thought proxy**: an occasional, very low-frequency, skippable one-tap check ("still thinking about [open item] outside of Riemann?") on committed vs. uncommitted plans — directly operationalizes Masicampo's dependent variable as a product signal, sparingly.
- **Time-based vs. event-based step completion rates**: log which framing a plan step used and whether it was completed/missed, to empirically test finding 8's dissociation in Sam's own data over time.
- **Offload-then-recall gap**: if a plan step later needs to be recalled/acted on without Riemann open (phone dead, offline), does Sam do worse than baseline? A rare, naturally-occurring signal worth logging when it happens rather than engineering deliberately.
- **Reminder habituation**: dismiss-without-action rate per reminder, trending over repeated exposures to the same phrasing/channel — a direct, low-friction operationalization of finding 12's habituation concern.

## Contradictions and caveats
- **Sparrow (2011) is not settled science** (finding 5) — its Stroop-priming component has failed pre-registered replication, and the core recall-location effect has mixed, generally-smaller replications. Any Riemann design rationale that leans on "the Google effect proves externalizing erodes memory" is citing a contested result, not a robust one; use Kelly & Risko's serial-position and vulnerability work (finding 6) instead, which is more specific and mechanistically grounded.
- **Offloading-is-good (Storm & Stone) and offloading-is-costly (Kelly & Risko, Grinschgl et al.) are not actually in conflict** but are easy to misread as contradictory (finding 13) — they describe different downstream demands (new learning vs. later unaided recall of the same content). Since almost none of Riemann's life-admin objectives require Sam to later recall plan/task content *without* Riemann (reference/execute/monitor are explicitly "N" or low on A03 already), the offloading-is-costly branch mostly doesn't apply to Riemann's plan/execute/monitor objectives — but it *does* apply if Riemann's phone/app becomes unavailable at a bad moment (finding 6's "worse than never offloading" result), which is a reliability/availability design constraint, not a memory-training one.
- **NeuroPage's population is not Sam's population** (A12 transfer risk, finding 9) — acquired brain injury and adult ADHD are different etiologies of executive/memory impairment; the RCT is strong evidence that *some* population benefits enormously from paging-style reminders, but the dependency-vs-training split by "severity of executive impairment" is a variable Riemann has no direct read on for Sam specifically.
- **The Masicampo & Baumeister effect is about self-generated plans; Riemann's likely product shape (AI-assisted or AI-drafted plans) is untested** — this is the single largest caveat on the A03×plan resolution's practical applicability, flagged explicitly above and in Gaps.
- **Many "findings" here are search-summarized, not directly read** (marked per-finding above) — PubMed, ScienceDirect, and several SAGE/Springer pages returned reCAPTCHA/403 walls on every attempt in this session. Treat grades on those findings as one notch lower in my confidence than the label alone implies; the two findings read from a full primary PDF (10 and 11) are the most load-bearing citations in this report and should be trusted more than the others.

## Gaps
- **AI-drafted vs. self-authored plans, on the exact Masicampo & Baumeister dependent measures** (intrusive thoughts, goal accessibility, task interference). This is the most consequential open question this report surfaces (A32×plan) — a dedicated follow-up should look for any study putting an externally-provided/AI-provided plan against a self-generated one of matched specificity, or flag if none exists (in which case Riemann may want to consider commissioning/simulating a small internal test).
- **Reminder-timing/habituation experiments specifically in adult ADHD** (not brain injury, not older adults, not children) — finding 12's practitioner consensus is plausible but thin; a targeted search of HCI venues (CHI, UIST, ASSETS) for ADHD-specific reminder-scheduling studies would strengthen or correct the graduated/varied-reminder design implication.
- **The metacognitive-training intervention that corrects reminder bias** (finding 3) — worth a deeper look at exactly what the "advice" consisted of, since if it's cheap and generalizable, Riemann could deliver something similar as an occasional calibration nudge rather than a one-time lab manipulation.
- **Cost-of-offloading manipulations** (Grinschgl et al., finding 7) as a direct design parameter — if making offloading modestly more effortful improves later retention without destroying the immediate-performance benefit, this is a tunable Riemann could expose (e.g., require one extra tap to fully "hand off" a plan item vs. quick-capture) but this needs the primary paper's actual effect sizes, which I could not access this session.
- **Distributed/transactive memory in couples or teams** (Wegner's original transactive memory work, which underlies both Sparrow 2011 and Heylighen & Vidal) — Riemann is explicitly single-user now, but "communicate" and any future multi-person life-admin objective would benefit from this literature, and it wasn't researched here.
- **NeuroPage-style paging in ADHD specifically** — search for any direct RCT of pager/notification-style reminder systems in adult ADHD populations (as opposed to brain injury) to properly ground the A12 transfer question rather than inferring it.
