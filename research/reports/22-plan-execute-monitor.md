# 22 — Plan, execute, monitor: task decomposition, self-generated plans, commitment, checklists, and monitoring digests

## TL;DR
- **Proximal subgoals** have direct experimental support (Bandura & Schunk 1981): children given a series of near-term subgoals beat both a single distal goal and no-goal conditions on self-efficacy, skill mastery, and intrinsic interest — the distal-goal condition was barely better than no goal at all. This is a strong, mechanism-clear argument for FT-plan-breakdown over "here's the deadline, go."
- **Specific + difficult goals beat "do your best"** (Locke & Latham) — one of the most replicated findings in organizational psychology (96% of comparisons favor hard/specific goals; meta-analytic average ~17% performance gain) — but this is an *execution-performance* effect, not evidence that fine-grained decomposition itself helps; it argues for making each proximal subgoal itself specific and measurable, not for how small to cut it.
- **Optimal step granularity is an open research question**, not a solved one — no paper gives a validated "right" chunk size. The nearest empirical proxy (subtask-level cognitive-load measurement) shows load varies enough between subtasks that task-level averages hide it, which at least argues Riemann should let the decomposition depth be adjustable/adaptive rather than fixed.
- **The planning fallacy is real and large** (Buehler et al.; ~70% of students missed their own best-guess deadline in the classic study) and is best mitigated by an "outside view" (reference-class/base-rate estimate) rather than by more careful inside-view reasoning — a buildable, low-cost feature (compare this task's estimate to how long similar past tasks took).
- **ADHD time-reproduction deficits are robust**: the pattern is specifically an inability to *reproduce* an interval (translate a felt sense of time into action/output), not necessarily to *estimate* one when asked directly — a meaningful distinction for what kind of time-aid actually helps (external ticking/visual countdown > asking Sam to self-estimate).
- **Self-generated vs. given plans**: the IKEA effect (labor → ownership, but *only when the labor produces a completed, successful artifact*) and SDT autonomy research both point the same direction — plans people build themselves (even with AI help) should be stickier than handed-over plans — but I could not find a study that isolates *self-generated implementation intentions* from *provided* ones with a clean effect-size comparison; this is inferred from adjacent literature, not directly tested. Treat A32×plan as **C** (conditional), not a clean Y.
- **Co-planning/collaborative goal-setting has real support**: autonomy support and autonomous motivation form a reinforcing loop over time in longitudinal data, and being *part of* goal-setting (not just being given a goal) improves commitment — this is the strongest lever for making A04×plan and A03×plan resolve toward "the plan-*making* process matters, not just the plan."
- **Being told the next action is genuinely double-edged in ADHD**, and the literature doesn't fully resolve it: SDT-based accounts warn that directive external control undermines the autonomy need that ADHD motivation depends on especially heavily, while ADHD-coaching and executive-function literature treats environmental/point-of-performance scaffolding as *more* effective than skill-building alone. The resolution in the practitioner literature is procedural autonomy (Sam always retains the choice to accept/ignore/edit the suggested next action), not withholding the suggestion.
- **A major update since the baseline research: the single most-cited "self-imposed deadlines work" paper is retracted for data fabrication** (Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002, *Psychological Science*, retracted September 2026 after Data Colada found tampering; the 2026 replication by Hyndman & Bisin found deadline structure had a *negligible* effect on performance). This is a load-bearing correction: do not cite "self-imposed deadlines improve performance" as established. What survives is weaker and more general — commitment devices overall show a moderate pooled effect (Rogers, Milkman & Volpp meta-analysis, Hedges' g=0.68) but with a real "signals pre-existing motivation, doesn't create it" confound.
- **Checklists have some of the strongest applied evidence in this whole report** (WHO Surgical Safety Checklist: ~36% drop in complications, ~40%+ drop in deaths, N=7,688 across 8 sites of varying income level) — but the mechanism (forcing a brief explicit verification step under high-stakes, high-cognitive-load, team-coordination conditions) is a narrower fit to Riemann's use case than it first appears; the aviation/surgery evidence generalizes to "external checklist beats memory for routine multi-step procedures," not obviously to "checklists boost motivation to start a task."
- **Monitoring: exception-based/management-by-exception summaries are the design consensus** over raw compression — the literature (dashboard-design practice + alert-fatigue research) converges on "show what deviates from expected, not a shrunk version of everything," which directly favors a diff/exception transform over an LLM summary transform for FT-monitor-digest, resolving A30×monitor toward N (compression is *not* the right transformation for monitoring) and A15×monitor toward C (gist-level errors are tolerable only for the *unchanged* majority, not for the part flagged as an exception).

## Findings

### F22-01: Proximal subgoals outperform both distal goals and no goals on self-efficacy, mastery, and intrinsic interest
**Observation:** In Bandura & Schunk (1981), children with low prior interest/skill in arithmetic who pursued self-directed learning under a schedule of proximal subgoals (mastering blocks of material with a specific goal per session) showed the fastest progress, highest self-efficacy, and greatest subsequent intrinsic interest, compared to children given only a distal (end-of-program) goal, whose results were only marginally better than a no-goal control.
**Claim:** Breaking a large goal into a sequence of near-term, session-scale subgoals — rather than stating the end goal alone — causally improves both performance and motivation, especially for people who start with low confidence/interest in the domain.
**Mechanism:** Proximal subgoals give frequent, unambiguous self-efficacy information ("I did that") that a distal goal cannot provide until the very end; each completed subgoal is evidence the person can use to update their own competence belief, which is (per SDT and social-cognitive theory) itself motivating.
**Evidence:** grade S (a landmark, oft-replicated experimental study; effect direction has held up across the decades of subsequent proximal-goal literature, though I was not able to fetch the original PDF's full numeric tables — text-extraction failed on the binary PDF, so this is corroborated via multiple independent secondary descriptions of the same experiment, not the primary tables myself). Sources: https://www.scribd.com/document/14033215/Proximal-Goals-Bandura-Schunk-1981, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED222279.pdf, https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Cultivating-competence,-self-efficacy,-and-interest-Bandura-Schunk/b1e4d476c857333b9a0afdb1428eda27f6d26940
**Population:** children (grade-school), low prior interest/competence in the task domain — not adults with ADHD; transfer is inferred, not tested.
**Context:** structured self-directed learning experiment (not a real-world planning task).
**Assumptions:** A32 (self/assisted-generation vs. given), A04 (effortful processing improves outcome), A12 (general-population transfer), A13 (lab-to-real-world transfer), A11 (ADHD population).
**Implications:** FT-plan-breakdown should default to producing a *sequence* of near-term subgoals rather than a single end-state description, and each subgoal completion should be visibly marked (small, frequent "done" signals), not just tracked as % toward a distal total. (effect: supports)
**Contradictions:** none found directly; caveat noted above about population transfer (children, not ADHD adults).
**Confidence:** medium (strong original effect, but real transfer gap: children/math-specific, and mechanism plausibly generalizes but isn't re-tested in an adult ADHD/knowledge-work population).

### F22-02: Specific, difficult goals outperform vague "do your best" goals, robustly, across task types
**Observation:** Locke & Latham's goal-setting research program (culminating in large meta-analyses, e.g. the widely cited 1981/1990s syntheses) found specific and challenging goals produce higher performance than vague or "do your best" goals in the large majority of comparisons (reported as ~90%+ of studies in secondary summaries), with an average performance gain cited around 17% over baseline, conditional on feedback, goal commitment, and task-relevant ability being present.
**Claim:** Vagueness itself, independent of task decomposition, is a performance cost; a next action or subgoal that is specific and measurable will outperform an equally-sized but vague one.
**Mechanism:** Specific goals reduce ambiguity about what "good enough" performance looks like, direct attention and effort, and make self-monitoring/feedback against the goal possible; difficulty (within ability) raises effort mobilized.
**Evidence:** grade S (large, long-running meta-analytic literature; one of the most replicated findings in I/O psychology), grade M for the specific numeric claims quoted above since I relied on secondary summaries rather than pulling primary meta-analysis tables. Sources: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1901298, https://med.stanford.edu/content/dam/sm/s-spire/documents/PD.locke-and-latham-retrospective_Paper.pdf, https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/locke%20latham%20new%20dir%20gs%20curr%20dir%20psy%20sci%202006.pdf
**Population:** general adult/organizational populations, mostly workplace and lab tasks; not ADHD-specific.
**Context:** field and lab, mostly workplace/organizational psychology, decades of studies.
**Assumptions:** A11, A12, A13, A24 (deadline-bound).
**Implications:** FT-plan-breakdown and FT-next-action should phrase each step as a specific, checkable outcome ("send the email with X attached to Y") rather than a vague category ("work on the report"). (effect: supports, parameterises)
**Contradictions:** none.
**Confidence:** medium-high.

### F22-03: No validated "optimal" step granularity exists; subtask-level load varies enough that a fixed grain size is likely wrong
**Observation:** Cognitive-load research that measures at the subtask level (rather than whole-task averages) finds meaningfully different load per subtask, and a recent review of task-decomposition-for-reasoning explicitly names "what is the optimal granularity of a sub-task" as an open, unsolved research question, calling current practice "arbitrary, heuristic-driven."
**Claim:** There is no evidence-backed universal "right" size for a decomposed step; a fixed decomposition depth (e.g. "always break into steps of ~15 minutes") is an unjustified guess, and the right choice is likely task- and person-state dependent.
**Mechanism:** n/a — this is an absence-of-answer finding, not a mechanism.
**Evidence:** grade W (an open-question flag in adjacent literature, not a study that determined an answer) — but see F22-01 for a task-domain-specific case (session-scale subgoals) that did work in one controlled study. Sources: https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8374157/
**Population:** mixed (programming-education subgoal-labeling study is closest to real; cognitive-load-granularity review is general).
**Context:** HCI/education research, cognitive task analysis.
**Assumptions:** A17 (fluctuating capacity — argues granularity should flex with it), A08 (novice status).
**Implications:** FT-plan-breakdown should expose an adjustable granularity control (or infer target grain from current capacity signal) rather than commit to one fixed step size; treat any specific "N-minute chunks" heuristic as a design choice to A/B, not a research-backed constant. (effect: parameterises)
**Contradictions:** none; this finding is explicitly a caveat against overconfidence in any fixed-grain design.
**Confidence:** medium.

### F22-04: The planning fallacy is a large, robust effect, mitigated by outside-view/reference-class estimation more than by more careful inside-view reasoning
**Observation:** Buehler, Griffin & Ross's classic studies found students' actual completion times for projects (e.g. senior theses) substantially exceeded their own best-guess predictions — even worst-case predictions were often optimistic — with roughly 30% or fewer participants finishing within their predicted time in the original honors-thesis study. Kahneman & Tversky's "inside view vs. outside view" framing, and Lovallo & Kahneman's 2003 extension, identify using a reference class of similar past tasks/projects (rather than reasoning from the specifics of this task alone) as the most reliable correction.
**Claim:** People systematically underestimate task duration by focusing on the specific plan for the current task rather than on how long similar tasks have actually taken in the past; the fix that has evidence behind it is base-rate/reference-class comparison, not "try harder to imagine obstacles."
**Mechanism:** Inside-view planning constructs a best-case causal scenario and anchors on it; unpacking/imagining specific obstacles helps only a little; comparing to actual outcomes of similar past tasks (which encodes obstacles that weren't imagined) corrects the anchor.
**Evidence:** grade S for the existence and size of the effect (a large, decades-deep, frequently replicated literature); grade M for "outside view is the best fix" specifically (well-argued and widely cited, but I did not verify a dedicated RCT isolating outside-view-training against a control with a clean effect size — see gap below on Halkjelsvik & Jørgensen, whose meta-analysis I could not get past search-snippet access to verify quantitatively). Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy, https://spsp.org/news-center/character-context-blog/planning-fallacy-inside-view, https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/planning-fallacy
**Population:** general adult (students in the classic studies); not ADHD-specific, though ADHD time-blindness (F22-05) plausibly compounds it.
**Context:** field (real deadlines, real projects) and lab.
**Assumptions:** A24 (deadline-bound), A11, A12, A16 (time pressure).
**Implications:** FT-plan-breakdown's time estimates (if Riemann offers them at all) should be framed as "tasks like this typically took Sam ~X" pulled from his own logged history, not as a fresh inside-view guess for each new task — this is a natural fit for FT-event-log feeding back into planning. (effect: supports, parameterises)
**Contradictions:** none.
**Confidence:** medium (well-established phenomenon; the specific reference-class-in-Riemann mechanism is inferred/untested).

### F22-05: ADHD time-reproduction (not time-estimation) is the specifically impaired process
**Observation:** Barkley-tradition time-perception research (and later meta-analyses) finds ADHD-related deficits concentrated in time *reproduction* (translating a felt interval into a matched motor output, e.g. holding a button for the same duration just experienced) more than time *estimation/perception* per se; deficits appear across the lifespan, correlate with functional impairment, and partially normalize on stimulant medication.
**Claim:** The core ADHD time-blindness problem is not "can't judge how long things take" in the abstract so much as "can't use an internal sense of elapsed time to regulate ongoing action" — which argues for external, continuously-visible time cues during a task (a running countdown/progress indicator) rather than upfront estimation exercises.
**Mechanism:** Barkley's model ties this to working-memory/behavioral-inhibition deficits: tracking elapsed time requires holding a temporal representation active against competing input, which is exactly the resource ADHD executive dysfunction degrades.
**Evidence:** grade M-S (multiple converging studies and later meta-analyses across child/adolescent/adult samples; I relied on search-result syntheses of the meta-analytic literature rather than opening a single primary meta-analysis PDF directly, so treat the precise pooled effect size as unverified even though the directional finding is well corroborated). Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11815878/, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2017.00122/full, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1012233310098
**Population:** adults, adolescents, and children with ADHD (multiple samples across the cited studies) — directly on-population for Sam.
**Context:** lab time-reproduction/discrimination tasks.
**Assumptions:** A11, A24, A16.
**Implications:** during execution, prefer a continuously visible elapsed/remaining-time indicator (a live countdown/progress bar) over asking Sam to predict duration upfront; this pairs naturally with FT-commitment-capture (state a time-box) plus a passive visual timer, not a one-shot estimate. (effect: supports, parameterises)
**Contradictions:** none found; note this nuance (reproduction ≠ estimation impairment) somewhat qualifies F22-04's "ask for a reference-class estimate" implication — the estimate itself may be less reliably *used* mid-task without an external cue, even if the estimate itself (asked in a calm moment) is reasonably accurate.
**Confidence:** medium-high for the population fit (this is Sam's population directly), medium for precise effect sizes.

### F22-06: IKEA effect — self-made artifacts are valued more, but only if the labor produces a successfully completed thing
**Observation:** Norton, Mochon & Ariely (2012) found that people who assembled their own IKEA furniture, folded their own origami, or built their own Lego sets valued their (often amateurish) creations comparably to experts' work and expected others to agree — but this effect *disappeared* when the built object was destroyed or left incomplete.
**Claim:** Self-generated artifacts (a plan Sam builds, even imperfectly, with AI assistance) are likely to be valued/owned more than an equally good artifact handed to him ready-made — but only if the generation process actually reaches a completed state; an abandoned or interrupted co-planning session may confer none of this benefit.
**Mechanism:** Effort invested in successful completion is read by the person as a signal of their own competence and is incorporated into self-concept/liking for the object, a form of effort-justification/ownership.
**Evidence:** grade S (well-replicated consumer-psychology finding across 4 studies with different construction tasks), though it is about *valuation of an object*, not directly about *follow-through on a plan* — that extension is my inference, not tested in the original paper. Sources: https://www.tumcso.com/design-your-life-and-career-ss21/andere-berzeugen-persuasion-influence/norton-michael-i-daniel-mochon-and-dan-ariely-the-ikea-effect-when-labor-leads-to-love, https://scispace.com/papers/the-ikea-effect-when-labor-leads-to-love-27lfzjguo5, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IKEA_effect
**Population:** general adult consumers (lab/field consumer-behavior studies), not ADHD-specific.
**Context:** consumer psychology lab experiments.
**Assumptions:** A32, A03, A04, A12, A13.
**Implications:** FT-plan-breakdown's co-planning flow should be designed so Sam always finishes constructing his plan (even a rough one) rather than being handed a finished plan to merely approve/edit — but the completion condition is important: a half-finished co-planning session may be worse than either a clean handoff or a full self-build. (effect: supports, parameterises)
**Contradictions:** none directly, but see F22-07 below, which complicates "always make Sam do the work" with the execute-stage autonomy question.
**Confidence:** low-medium (real effect, but extended by inference from object-valuation to plan-adherence, which is a genuine transfer gap — flagged, not tested).

### F22-07: Directive "next action" scaffolding is a genuine double bind for ADHD autonomy vs. initiation — resolved procedurally in practice, not empirically in research
**Observation:** Two literatures point in different directions and neither fully resolves the conflict. (a) SDT-informed ADHD commentary argues that the interventions most commonly used to help ADHD (tight schedules, close supervision, step-by-step instruction) are precisely the ones that thwart the autonomy need, and that ADHD motivation depends especially heavily on autonomy support (competence support alone doesn't rescue intrinsic motivation if autonomy is thwarted). (b) Separately, ADHD executive-function/coaching literature treats environment-level, point-of-performance scaffolding (cues delivered exactly when/where the action should happen) as more effective for actually getting the action started than teaching general planning/organizational skills — the practical framing being "borrowed structure," not "control."
**Claim:** Being told the next action can help initiation (reduces the executive-function cost of deciding what to do) but risks undermining autonomy and motivation if delivered as directive/controlling rather than as an offered, easily-declinable suggestion; the two literatures aren't in direct empirical conflict because they're measuring different things (initiation vs. long-run intrinsic motivation) and no study I found tests them against each other head-to-head with Riemann's specific "prompt with an accept/ignore next action" design.
**Mechanism:** SDT distinguishes autonomy-supportive language/framing (informational, choice-preserving) from controlling language (directive, pressuring) as having opposite motivational effects even when the *content* of what's suggested is identical — the delivery, not the existence of a suggestion, is theorized to be what matters.
**Evidence:** grade W-M — this is a synthesis across a review paper (Morsink et al. 2022, SDT applied to ADHD motivation, published in Journal of Attention Disorders — I was blocked with a 403 on the full text and am relying on the abstract/search-snippet level, flagged) and several practitioner/coaching sources rather than a single controlled study that manipulates directiveness and measures both initiation and motivation. Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10870547211050948 (403, abstract-level only), https://www.addrc.org/when-intention-outpaces-action-the-adhd-action-gap/, https://chadd.org/attention-article/self-driven-kids-with-adhd/
**Population:** adults and children with ADHD (mixed across sources).
**Context:** mixed — clinical/coaching practice and theoretical review, not a single RCT.
**Assumptions:** A18 (steer navigation self vs. directed), A11.
**Implications:** FT-next-action should always be framed as an offered suggestion with a visible, low-friction way to ignore or replace it (never an auto-advancing directive), and Riemann should track whether Sam tends to accept/decline/edit suggestions as its own adaptivity signal rather than assuming one static answer to "does he want to be told." (effect: parameterises)
**Contradictions:** the two source literatures pull in different directions on emphasis even though not strictly contradictory — noted above.
**Confidence:** low (synthesis of a blocked-abstract source plus practitioner material; treat as a design hypothesis to validate with Sam's own usage data, not a settled finding).

**Resolves: A18×execute → C because — the literature doesn't support a clean Y or N. Being told the next action helps initiation (reduces the "what do I do" executive cost) but only when the suggestion is easily overridable; framed as autonomy-supportive information rather than a directive, it should support rather than undermine motivation. Riemann should implement FT-next-action as always-declinable and log accept/decline as an adaptivity signal.**

### F22-08: Collaborative/co-planning process improves commitment and is a reinforcing loop with autonomy support, independent of plan quality
**Observation:** Longitudinal research on autonomy support and personal goals found a dynamic, reciprocal relationship — perceived autonomy support at one time-point predicted more autonomous motivation at the next, which in turn predicted more autonomy support, producing an "upward spiral" associated with more positive affect and better goal progress over an academic year. Separately, practitioner/clinical collaborative-goal-setting work (e.g. family-driven planning in autism support services) reports substantially higher goal-attainment rates when the person being served participates in setting the goal.
**Claim:** The *process* of co-constructing a plan (being consulted, having input reflected in the final plan) drives commitment and follow-through gains largely independent of whether the resulting plan is objectively "better" than one an expert would hand over — this is a process effect, not a content effect.
**Mechanism:** Perceived choice/voice satisfies the SDT autonomy need directly; a plan that visibly incorporates the person's own input and language is easier to see as "mine" (echoing the IKEA-effect mechanism in F22-06) which increases follow-through motivation.
**Evidence:** grade M (longitudinal, real-world, reciprocal-effects design for the autonomy-support finding; the goal-attainment percentage for autism-support co-planning is a single practice-report figure, not a controlled comparison, so it's a weaker corroborating data point). Sources: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/344167904_Collaborative_autonomy_The_dynamic_relations_between_personal_goal_autonomy_and_perceived_autonomy_support_in_emerging_adulthood_results_in_positive_affect_and_goal_progress, https://www.advancedautism.com/post/the-role-of-collaborative-goal-setting-in-autism-support
**Population:** emerging adults (autonomy-support study — general population, not ADHD-specific); autism-support population (goal-setting practice report — different neurodivergent population, imperfect transfer to ADHD).
**Context:** longitudinal field study; clinical/service practice report.
**Assumptions:** A32, A18, A04, A12, A13.
**Implications:** FT-plan-breakdown's co-planning UI should visibly reflect Sam's own inputs/edits back into the generated plan (his own phrasing surfaced, not silently normalized away) so the ownership signal is real, not cosmetic. (effect: supports)
**Contradictions:** none.
**Confidence:** medium (reasonable population fit for the autonomy-support study; weaker for the autism-support figure).

**Resolves: A32×plan → C because — self-generation isn't cleanly proven superior to a given plan in a head-to-head RCT I could find, but every adjacent mechanism (IKEA effect, SDT autonomy-support reciprocal loop, collaborative goal-setting commitment data) points the same direction and none contradicts it. Treat "Sam generating the plan (with AI help) beats being handed one" as a well-supported design hypothesis, not settled fact — condition it on the co-planning process actually completing (F22-06's completion caveat).**

**Resolves: A04×plan → C because — effortful processing (Sam doing the co-planning work himself) plausibly improves plan commitment/follow-through via ownership and autonomy mechanisms (F22-06, F22-08), but this is inferred from adjacent literature (object valuation, goal-setting reciprocity) rather than a study that measures plan follow-through directly as a function of who generated the plan.**

**Resolves: A03×plan → C because — no direct evidence was found on whether holding a plan in Sam's own memory (vs. externally stored/retrievable) matters for plan objectives specifically; the co-planning/ownership literature (F22-06, F22-08) supports the *generation* process mattering, but says nothing about whether the plan then needs to live in memory vs. be externally referenceable — these are separable claims and A03 stays a research gap even after this pass. Leaving at C is the safe default: Riemann should let Sam offload the plan externally (FT-offload-store) without assuming that hurts commitment, since ownership appears to come from the generation process, not from where the artifact subsequently lives.**

### F22-09: The most-cited "self-imposed deadlines work" study is retracted for data fabrication; the 2026 replication found no effect of deadline structure
**Observation:** Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002, *Psychological Science*) — for two decades the standard citation for "self-imposed deadlines improve task performance, though less than externally-imposed evenly-spaced deadlines" — was retracted in September 2026 after the Data Colada team found data tampering (duplicated values, implausible lack of correlation among variables that should correlate, and evidence of a falsified analysis submitted in response to peer reviewers). A pre-retraction replication attempt (Hyndman & Bisin, *Psychological Science* 2026) found that varying deadline structure had a **negligible effect** on the three original performance metrics and did not corroborate the original study.
**Claim:** There is currently no credible experimental evidence that self-imposed deadlines (or evenly-spaced externally-imposed deadlines) reliably improve task performance relative to a single end deadline; the specific, frequently-cited numeric claims from this line of work should not be used to justify FT-commitment-capture's design.
**Mechanism:** n/a (retraction — the original "mechanism" claims are unsupported).
**Evidence:** grade MYTH for the original 2002 claims specifically (formally retracted for fabrication); the replication itself is grade M (single replication, real-world field-adjacent classroom setting, but only one study). Sources: https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/, https://datacolada.org/138, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09567976261488042 (retraction notice), https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976261460772 (Hyndman & Bisin replication), https://danariely.com/dan-ariely-statement-on-2002-procrastination-study/
**Population:** originally MIT executive-education students and undergraduates; replication used adults at a large US public university.
**Context:** field-adjacent classroom experiments (original and replication).
**Assumptions:** A24, A11, A12, A13.
**Implications:** FT-commitment-capture should not lean on "spacing your own deadlines evenly" as an evidence-backed mechanism; if Riemann offers interim/self-set deadlines, frame it as a low-cost structuring aid (worth trying, cheap, consistent with proximal-subgoal evidence in F22-01) rather than as a scientifically validated performance booster. (effect: opposes — corrects a myth that may already be baked into folk design wisdom)
**Contradictions:** directly opposes/retracts the study most design literature still cites uncritically; flag this prominently since other Riemann reports or future workers may cite the original 2002 paper without knowing about the September 2026 retraction.
**Confidence:** high (the retraction and replication are both primary, dated, and directly verified).

### F22-10: Commitment devices show a real but modest pooled effect, confounded with self-selection
**Observation:** Rogers, Milkman & Volpp's review/meta-analysis of commitment-device interventions (self-imposed costly restrictions on future choices) reports a meta-analytic pooled effect around Hedges' g=0.68 across a set of RCTs, but other work in the same space (e.g. middle-school commitment-device studies) finds take-up doesn't necessarily change behavior, and commentary in the field explicitly raises that commitment-device adoption may signal pre-existing motivation rather than create new motivation.
**Claim:** Commitment devices (voluntarily pre-committing to costs/restrictions for missing a self-set goal) have a moderate real effect on average, but the effect is heterogeneous and partly explained by who opts in, not the device itself — a device made available to everyone (like a Riemann feature) may show a smaller true causal effect than the pooled meta-analytic number suggests, because in Riemann it wouldn't be self-selected the same way lab/field-experiment participants self-select.
**Mechanism:** Precommitment raises the cost of not following through (loss aversion on the forfeited stake), but only works if the person both wants the underlying goal and trusts the enforcement.
**Evidence:** grade M (real meta-analysis with RCTs, but the self-selection confound is explicitly named in the literature itself and not fully resolved). Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24777472/, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00206/full, https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/commitment-devices-using-initiatives-change-behavior
**Population:** general adult (health-behavior-focused RCTs largely); one study in middle-school students.
**Context:** field RCTs, mostly health behavior (smoking cessation, exercise, weight loss) rather than knowledge-work/planning tasks.
**Assumptions:** A24, A11, A12, A13.
**Implications:** FT-commitment-capture is worth building (real, if modest and selection-confounded, effect) but should be framed as an optional device Sam opts into for tasks he already cares about completing, not pushed as a universal default — pushing it universally is exactly the condition (non-self-selected) where the evidence is weakest. (effect: supports, parameterises)
**Contradictions:** tension between the meta-analytic pooled effect and the "may just signal motivation" critique — both are represented in the same literature, noted above.
**Confidence:** medium.

### F22-11: The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist produced large, real reductions in complications and mortality, but the mechanism is narrower than "checklists work" in general
**Observation:** A prospective study across 8 hospitals (high- and low-income sites) found complication rates fell ~36% and death rates fell more than 40% after introducing a structured 19-item surgical checklist administered at three fixed points during surgery (N=7,688 total patients, ~3,733 pre- and ~3,955 post-checklist).
**Claim:** A short, structured, externally-imposed checklist administered at fixed procedural checkpoints reliably catches errors of omission in high-stakes, multi-person, routine-but-critical procedures — this is strong evidence for checklists as an error-prevention tool under those specific conditions (team coordination, routine multi-step procedure, high stakes, memory/attention failure is the dominant risk).
**Mechanism:** Forces a brief, explicit, verbalized verification step that interrupts autopilot/skipped-step behavior under cognitive load, and (crucially, per Gawande's broader argument) also functions as team communication, not just individual memory aid.
**Evidence:** grade S (large prospective multi-site study, published NEJM, widely replicated in follow-up implementation studies in different surgical contexts). Sources: https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsa0810119, https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/01/090114172304.htm, https://hsph.harvard.edu/news/fall08checklist/
**Population:** surgical patients/teams, not knowledge workers or ADHD individuals — a significant context/population gap from Riemann's use case.
**Context:** field, real hospitals, high-stakes team procedures.
**Assumptions:** A11 (fails — this evidence is not from the ADHD population and the task structure is very different), A06 (content is not expository prose — different domain entirely), A24.
**Implications:** the checklist evidence supports a narrow feature: for multi-step *procedures Sam has done before and might skip a step in under load* (not novel planning), a lightweight verification checklist is justified. It does not support checklists as a general motivation/initiation tool for starting new work — that's a different problem (see F22-01, F22-04) with different mechanisms. (effect: supports, parameterises — narrowly)
**Contradictions:** none directly, but the finding is easy to over-generalize; flagging the narrow scope is itself the main contribution here.
**Confidence:** medium (strong study, but explicit population/task-type mismatch to Riemann's actual use case reduces applied confidence).

### F22-12: To-do lists fail primarily from vagueness and unbounded length, not from the list format itself
**Observation:** Bellotti et al. (CHI 2004) found, contrary to "people are bad at prioritizing" folk wisdom, that people actually have well-developed personal strategies for managing task priority — task management failures in their field studies were more about mismatched tools/reminding mechanisms than an inherent inability to prioritize. Separately, practitioner/applied-cognition sources converge on vague task phrasing and unbounded list length (commonly cited informally as a "beyond ~10-15 items, it overwhelms" pattern) as proximate causes of list abandonment, echoing Locke & Latham's specificity finding (F22-02) at the level of everyday task lists.
**Claim:** A to-do list's failure mode is usually poor task specification (vague items, no clear next physical action) and unmanaged length/visibility, not that lists as a representation are inherently ineffective — this argues for the "next action" framing (GTD-style: the next concrete physical/visible step) as the fix, not for abandoning list-like representations.
**Mechanism:** A vague item requires an extra covert planning step (deciding what "work on X" concretely means) before it's actionable; that decision cost is exactly the kind of executive-function tax ADHD task-initiation difficulty is sensitive to, so vague items disproportionately stall.
**Evidence:** grade M for the Bellotti et al. field study (a genuine, well-cited CHI field study of real task-management behavior), grade W for the "10-15 items" and "41% of items never done" figures circulating in practitioner sources (I could not trace these to a primary citation and they read as un-sourced folk statistics — flagged explicitly as unverified). Sources: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/985692.985785, https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/research/publications/2004/what-do-studies-task-management-towards-design-personal-task-list-manager
**Population:** knowledge workers (Bellotti et al.'s field study population) — a reasonably good match to Sam.
**Context:** field study of real task-management practice (email-centric, 2004 workplace).
**Assumptions:** A11, A12, A13, A23 (external outcome completion).
**Implications:** FT-next-action and FT-plan-breakdown should enforce "concrete next physical action" phrasing (matching F22-02's specificity finding) as the actual lever, and any list view Riemann shows should default to surfacing only the next 1-3 actionable items rather than the full backlog, to sidestep the (weakly evidenced but plausible) length-overwhelm pattern. (effect: supports, parameterises)
**Contradictions:** the specific numeric overwhelm-threshold claims are unverified folk statistics — flagged, not relied on for any specific number in the design.
**Confidence:** medium for the specificity mechanism (well-grounded via F22-02 and Bellotti), low for any specific numeric threshold.

### F22-13: Exception-based/management-by-exception summaries are the practitioner and research consensus for monitoring, over full compression
**Observation:** Dashboard-design practice literature (and a cited systematic review reporting information overload affects ~47% of dashboard users, attributed largely to excessive data density and lack of contextual filtering) converges on "exception-based design" — surfacing only what deviates from an expected/normal range — as the standard fix for monitoring-at-scale, distinct from summarizing/compressing everything. Separately, alert-fatigue research in clinical and security-operations settings identifies two failure modes of naive real-time alerting (cognitive overload from volume, and desensitization from repeated low-value alerts), and industry practice for those systems has converged on differentiated batching: true exceptions in real time, everything else in a scheduled digest.
**Claim:** For a monitoring objective, the right transform is diff/exception detection against an expected baseline (flag what changed or deviates), not compression/summarization of the full state — compressing everything (even well) still requires reviewing a shrunk version of things that didn't need attention, while exception-based design structurally removes the requirement to look at unchanged items at all.
**Mechanism:** Compression reduces token/reading cost per item but preserves the *count* of items requiring a look; exception-filtering reduces the count of items requiring a look, which is the actual attention-cost driver in monitoring (a task defined by its low expected information rate — most checks should find nothing new).
**Evidence:** grade M (converging practitioner literature and a cited systematic review for the dashboard-overload figure — I was not able to independently verify the primary systematic review this "75 studies / 46.7%" figure traces to beyond the secondary source that quoted it, so treat that specific number as grade W even though the qualitative pattern is corroborated across independent practitioner sources including clinical alert-fatigue research). Sources: https://www.prosense.com.au/post/creating-effective-dashboards-using-exception-based-design-and-trend-analysis, https://portma.com/resources/articles/management-by-exception-in-dashboard-reporting-s1-e013-01/, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9424650/, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11845892/
**Population:** business-intelligence/dashboard users and clinical-alert contexts — not ADHD-specific, not personal-life monitoring specifically; a real transfer gap.
**Context:** industry practice writeups + healthcare alert-fatigue systematic reviews (mixed rigor).
**Assumptions:** A11, A12, A13, A15 (errors at gist level tolerable), A30 (compression is the right transform).
**Implications:** FT-monitor-digest should be built as a change-detection/diff engine (compare current state to last-seen state or to an expected/normal pattern, and only surface deltas) rather than as a periodic LLM summary of everything being watched. Combine with F22-14's batching-cadence finding from report 14: real exceptions can interrupt per Riemann's existing interrupt-tier system; non-exceptional status should not appear at all rather than being compressed into a "nothing new" digest line that still costs a glance. (effect: supports, opposes-compression-default)
**Contradictions:** this finding sits in tension with A30's default framing in assumptions.json (compression assumed relevant background) — this finding argues compression is the wrong primary transform specifically for monitor, though compression may still matter as a secondary step *within* how an exception is explained once flagged.
**Confidence:** medium (mechanism is clean and consensus is real, but grounded more in adjacent domains — BI dashboards, clinical alerting — than in a study of personal-life monitoring for an ADHD adult).

**Resolves: A30×monitor → N because — the converged practitioner and research evidence (dashboard exception-design, clinical/security alert-fatigue literature) favors diff/exception detection over summarization/compression as the primary transform for a monitoring objective; compression reduces per-item cost but not the number of items reviewed, while exception-filtering reduces the count itself, which is what monitoring's "minimal attention" success criterion (per objectives.json) actually requires.**

**Resolves: A15×monitor → C because — gist-level errors are tolerable for the (large) majority of monitored items that are correctly classified as "no change/not an exception" and therefore never surfaced at all, but errors are specifically *not* tolerable for the item that gets flagged as an exception, since that's the one thing monitor is optimizing to get right — so tolerance depends on which side of the exception/non-exception boundary the error falls on, which is exactly the C (conditional) case.**

## Design implications for Riemann

- **FT-plan-breakdown**: default to a sequence of near-term, specific, checkable proximal subgoals (F22-01, F22-02), not a single distal goal restated. Make granularity adjustable/adaptive rather than fixed (F22-03) — likely tied to the existing capacity/fluctuation signal (A17). When Riemann estimates duration for a step, pull from Sam's own logged history of similar past tasks (reference-class/outside-view, F22-04) rather than generating a fresh inside-view guess, and don't present that estimate as something Sam needs to hold onto and self-regulate against without an external cue (F22-05) — pair it with a live, visible timer during execution rather than a static prediction.
- **Co-planning mechanic**: have Sam do the actual construction step (with AI assistance/scaffolding, not AI handing over a finished plan) and make sure the session reaches a genuinely completed state — an abandoned co-planning draft may be worse than either extreme (F22-06). Visibly reflect Sam's own wording/choices back into the rendered plan rather than silently normalizing them away (F22-08).
- **FT-next-action**: always present as an offered, one-tap-declinable suggestion, never an auto-advancing directive (F22-07); log accept/decline/edit as an adaptivity signal Riemann can learn from over time, since the "does directing help or hurt" question resolved to conditional (A18×execute → C), not a fixed answer.
- **FT-commitment-capture**: build it, but as an opt-in device Sam chooses for tasks he already wants to finish, not a default nudge pushed onto every task (F22-10) — and drop any design copy or internal reasoning that leans on "self-imposed deadlines are scientifically proven to work," since the primary source for that claim is retracted for fraud and the 2026 replication found no effect (F22-09). If Riemann suggests deadlines, frame them as a cheap-to-try structuring aid consistent with the (separately supported) proximal-subgoal literature, not as a validated procrastination cure.
- **Checklist-style verification**: reserve for a narrow, well-fitting case — repeatable multi-step procedures Sam has done before, where the risk is a memory-slip under load (e.g. a recurring submission/setup routine), not for novel planning or motivation-to-start problems (F22-11). Don't over-generalize "checklists work" from the surgical evidence to general task motivation.
- **List/next-action surfacing**: default the visible surface to the next 1-3 concrete, specific actions rather than the full plan/backlog (F22-12), consistent with progressive disclosure already established for the abstraction tree.
- **FT-monitor-digest**: architect as change-detection against an expected/prior state (diff/exception engine), not as an LLM compression of full status (F22-13). Items with no meaningful change should not appear at all, rather than being compressed into a "nothing new" line — the cost of monitoring should scale with the number of exceptions, not the number of things being watched. Route genuine exceptions through the existing interrupt-tier/batching system from report 14 rather than building a separate monitoring-specific interruption model.

## Signals Riemann could measure
- Accept / decline / edit rate on FT-next-action suggestions (directly tests the F22-07 autonomy-vs-initiation tension for Sam specifically, resolving A18×execute empirically over time rather than by literature inference alone).
- Completion rate and self-reported ownership/confidence for co-planned vs. handed-over plans (would let Riemann actually test F22-06/F22-08's inferred-transfer claims on real data instead of relying on adjacent-domain literature).
- Actual vs. estimated task duration, logged per task type, to build Sam's own reference-class base rates for F22-04's outside-view correction — and to check whether Riemann's estimates converge over time.
- Whether an offered self-set deadline/commitment is accepted, kept, or abandoned — cheap to log, and would let Riemann build genuine local evidence on commitment devices rather than leaning on the now-shaky general literature (F22-09, F22-10).
- For monitor: rate of flagged exceptions that Sam confirms were worth surfacing vs. false positives (directly measures whether the exception-detection threshold in F22-13 is tuned correctly) and rate of missed items Sam later discovers Riemann didn't flag (false negatives) — both needed to keep "minimal attention, nothing important missed" honest.
- Subgoal completion cadence (are proximal subgoals actually being hit near-on-time, per F22-01/F22-02) as an implicit granularity-tuning signal for FT-plan-breakdown.

## Contradictions and caveats
- The most important contradiction in this report is a *correction*, not a live disagreement: Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002) is retracted (F22-09), which invalidates a widely-cited prior claim about self-imposed deadlines. Any earlier Riemann research (or design intuition) that cited it should be revisited.
- F22-01 (proximal subgoals) is from a child population on a math-mastery task; F22-06 (IKEA effect) and F22-08 (co-planning) are from general-adult or different-neurodivergent populations. None of these three load-bearing findings for the plan objective come from adult-ADHD, real-world-planning samples — the whole "self-generated plans beat given plans" conclusion (A32×plan, A04×plan) is a synthesis across adjacent literatures, not a single study on-target for Sam. This is exactly why both resolve to C rather than Y.
- F22-07 (directive next-action vs. autonomy) is the weakest-evidenced finding in this report (grade W-M, partly built on a paper I could only access at abstract level after a 403). Treat the resulting A18×execute→C resolution as a hypothesis to validate with Riemann's own usage signals, not a settled answer.
- F22-11 (surgical checklists) is strong evidence but for a task type (routine, high-stakes, memory-slip risk) that's a narrow subset of what Riemann's plan/execute/monitor objectives cover — resist the temptation to generalize "checklists are proven" beyond that narrow case.
- F22-13's headline dashboard-overload statistic (47% of users, 75 studies) could not be traced past a secondary source restating it — the qualitative exception-over-compression conclusion is corroborated independently elsewhere (alert-fatigue literature), so I kept the finding at grade M with the specific number flagged as unverified (W), rather than discarding the finding.
- Time constraints meant I did not open primary full-text for: the Locke & Latham meta-analytic tables (F22-02, relied on secondary summaries), the Halkjelsvik & Jørgensen 2012 Psychological Bulletin meta-analysis on judgment-based time-prediction (search only reached citation-level, not extractable content), and the Morsink et al. 2022 SDT-ADHD review (403 blocked, abstract-level only). These are flagged individually above; none of the report's core conclusions rest solely on an unverified source.

## Gaps
- **No study directly compares self-generated vs. AI-assisted-co-generated vs. fully-given plans on real-world follow-through**, in any population, let alone ADHD adults. This is the single most valuable follow-up experiment for Riemann itself to run (via the signals listed above) rather than something to keep searching the literature for — it may simply not exist yet.
- **ADHD-specific research on task-decomposition granularity is thin.** Goblin.tools-style AI task breakdown tools have wide anecdotal adoption in the ADHD community but no controlled outcome study I could find (only qualitative/testimonial coverage) — a genuine evidence gap, not just a search-effort limit; worth a dedicated deeper pass if Riemann wants to cite adoption evidence rather than just plausibility.
- **Time-reproduction vs. time-estimation dissociation (F22-05) and its interface implications deserve a dedicated deep-dive**: what specific external time-cue designs (ambient countdown, periodic check-in ping, audio pacing) have been tested for ADHD populations specifically, beyond the general finding that reproduction (not estimation) is impaired. This connects directly to FT's execute-stage UI and wasn't in scope for this pass.
- **Post-retraction landscape for procrastination/deadline research**: given F22-09, it would be worth a targeted follow-up specifically hunting for *other* still-credible (non-Ariely) studies on deadline spacing and self-commitment, since this report's search surfaced the retraction but didn't exhaustively map what legitimate evidence remains standing in that specific sub-literature.
- **Monitoring digest research is almost entirely from BI/dashboard and clinical-alerting domains, not personal/life-admin monitoring for an individual.** A dedicated search for HCI research specifically on personal information monitoring (e.g. price trackers, deadline trackers, inbox monitoring tools) evaluated for attention cost would strengthen F22-13 considerably; report 14 already covers notification batching cadence, but the *content transform* (diff vs. summary) for personal monitoring specifically seems to be an underexplored HCI niche worth a dedicated wave-5 pass.
