# How Large Software Systems Actually Get Built, Run, and Kept Alive

Software that only a handful of people use can survive almost any amount of sloppiness. A script that one person runs once a week can be badly named, undocumented, and full of shortcuts, and none of that matters much because the person who wrote it still remembers how it works. Everything changes once a system has to serve thousands or millions of people, stay up continuously, and outlive the engineers who first built it. This document walks through what actually changes as software grows from a personal script into critical infrastructure: how teams organize the work, how they keep it from breaking, and what they do when it breaks anyway.

## Why small systems and large systems are different problems

It is tempting to think of a large system as just a small system multiplied: more users, more servers, more code. That framing misses the more important shift, which is that large systems accumulate people. A one-person project has a single, consistent mental model of how everything fits together, held in one head. A hundred-person project has a hundred partial, sometimes contradictory mental models, updated at different times, based on different parts of the system each person happened to touch. Most of the practices described in this document exist to manage that divergence, not to make the code itself faster or cleverer.

This has a counterintuitive consequence: a lot of what looks like bureaucratic overhead in a large engineering organization, code review, style guides, architecture documents, deprecation policies, is really a coordination mechanism, a way of keeping a hundred partial mental models close enough to each other that people can work on the same system without constantly breaking each other's assumptions. Teams that skip this coordination do not become faster; they become fast for a while and then spend disproportionately more time on confusion, duplicated work, and incidents caused by two people making incompatible assumptions about the same piece of code.

The other major difference is that large systems fail continuously, in small ways, as a matter of course. A server will crash. A network link will drop packets. A disk will fill up. None of these events are exceptional at scale, they are expected background noise, and systems that are not designed to tolerate this noise will be down constantly. Small systems can often get away with pretending failures do not happen, because they mostly do not, for that particular system, in that particular week. Large systems cannot make that assumption even for a single day.

## Version control as a shared memory, not just a backup

Most engineers first learn version control, typically Git, as a way to undo mistakes and keep backups. That is true but undersells what it does for a team. In a large project, the version history is the closest thing the team has to a shared memory: it records not just what the code looked like at any point, but why it changed, through commit messages, linked issues, and code review discussion. When something breaks six months after it was written, the person debugging it is very often not the person who wrote it, and the commit history plus the associated review conversation is frequently the only way to reconstruct the original reasoning.

This is why teams that care about long-term maintainability tend to be strict about commit hygiene: small, focused commits with messages that explain the reasoning, not just the mechanics, of a change. A commit message that says "fix bug" is nearly useless six months later. A commit message that explains what was actually going wrong, and why this particular fix was chosen over the alternatives, turns the history into documentation that updates itself as the system changes, instead of a separate design document that inevitably drifts out of sync with the code.

Branching strategy is the other half of this. Most large teams work with some variant of feature branches merged into a shared main line after review, precisely because it lets many people work on the same codebase concurrently without stepping on each other's half-finished work. The specific workflow, trunk-based development, Git flow, or something in between, matters less than the underlying discipline: changes are reviewed before they reach everyone else, and the main line is kept in a state that can be deployed at essentially any time.

## Why code review exists beyond catching bugs

Code review is often justified purely as a bug-catching mechanism, and it is true that a second pair of eyes catches mistakes the author was too close to the problem to see. But in large systems, code review does at least three other jobs that matter just as much. First, it spreads knowledge: a reviewer who reads a change learns something about a part of the system they might otherwise never have touched, which reduces the number of areas only one person understands. Second, it enforces consistency: without review, a hundred engineers will independently invent a hundred slightly different ways to do the same kind of thing, and that variation is itself a maintenance cost, because every new pattern is one more thing engineers have to learn to work anywhere in the codebase. Third, it creates a natural point to ask whether a change is a good idea at all, not just whether it is correctly implemented, catching cases where a technically correct change solves the wrong problem or introduces complexity the team will regret later.

The cost of code review is real: it slows down how quickly a single change can ship, and a badly run review process, one that nitpicks trivial style issues while missing substantive problems, can become a source of friction rather than quality. The teams that get the most value from review tend to separate concerns explicitly, using automated tools for anything mechanical, formatting, linting, straightforward style rules, so that human reviewers can spend their limited attention on the things a machine cannot judge: is this the right approach, does it handle the edge cases that matter, will the next person understand it.

## Testing as a way of encoding assumptions

A test suite is often described as a safety net that catches regressions, and it is that, but a more useful way to think about it is as a written record of assumptions. Every test encodes a claim about how the system is supposed to behave in a specific situation. When that claim is written down as a test rather than left implicit in someone's head, it survives that person leaving the team, and it gets checked automatically every time the code changes, rather than only when someone happens to remember to check it manually.

This reframing explains why test coverage percentage is a weak proxy for test quality. A test suite can have high coverage while testing almost nothing that matters, if the tests exercise code paths without asserting anything meaningful about the results. Conversely, a smaller number of well-chosen tests, especially tests that cover the boundary conditions and failure modes that are easy to get wrong, can catch far more real bugs than a much larger suite of tests that mostly restate the obvious. The most valuable tests tend to be the ones written in response to an actual bug: when something breaks in production, a good practice is to write a test that would have caught it before fixing the underlying issue, which both confirms the fix works and prevents the same class of bug from recurring silently.

Different kinds of tests catch different kinds of problems, which is why mature systems usually have several layers rather than relying on just one. Unit tests check small, isolated pieces of logic quickly and cheaply, and are good at catching straightforward correctness bugs close to where they are introduced. Integration tests check that multiple pieces work together correctly, catching the class of bug where each piece is individually correct but the assumptions they make about each other do not actually match. End-to-end tests exercise the system the way a real user or client would, catching problems that only show up when everything is assembled, at the cost of being slower and more brittle than the layers below them. No single layer replaces the others; each one is cheap to run and good at catching bugs the other layers systematically miss.

## Deployment: getting code from a laptop to production safely

Writing correct code is only part of the problem; getting it safely in front of real users is its own discipline. The naive approach, manually copying files to a server and restarting it, does not scale past a handful of servers and is dangerous even at small scale, because there is no consistent record of what is actually running where, and a mistake made by hand during a deploy is a mistake made in production with no easy way to know it happened.

Modern deployment pipelines automate this end to end: a change that passes review and automated tests triggers a build, which produces a versioned, reproducible artifact, which is then rolled out to production through a controlled process rather than a manual one. The specific mechanics vary, containers, virtual machine images, serverless functions, but the underlying goal is the same in every case: make deploying a known-good, tested artifact the easy path, and make any deviation from that path, a manual change, an untested shortcut, visibly abnormal rather than routine.

Rolling deployments and canary releases exist because deploying a change to every server simultaneously is a way of finding out about a bug from every user at once. A rolling deployment updates a small fraction of servers first, watches for problems, and only proceeds to the rest if nothing looks wrong. A canary release goes further, routing a small fraction of real traffic to the new version specifically so its behavior can be compared against the old version before a full rollout. Both techniques trade a slightly slower rollout for a dramatically smaller blast radius if something is wrong, which is a trade almost every large system decides is worth making, because the cost of a full outage is far higher than the cost of a slightly slower release.

Feature flags add another layer of control, separating the act of deploying code from the act of turning a feature on for users. A change can be deployed to production, fully tested in that environment, and still be invisible to real users until a flag is flipped, which means a bad deploy and a bad feature are two different, independently recoverable problems rather than one tangled one. This separation is especially valuable for large or risky changes, where the team wants the code running in production for confidence before committing to the user-facing behavior it enables.

## Observability: knowing what is actually happening

A system that is running does not mean a system that is working correctly, and at scale it is not possible to know the difference by watching it directly, the way you might notice your own laptop acting strangely. Observability is the set of practices and tooling that let engineers answer, after the fact, what a system was actually doing at any given moment, without having to have anticipated the exact question in advance.

The three pillars usually named are logs, metrics, and traces, and each answers a different kind of question. Logs are detailed, timestamped records of discrete events, useful for reconstructing exactly what happened in one specific request or process, but too voluminous to scan by eye across a whole fleet. Metrics are aggregated numerical measurements over time, request counts, error rates, latency percentiles, useful for seeing trends and anomalies at a glance but stripped of the detail needed to explain any single event. Traces follow a single request as it moves through multiple services, which is essential in a system built from many small services calling each other, because a slow response might be caused by any one of a dozen services in the chain, and without tracing there is no efficient way to find out which one.

None of these tools are useful if nobody looks at them until something is already badly broken. The more valuable practice is proactive: defining what normal looks like for a system's key metrics, and alerting automatically when reality drifts away from that baseline, ideally before it becomes visible to users at all. This requires real judgment, because an alert that fires too often for things that turn out to be fine trains engineers to ignore it, which defeats the purpose, while an alert threshold set too loosely misses real problems until they are severe. Getting this balance right is an ongoing, iterative process, not something that gets configured once and left alone.

## Incidents: what happens when it breaks anyway

Despite all of the above, large systems still break, regularly, because they are built by fallible people, run on fallible hardware, and depend on other systems outside their control that can also break. The question worth asking is not how to prevent every incident, which is not achievable, but how to make incidents shorter, less damaging, and less likely to repeat.

The first phase of any serious incident is detection and triage: noticing something is wrong, usually through an automated alert rather than a person happening to notice, and quickly assessing how bad it is. This assessment drives everything that follows: a minor degradation affecting a small fraction of users is handled very differently from a full outage affecting everyone, in terms of how many people get pulled in, how loudly it gets communicated, and how much risk the team is willing to accept in a fast fix.

The second phase is mitigation, which is deliberately distinct from a full fix. In the middle of an incident, the priority is almost always to stop the bleeding as fast as possible, rolling back a bad deploy, failing over to a backup system, disabling a broken feature flag, rather than to understand the root cause in full. Root-causing under time pressure, with users actively affected, tends to produce worse decisions than doing it calmly afterward. A rollback that restores service in two minutes and buys time to investigate properly is almost always better than an attempt at a precise fix that takes twenty minutes and might not even be correct.

The third phase, after service is restored, is the postmortem: a structured writeup of what happened, why, and what will change to make it less likely or less damaging next time. The most effective postmortems are explicitly blameless, focusing on what in the system or the process allowed the incident to happen, rather than on which individual made a mistake. This is not just a cultural nicety; it has a practical effect. In a culture where incidents are met with blame, people become reluctant to report problems early or admit uncertainty, which means small issues fester until they become large ones, and root causes get hidden rather than surfaced. In a blameless culture, someone who notices they might have caused a problem is far more likely to say so immediately, which is almost always the single biggest factor in how much damage an incident ends up causing.

## Technical debt and the cost of shortcuts

Every codebase accumulates technical debt: shortcuts taken under time pressure, code written for requirements that later changed, patterns that made sense at a smaller scale and stopped making sense at a larger one. The term is borrowed from financial debt deliberately, because the analogy is apt in both directions. Taking on debt, cutting a corner to ship faster, is sometimes the right decision, the same way borrowing money to seize a real opportunity can be the right decision. The problem is not debt itself but debt that is taken on without anyone tracking it, or debt that is never paid down, which compounds the way financial debt does: each new feature built on top of a shortcut has to work around it, making the eventual fix more expensive the longer it is deferred.

The hard part of managing technical debt is that paying it down rarely produces a visible feature anyone outside engineering will notice, which makes it perpetually easy to deprioritize in favor of visible new work. Teams that manage this well tend to make technical debt visible in the same systems they use to track other work, rather than leaving it as informal tribal knowledge, and they build in some baseline expectation, a fixed fraction of time, a policy of fixing what you touch, so that debt paydown is not entirely dependent on someone finding the political will to argue for it project by project.

Not all debt is equally worth paying down, though, and treating it that way is its own mistake. Debt in a part of the system that changes constantly and is read by many engineers compounds fast and is worth prioritizing. Debt in a part of the system that is stable, rarely touched, and understood by nobody who still works there might genuinely be cheaper to leave alone indefinitely, since the cost of fixing it well might exceed any realistic benefit. Recognizing this distinction, rather than treating all debt as equally urgent or equally ignorable, is itself a skill that experienced engineers develop over time.

## Documentation as an artifact that decays

Documentation has an unusual property compared to code: code that is wrong usually fails loudly, through a test failure, a crash, an obviously wrong result, while documentation that is wrong usually fails silently, sitting there looking authoritative while quietly describing a system that no longer exists. This asymmetry means documentation requires deliberate maintenance in a way that code partially enforces on itself.

The most durable documentation tends to be the kind that is close to the thing it describes and hard to let drift: comments explaining why a specific piece of code does something non-obvious, architecture diagrams checked into the same repository as the code they describe, README files that are exercised every time a new engineer sets up the project locally. Documentation that lives far from the code it describes, a wiki page written once and never revisited, a design doc from a project's kickoff, tends to decay faster, precisely because nothing forces anyone to notice when it goes stale.

This does not mean high-level documentation is worthless, only that it needs a different kind of ownership than code does. Code has an implicit maintenance mechanism: if it is wrong, something breaks, and someone eventually has to fix it. Documentation needs an explicit one: a periodic review, an owner who is expected to keep it current, or at minimum a visible timestamp and a culture where stale docs get flagged and either updated or deleted rather than left to mislead the next person who finds them.

## Why none of this is really about the code

Reading through all of the practices above, version control discipline, code review, layered testing, careful deployment, observability, incident response, debt management, documentation, it is worth noticing what they have in common: almost none of them are really about writing better algorithms or faster code. They are about managing information across time and across people: making sure the reasoning behind a decision outlives the meeting where it was made, making sure a problem discovered by one engineer becomes knowledge the whole team has, making sure a system's actual behavior can be understood after the fact rather than only guessed at.

This is the real difference between a small system and a large one. A small system's hardest problems are usually technical: how do you make this algorithm fast enough, how do you model this data correctly. A large system's hardest problems are usually about coordination and memory: how do you keep hundreds of people, working over years, with constant turnover, building something coherent instead of something that only makes sense in fragments. The engineering practices that seem like overhead from the outside are, from the inside, the accumulated answer to that second, harder problem.

## Scaling data storage as growth outpaces a single machine

For a long time, the simplest possible answer to "where does the data live" is a single database on a single machine, and for a surprising number of systems this remains entirely sufficient for their whole lifetime. The trouble starts when either the data no longer fits on one machine's disks, or the rate of reads and writes exceeds what one machine can serve, or both. At that point a team has to choose among a handful of strategies, each with real trade-offs, rather than a single obviously correct answer.

Read replicas are usually the first step: copies of the data kept in sync with the primary database, which can serve read queries so the primary is freed up to handle writes. This works well when a system is read-heavy, which most systems are, but it introduces replication lag, a small delay between a write landing on the primary and that write becoming visible on a replica. Applications that are not built with this lag in mind can produce confusing bugs, such as a user submitting a change and then not seeing it reflected immediately, because their next read happened to land on a replica that had not caught up yet.

Sharding goes further, splitting the data itself across multiple machines, typically by some key, a user ID, a geographic region, so that no single machine has to hold or serve the whole dataset. Sharding solves the capacity problem directly but reintroduces complexity that a single database used to hide for free: a query that needs data from two different shards can no longer be answered with one simple lookup, and operations that used to be atomic, guaranteed to happen entirely or not at all, become much harder to guarantee across shard boundaries. Teams that shard prematurely, before they actually need the capacity, often pay this complexity cost for years without ever needing the scale that justified it.

Caching sits alongside both of these as a third lever, storing the results of expensive or frequently repeated reads in a fast, temporary store so the underlying database does not have to redo the same work over and over. Caching is often the highest-leverage change available, because a well-chosen cache can eliminate the majority of load on a database without touching the database itself. Its cost is a new and famously tricky problem: cache invalidation, making sure that when the underlying data changes, the cached copy is updated or removed rather than silently serving stale data indefinitely. Getting invalidation subtly wrong is one of the most common sources of hard-to-reproduce bugs in large systems, because the system appears to work correctly most of the time and only shows wrong data under specific, hard-to-predict timing.

## On-call and the human cost of always-on systems

A system that needs to stay up continuously needs people who can respond when it does not, and the practice of on-call, a rotating responsibility where someone is designated to be reachable and ready to act if something breaks, is how most teams solve this. Getting on-call right is as much a human problem as a technical one, because a poorly run on-call rotation is one of the most reliable ways to burn out otherwise excellent engineers.

The core tension is between coverage and sustainability. A system needs someone available at all times, but no single person can sustainably be on-call constantly without it eating into the rest of their life and, eventually, their willingness to keep doing the job at all. Mature teams address this by spreading the load, rotating who is on-call on a predictable schedule, and by treating a high volume of pages as a problem to fix rather than a fact of life to endure. A team that pages someone awake three times a night, every week, does not have a people problem, it has a systems problem: either the alerting thresholds are wrong, generating noise for things that do not actually need immediate human attention, or the system itself is genuinely too fragile, and the right response is to invest in fixing the fragility rather than in asking people to simply tolerate more disruption.

Alert quality matters enormously here. An alert that pages a human should, in principle, mean that a human needs to do something right now that a machine cannot do automatically. In practice, many on-call rotations accumulate alerts that fire on symptoms without any corresponding action the on-call person can actually take beyond acknowledging and waiting, which trains people to treat pages as noise rather than as signals worth taking seriously, an effect sometimes called alert fatigue. The fix is not more alerts but fewer, better ones: alerting on user-visible symptoms rather than every internal metric that moves, and routing anything that does not need immediate human judgment to a queue that gets reviewed during working hours instead of a page that wakes someone up.

Good on-call practice also includes deliberately building institutional memory about incidents, so that the person paged at three in the morning is not starting from zero. Runbooks, step-by-step guides for how to diagnose and respond to known categories of problems, are one of the highest-leverage documents a team can maintain, precisely because they are written and refined calmly, in advance, and then used under exactly the conditions, tired, stressed, time-pressured, where clear thinking is hardest to do from scratch.

## Security as a continuous practice, not a one-time audit

It is tempting to think of security as a discrete task: build the system, then have someone check it for vulnerabilities before launch, then move on. This model fails for the same reason a one-time code review of a system that keeps changing would fail: the system does not stay still. New features add new surface area for something to go wrong, dependencies get updated and occasionally introduce new vulnerabilities, and attackers themselves adapt, finding new techniques against defenses that used to be sufficient. Security that only happens once, at launch, is security that degrades from the moment it is finished.

Mature teams instead treat security as an ongoing practice woven into the same processes used for everything else. Code review checks for common vulnerability patterns as a matter of course, not as a separate specialized audit. Dependencies are tracked and updated on an ongoing basis, since a large fraction of real-world security incidents trace back not to a novel attack but to a known vulnerability in a dependency that was not updated in time. Access to production systems and sensitive data is granted narrowly and reviewed periodically, on the assumption that permissions granted once tend to accumulate and rarely get revoked unless someone deliberately checks.

The principle of least privilege, granting each person and each system component only the access it strictly needs to do its job, is one of the most consistently useful ideas in this space, precisely because it limits the damage any single mistake or compromise can do. A service that can only read from one specific database table, rather than having broad administrative access to everything, is a service where a bug or a compromised credential can only do a bounded amount of harm. This is a deliberate trade against convenience: it is always faster, in the moment, to grant broad access so nobody has to ask for more later, and that convenience is exactly what makes narrow access hard to maintain discipline around without treating it as a first-class practice rather than an afterthought.

Just as with incidents in general, how a team responds to a discovered vulnerability matters as much as whether one exists in the first place. Systems that make it easy and low-stakes for an engineer to report "I think I might have introduced a security problem" catch issues early, while systems where such reports are met with blame push people toward staying quiet and hoping nobody notices, which is exactly the outcome that makes a small mistake into a serious incident.

## Migrations: the cost of changing your mind at scale

Every large system eventually needs to change something foundational: a database gets replaced, a core library is deprecated, an old API version needs to be retired in favor of a new one. In a small system this kind of change might take an afternoon. In a large system, the same change can take months or years, not because the new thing is hard to build, but because migrating everything that depends on the old thing, safely, without breaking things that are currently working, is a genuinely different and much larger problem than building the new thing in isolation.

The naive approach to a migration, build the replacement, then flip a switch to cut everything over at once, is tempting because it is conceptually simple, but it concentrates all of the risk into a single moment, and it is exactly the kind of all-at-once change that the deployment practices described earlier are designed to avoid. Large migrations are almost always done incrementally instead: running the old and new systems side by side, migrating one piece of functionality or one slice of traffic at a time, and validating at each step that the new system actually produces the same results as the old one before removing the old one. This is slower than a single cutover but dramatically reduces the chance that a subtle difference between old and new goes unnoticed until it has affected a large fraction of users.

A particularly important discipline in migrations is maintaining the ability to roll back at every stage, not just at the very end. A migration that has quietly deleted the old system's data halfway through, to save on infrastructure cost or to simplify the work, has thrown away its safety net exactly when it might still be needed. The extra cost of running both systems in parallel for longer than feels strictly necessary is usually far cheaper than the cost of a migration that goes wrong with no way back.

Migrations also tend to reveal how much implicit, undocumented behavior has accumulated in the old system over time, behavior nobody remembers deciding on, that some downstream consumer nonetheless quietly depends on. Uncovering and deciding what to do with this kind of accidental behavior, preserve it in the new system exactly as it was, or treat it as a bug to fix, is frequently the most time-consuming part of a migration, more so than writing the new system's core logic, precisely because it requires understanding not just what the system does but why, when the people who made the original decisions are often long gone.

## Conclusion: engineering practice as accumulated judgment

None of the practices described in this document are universal rules that apply identically to every system at every scale. A three-person startup that adopts the full weight of code review policies, migration playbooks, and on-call rotations designed for a thousand-engineer organization will likely slow itself down without gaining much, since it does not yet have the scale of people or risk that those practices exist to manage. Equally, a large organization that tries to operate the way a three-person startup does, skipping review, skipping tests, deploying by hand, will accumulate exactly the kind of confusion, duplicated effort, and preventable incidents these practices are designed to prevent.

The through-line across every section here is that software engineering practice, as distinct from programming itself, is mostly about managing two things that get harder as a system grows: the number of people who need a shared, accurate understanding of how the system works, and the number of ways the system can fail that nobody can fully predict in advance. Every practice described, version control discipline, code review, layered testing, careful deployment, observability, incident response, debt management, documentation, data scaling strategy, sustainable on-call, continuous security, and careful migrations, is, underneath its specific mechanics, an answer to one or both of those two problems. Recognizing that shared purpose is often more useful than memorizing any individual practice, because it means a team can reason from first principles about what they actually need, at their actual scale, rather than cargo-culting a checklist built for a different system entirely.

## Build versus buy, and the trap of underestimating maintenance

At some point in almost every large system's life, a team faces a choice between building a piece of infrastructure themselves or adopting something that already exists, whether that is an open-source project, a managed cloud service, or a commercial vendor. The instinct to build is often strongest among the most capable engineers, precisely because they are the ones most likely to believe, correctly, that they could build a better-fitting version of whatever the existing option offers. This instinct is also one of the most reliable ways for a strong engineering team to waste years of effort on infrastructure that was never the actual product.

The mistake is usually not in underestimating the difficulty of the initial build, experienced engineers are often quite good at estimating that, but in underestimating the ongoing cost of maintaining it afterward, indefinitely, for as long as the system depends on it. A message queue, a search index, an authentication system: these are the kind of components that look like a self-contained weekend project and turn out to require years of accumulated operational knowledge to run reliably at scale, handling edge cases, security patches, and failure modes that an existing, widely used option has usually already encountered and fixed. Choosing to build internally is sometimes still the right call, when the requirements are genuinely unusual, or when the component is close enough to the actual product that owning it is a durable advantage, but the decision should be made with the full lifetime cost in view, not just the cost of the first working version.

The opposite mistake also exists, though it is less discussed: adopting an external dependency for something that is actually close to the core of what makes a product valuable, and therefore something the team should want tight control over. A team that outsources the one piece of technology that differentiates their product from competitors has traded short-term convenience for long-term strategic exposure, since they now depend on a third party's priorities and roadmap for the thing that is supposed to be their advantage. The useful question is rarely build versus buy in the abstract, but rather: is this component something our users will notice and care about directly, or is it something that just needs to work reliably so people can get to the part they actually care about. The former is usually worth owning. The latter is usually worth buying, even when a team is confident they could build a better version, because being slightly better at solved problems is rarely worth the years of ongoing maintenance it costs to prove it.
